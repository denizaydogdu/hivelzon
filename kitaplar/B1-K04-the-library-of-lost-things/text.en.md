## [c1] The Door

### [p01]
Behind the lost-property office, Ines found a door that nobody else could see.

It was a grey Tuesday in November. Ines was fourteen, and she had come to the station after school, as she had done every month for a year. She always asked the same question at the same window.

"A green notebook," she said. "For drawing. I lost it on the six o'clock train last October."

The woman behind the window didn't even look up. "Nothing green this week, love. Sorry."

Ines nodded. She had expected that answer. She had already decided that this was the last time she would ask.

She used to draw every day. She drew on the bus, in class, at dinner and in bed. Her notebook was full of strange birds, tired dogs, and people who were waiting for trains. Then she had left it on a seat, and the drawings had disappeared with it. Since that day, she hadn't drawn a single line.

### [p02]
She turned to leave. That was when she saw the door.

It was between the office window and a machine which sold hot drinks. It was small and blue, with a round metal handle. Ines frowned. She had walked past this wall twelve times, and she had never seen a door there before.

A man with a suitcase walked straight past it. Two students were standing next to it, laughing at something on a phone. Nobody looked at it. It was as if the door was only there for her.

"Excuse me," Ines said to the woman at the window. "Where does that door go?"

The woman looked at the wall. "What door?"

Ines's heart was beating fast. She waited until the woman was busy with another customer. Then she put her hand on the cold metal handle.

If it's locked, I'll go home, she told herself. I'll go home and forget about it.

It wasn't locked.

### [p03]
On the other side, there was a long, warm room which smelled of old paper and rain. It was much bigger than the station. That was impossible, but Ines was too surprised to worry about it.

Shelves rose on every side, higher than houses. They were full of things. Not books, or not only books. Ines saw keys, scarves, toys, glasses, single shoes, and hundreds and hundreds of umbrellas.

At a wooden desk in the middle of the room sat a tall, thin man in a dark blue jacket. He had silver hair and small round glasses, and he was writing something very slowly with a very long pen.

He looked up and smiled, as if he had been expecting her.

"Good afternoon," he said. "I'm Mr Orrin. Welcome to the Library of Lost Things."

"The library of what?" Ines asked.

"Lost things. Everything that people lose comes here in the end. Well, almost everything."

### [p04]
Ines looked around her. A small red glove was floating slowly down from a high shelf, like a leaf. Mr Orrin caught it without looking and put it in a box on his desk.

"Is this a dream?" Ines asked.

"People often ask that. It isn't. Dreams are kept in a different building." He put down his pen. "How can I help you?"

Ines took a deep breath. "I lost a notebook. A green one, for drawing. On a train, a year ago. Have you seen it?"

Something changed in Mr Orrin's face. For a moment he looked almost excited, like a child before a birthday. Then he stood up and straightened his jacket.

"Ah," he said softly. "The green notebook. Yes. We know it very well."

"So it's here?"

He smiled again, and this time the smile was a little mysterious. "We've been waiting for you to ask about it."

## [c2] Umbrellas to the Ceiling

### [p05]
Mr Orrin took a ring of keys from his pocket. "First," he said, "you need to understand how the library works. Follow me, please, and don't touch anything that makes a noise."

They walked between long rows of shelves. The first room was the Umbrella Hall. Umbrellas hung from the ceiling in their thousands, red and yellow and black and spotted, open and closed, like a sky made of colours.

"Umbrellas are the thing that people lose most," said Mr Orrin. "Every time it stops raining, somebody forgets one. We have received forty-two today, and it's only Tuesday."

The next room was full of single gloves. Each one was waiting on a small hook.

"They're very patient," Mr Orrin said. "Most of them have been waiting for years. They're hoping that the other glove will arrive one day."

"Does that happen?"

"Sometimes. When it does, we have a little party."

### [p06]
After the gloves came a quiet room with tall glass jars. Each jar was shining softly, and inside each one a little tune was going round and round.

"Lost songs," said Mr Orrin. "Songs that people used to know but can't remember any more. If you put your ear close, you might recognise one."

Ines put her ear next to a jar. She heard a song which her grandmother used to sing in the kitchen. She had forgotten it completely. For a moment, she could smell onions and warm bread.

The next room was the strangest of all. It was full of small, angry clouds which were moving fast and making a noise like a kettle.

"Lost patience," explained Mr Orrin. "People lose it in traffic, mostly. And in queues. Please walk quickly. They bite."

Then came a soft golden room that was full of light and the sound of bees.

"Lost afternoons," he said, with a sigh. "The ones people spend looking at their phones. They are lovely, aren't they? Nobody ever comes back for them."

### [p07]
At last they arrived at a room with drawers from the floor to the ceiling. There were thousands of small wooden drawers, and every drawer had a card on the front. The cards had no words on them, only little pictures: a key, a cup, a moon, a hand.

"Before we look for your notebook," said Mr Orrin, "you must know the rule. It's very old and very simple. If you take something from the library, you have to leave something in its place."

"What kind of thing?"

"Something that you won't miss. That's important. It must be something you really don't need any more." He looked at a large clock on the wall. It had no numbers, only lines. "And you must leave before midnight. If you're still here at midnight, you'll become one of our lost things. We'll have to put you on a shelf. Visitors don't enjoy that."

Ines couldn't tell if he was joking. She decided not to ask.

### [p08]
Mr Orrin walked along the drawers, reading the little pictures with his finger. He stopped, opened a drawer near the floor and took out a card.

"Green notebook, for drawing," he read. "Lost on the six o'clock train. Found the same evening." He smiled. "That was quick. Your notebook wanted to be found."

Ines's heart jumped. "So where is it?"

Mr Orrin turned the card towards her. At the bottom there was a small picture: an open hand, holding something out to someone else.

"What does that mean?" Ines asked.

"It means borrowed," he said. "Your notebook isn't on the shelf at the moment. Somebody has borrowed it."

"Borrowed?" Ines stared at him. "But it's mine! Who borrowed it?"

Mr Orrin turned the card over. On the back there was a long line of tiny hands, one after another, all the way to the edge of the card.

"Ah," he said. "That's a good question. You see, it isn't exactly one person."

## [c3] Borrowers

### [p09]
"People can borrow from the library," explained Mr Orrin, "if they need something that somebody else has lost. A man who has lost his courage can borrow somebody's old courage for a day. A girl who has forgotten how to whistle can borrow a whistle."

"And somebody needed my notebook?"

"Several people did." He took out a small silver bell and rang it. "The last borrower brought it back yesterday, but it went out again this morning. It's very popular. The person who has it now is still in the library, I think. Look for the man who is lost. He's lost quite often."

"How will I know him?"

"He'll be looking for the way out," said Mr Orrin. "He usually is."

Ines looked at the clock with no numbers. The long line was moving slowly round. She didn't know what time it was, but she had a feeling that it was later than she thought.

Then she started to run.

### [p10]
She ran through the Umbrella Hall and past the gloves. She ran around the lost afternoons, which were singing softly to themselves. She looked in every room, but there was no man.

Finally, while she was going down a narrow staircase, she heard a voice.

"Left, then right… or was it right, then left?"

At the bottom of the stairs, in a room full of old maps, an old man was standing in front of a wall. He was wearing a long brown coat and a soft hat, and he was turning a map round and round in his hands.

"Excuse me," said Ines. "Are you lost?"

"Completely," the old man said happily. "I came in to find my reading glasses, and that was on Saturday, I think. Or perhaps Sunday. I'm Mr Pell. Who are you?"

"I'm Ines. I'm looking for a green notebook. Mr Orrin said you might have it."

"Oh!" His face lit up. "You're the girl who draws!"

### [p11]
Mr Pell felt in all his pockets. He found a pencil, two keys, an apple and, finally, a green notebook.

Ines stopped breathing. It was her notebook. There was the corner that her cat had chewed. There was the blue mark on the cover from the day she had dropped her pen. But it looked different somehow. It looked fatter.

"I've had it for a week," said Mr Pell. "I hope you don't mind. I get lost a lot, you see, and your drawings help me. There's one of a station in the rain. Whenever I look at it, I remember the way home."

"You've been using my notebook as a map?"

"Not exactly as a map," he said. "More like a friend." He held it out to her, but then he stopped. "Oh, I should tell you. I added something to it. It seemed rude to take and give nothing back."

Before Ines could ask what he meant, a bell rang somewhere above them. It was loud, deep and slow.

"That's the eleven o'clock bell," said Mr Pell. "You'd better hurry."

### [p12]
Ines reached for the notebook, but Mr Pell's fingers closed around it gently.

"Not like that, my dear," he said. "I borrowed it from the library, so I have to return it to the library. That's the rule. Then Mr Orrin will give it to you, if you leave something behind."

"But it's mine!"

"Yes," he said kindly. "That's why you have to leave something. You didn't come here only for a notebook, did you?"

Ines didn't know what he meant. She was feeling confused and a little annoyed. She had waited a whole year, and now there was another rule.

"I think the desk is that way," said Mr Pell. "Or possibly that way. Shall we try both?"

They tried both. They got lost twice. At one point they walked through the lost patience by mistake, and one of the little clouds bit Mr Pell's hat.

When they finally reached Mr Orrin's desk, the clock with no numbers showed that there was less than an hour left.

## [c4] What to Leave

### [p13]
Mr Orrin took the notebook from Mr Pell and put it in a drawer under his desk. Then he wrote something on the card with his very long pen.

"Thank you, Mr Pell," he said. "Your glasses are on your head, by the way. They've been there since Saturday."

Mr Pell touched his head and laughed. "So they are! Well, goodbye, young lady. Keep drawing." He walked off towards the door, turned left, then right, and was lost again almost at once.

Mr Orrin turned to Ines. "Now," he said. "What will you leave?"

Ines emptied her school bag onto the desk. There was a maths book, a pen that didn't work, half a sandwich, a hair band, and a ticket from a film that had been very boring.

"I won't miss any of these," she said hopefully.

Mr Orrin looked at the things politely. "These are things you don't need. That isn't the same as things you won't miss. The library can tell the difference."

### [p14]
Ines tried the half sandwich. She put it on the scales on Mr Orrin's desk, strange old scales that were made of glass. Nothing happened.

She tried the boring ticket. Nothing happened.

"You're thinking about it like a shop," Mr Orrin said gently. "It isn't about money or size. Think about what you've been carrying. Something which has been heavy for a long time."

Ines looked at the clock. The line was close to the top. Her hands were cold.

She thought about the year without drawing. She remembered sitting on her bed with a new notebook that her mum had bought her, and not opening it. She remembered the art teacher asking where her drawings were. Every time, a small voice inside her had said the same thing. You lost the good ones. The new ones won't be as good. You can't do it again.

Ines realised that she had been carrying that voice for a whole year. It was heavier than her school bag. It was heavier than anything.

### [p15]
"Can I leave a sentence?" she asked quietly.

Mr Orrin's eyes grew very bright behind his small round glasses. "You can leave anything that is really yours."

He gave her a white card and his long pen. Ines's hand was shaking a little. She hadn't drawn anything for a year, and now even writing felt difficult. She wrote slowly, in small letters.

I'm not good enough.

She looked at the words for a long time. They looked small and silly on the card. They didn't look heavy at all.

She put the card on the glass scales. This time the scales moved. They went down, down, down, as if she had put a stone on them. Then there was a soft sound like a sigh, and the card disappeared.

Ines felt strange. Lighter. It was as if she had put down a suitcase that she had forgotten she was carrying.

"Will I miss it?" she asked.

"Never," said Mr Orrin. "People never do."

### [p16]
Mr Orrin opened the drawer under his desk. He took out the green notebook and put it in her hands.

Ines nearly dropped it. It was heavy, much heavier than she remembered, and much thicker. When she had lost it, it had been half empty. Now it was twice as thick, and its pages were so full that the cover would not close properly.

Pieces of coloured paper were coming out between the pages. There was a dry flower, an old train ticket and a feather. Somebody had added an extra page with glue. Somebody else had added three.

"What happened to it?" she whispered.

Mr Orrin did not answer. He only looked at her over his glasses, and he was smiling.

Far above them, the bell began to ring again. One, two, three… Ines counted. Midnight was coming.

"Open it," said Mr Orrin, "but please, open it quickly."

## [c5] The Most Borrowed Book

### [p17]
Ines opened the notebook at the first page. There was her own drawing of a tired dog on a bus. She remembered drawing it with cold fingers.

But on the next page, there was something new. Somebody had drawn the same dog, but now it was asleep on a warm sofa, with a cat next to it. Under the drawing, in careful writing, someone had written a few words. For the dog, who looked so tired.

Ines turned the page. Her strange bird with long legs was there. Beside it, someone else had drawn a nest with three little long-legged birds inside it.

Page after page, it was the same. Every one of her drawings had an answer. Someone had given her people at the station a train at last. Someone had added sun to her rainy street. Someone had drawn a small blue door in the wall of her empty room, with a round handle.

### [p18]
The drawings were all different. Some were beautiful. Some were funny. Some were terrible, and Ines liked those even more. One was clearly by a very small child: a purple horse with seven legs.

Near the end, she found her drawing of a station in the rain. Next to it, in shaky lines, someone had drawn a little old man in a soft hat, walking home under an umbrella. Under it were the words: This one always shows me the way. Thank you.

"Mr Pell," Ines whispered.

"Your notebook," said Mr Orrin, "is the most borrowed thing in the whole library. More than umbrellas. More than courage. People have been borrowing it all year. A fisherman who had lost his luck. A woman who had forgotten how to laugh. A boy who used to be afraid of the dark. Every one of them took something from your drawings, and every one of them left something behind."

### [p19]
Ines looked up. Her eyes were wet. "I thought my drawings were lost," she said. "I thought they were gone for ever."

Mr Orrin took off his glasses and cleaned them slowly.

"Your drawings were never lost," he said. "Only you were."

The bell rang for the eleventh time. Ines jumped up. "Midnight!"

"Yes. You have one minute. Go."

She ran towards the blue door. Then she stopped, turned and ran back to the desk.

"What are you doing?" said Mr Orrin. "There isn't time!"

Ines opened her school bag again. At the bottom was the new notebook that her mum had bought her. It was empty, with clean white pages, and she had carried it everywhere for months without opening it once.

She put it on the shelf behind Mr Orrin's desk.

"For the next person who gets lost," she said.

Mr Orrin smiled. It was the biggest smile she had seen all evening.

### [p20]
The twelfth bell was still ringing when Ines pulled open the blue door and fell out into the station.

It was quiet and cold. The drinks machine was making its usual noise. The woman at the lost-property window was putting on her coat to go home. Ines looked at the big station clock. Only one minute had passed since she had opened the door.

She turned around. The wall between the window and the drinks machine was just a wall again.

But the green notebook was in her hands, heavy and warm.

Ines sat down on a bench and took a pencil from her bag. For a moment, the small voice tried to speak, but it had nothing to say. She had left it somewhere else.

So she opened the notebook at the first empty page, and she began to draw. She drew a tall man with silver hair and small round glasses, standing at a desk under a sky of umbrellas.

Under it, she wrote a few words. For Mr Orrin, who waited.

Then she turned the page and kept drawing.
