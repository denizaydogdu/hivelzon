## [c1] Snow

### [p01]
Eight strangers were invited to dinner, but the table had been laid for nine.

Ruth Okafor counted the places twice, because counting things was a habit she had never managed to lose. Nine white plates. Nine sets of silver, laid out with the kind of precision that suggests either a very good waiter or a very nervous one. Nine crystal glasses catching the light of the candles, and nine green velvet chairs, their backs curved like question marks.

Behind her, the other seven guests were still shaking snow from their coats in the doorway and saying, in various ways, that they had never seen anything like it.

Ruth had. She had seen it in the small hours of a great many Saturday mornings, when a crossword grid had nine words going across and eight going down and the whole thing refused to fit. For twenty-six years she had set the Saturday puzzle for a newspaper in Calderbury under the name Night Owl, and for twenty of those years nobody but her editor had known who Night Owl was. She was fifty-eight now, retired, and supposedly enjoying a quiet life. She had not finished a single crossword since the day she retired, neither her own nor anybody else's, and she had told nobody why.

The dining room of the Glasswater Hotel looked as if it had been designed by someone who had loved the nineteen-twenties and never quite forgiven them for ending. There were mirrors framed in golden fans, lamps shaped like tulips, and walls the deep green of a wine bottle held up to the light. Outside the tall windows, Lake Fennick had vanished under the snow. So had the road.

"Nine," said a voice beside her. "Did you count nine as well?"

"I did," said Ruth. "Twice."

### [p02]
The voice belonged to a small, upright woman with silver hair cut as straight as a ruler. She had introduced herself in the taxi as Agnes Thorne, a librarian, and had said almost nothing else for the entire journey, which in Ruth's experience meant that she had been listening to everything.

The taxi itself belonged to Desmond Pike, a large, cheerful man of about sixty-five who called everyone "love" and drove as though the snow were a personal friend. He, too, had received an invitation. His had come with a second, smaller note asking whether he would be kind enough to collect the other guests from the little station at Fennick Halt, and enclosing payment for the trip. "Paid in advance," he had told them three times. "Never happened to me before. Not once in forty years."

Ruth took her own invitation out of her handbag and read it again, although she knew it by heart.

It was printed on thick cream card, with nothing on the front at all. Inside, in small, careful letters, it said: You are warmly invited to dinner at the Glasswater Hotel, Lake Fennick, on the fourteenth of January, at seven o'clock. A room has been reserved for you. There is nothing to pay. Once, a long time ago, you did something kind that you have very probably forgotten. I have not.

Underneath, in ink, was the signature: Daryl Brie.

It was the signature that had brought Ruth all this way. Nobody was called Daryl Brie. Brie was a cheese, not a surname, and nobody under sixty was called Daryl. Ruth had looked at the two words for a week, and the longer she looked, the more certain she became that the letters were hiding something. What they were hiding, she had not yet worked out.

She had tried, of course. On the train she had managed Bridal Rye, which was not a person but a rather unpleasant sandwich.

### [p03]
The others were introducing themselves now, in the slightly too loud voices of people who have been cold for a long time and are not yet sure that they are warm.

There was Ambrose Hale, a retired history teacher of about seventy-eight, as tall and thin as a coat stand, who shook everybody's hand and said "How do you do" as though the phrase were still in daily use. There was Felix Arden, a vet in his forties with anxious eyebrows, who apologised to the coat stand when he walked into it. There was Bev Hartley, who drove buses in Calderbury and laughed at everything, including, it appeared, the snow. There was Priya Nair, a pharmacist, who read the label on her bottle of water before she drank it. And there was Theo Fallon, a thin red-haired man of about thirty, who carried a guitar case he had refused to leave in the taxi.

"Musician," he explained, to nobody in particular. "Well. Sort of. Used to be."

Not one of them, it turned out, had ever met any of the others. Not one of them knew anybody called Daryl Brie.

"Perhaps the manager knows," said Priya.

The manager, Mr Crane, was a narrow, anxious man in a waistcoat the colour of strong tea. He had met them at the door with an umbrella that the wind had immediately turned inside out, and he had been apologising ever since. The hotel, he explained, was normally closed in winter. The cook had gone home hours ago because of the snow. Dinner, however, had been prepared, and he would serve it himself.

"And our host?" asked Ambrose. "Mr Brie?"

Mr Crane's eyes moved to the ninth chair and then quickly away again. "I'm afraid," he said, "I am not at liberty to say."

### [p04]
"Not at liberty," repeated Desmond, when the manager had gone to fetch the soup. "That's what they always say on the telly, just before the detective works it all out."

"Or just before somebody gets a pay rise," said Bev. "Come on. Let's sit down before the soup gets cold."

There were small cards on the table, folded in half, with a name written on each in the same careful hand as the invitations. Ruth found hers between Agnes and the empty place at the end. Nobody's name was on the ninth card. It simply said: Reserved.

"Reserved for whom?" said Ambrose, lowering himself into his chair. "One does like to know with whom one is dining."

"Maybe he's late," said Theo. "Mr Brie. Stuck in the snow."

"Then why didn't he put his name on the card?" said Priya.

Nobody had an answer to that. Felix suggested, quite reasonably, that perhaps the host was shy. Desmond suggested, less reasonably, that perhaps the host was a ghost. Agnes said nothing at all, but Ruth noticed that she had moved her own glass a little closer to her plate, as if to make room for somebody.

Ruth looked at the empty chair beside her. It was the only chair in the room that nobody had touched, and yet the velvet of the seat was pressed down slightly in the middle, the way a cushion is when something has been resting on it for a long time.

She leaned over. Half hidden under the edge of the long white tablecloth, sitting very neatly on the ninth chair, was a parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with green string.

On the label, in the same careful letters, was her own name.

## [c2] Parcels

### [p05]
Ruth's first instinct was to open it. Her second, which came about half a second later, was that this was exactly what somebody wanted her to do.

"Please don't," said Mr Crane from the doorway. He was carrying a large silver soup pot and wearing the expression of a man who has been told to say one thing and is terrified of saying anything else. He put the pot down on the sideboard. "Not yet, I mean. The instructions were very clear. Nothing is to be opened until after the soup."

"What instructions?" said Ruth. "From whom?"

"After the soup," said Mr Crane, filling the bowls at great speed, and fled.

"Instructions," said Desmond, gazing after him. "Somebody's been very organised."

It was then that Felix lifted his napkin and made a small sound, like a man who has found a spider in his shoe. Beneath the folded napkins, it turned out, there were parcels: seven in all, each wrapped in brown paper and green string, each labelled with a name. There was nothing under Ruth's napkin, and nothing under the napkin at the empty place. Her own parcel, it seemed, had been given a chair of its own, which was the kind of arrangement Ruth distrusted on principle.

The soup was tomato, thick and dark red and very hot, with a swirl of cream on top and a scattering of black pepper. It was, Bev declared after the first spoonful, the best soup she had ever eaten in her life, and she had eaten a great deal of soup. Ruth agreed, privately. There was something about it that she could not quite place, as if she had tasted it somewhere before and had been trying to remember where ever since.

Nobody ate slowly. Hardly had the last spoon been put down when eight pairs of hands reached for eight pieces of green string.

### [p06]
Bev opened hers first, because Bev, it was already clear, did everything first. Inside was a small paper bus ticket, faded to the colour of weak tea, with a neat hole punched through the middle.

"Well, I never," she said. "That's one of ours. That's an old Calderbury ticket, that is. We haven't used these for years." She turned it over. "And that's my punch. Every driver had their own shape, see. Mine was a little star."

Felix's parcel contained a single brown leather glove, lined with wool and worn smooth at the fingertips. He held it up and stared at it as though it had spoken to him.

"This is mine," he said slowly. "My mother sewed my name inside. Look. I lost the pair years ago. At least, I always thought I'd lost them."

"You might well have given them away," said Agnes quietly, "and simply forgotten."

Agnes's own parcel held a library card, the old-fashioned kind made of stiff blue cardboard, with a name typed across the top. She did not hold it up. She looked at it for a long moment and then placed it beside her plate, face down, as one might place a card in a game one was not yet ready to play.

Ambrose unwrapped a red pencil, sharpened almost to nothing.

"Good heavens," he said. "I marked essays with these for forty-one years. I had one in every pocket. My wife used to find them in the washing machine." He turned it in his long fingers. "I cannot imagine why anyone would wish to return one. They are not, I assure you, valuable."

"Maybe it's valuable to somebody else," said Theo.

### [p07]
Theo's own parcel was the smallest of all. He shook out into his palm a little triangle of green plastic, scratched and slightly bent at one corner.

"A plectrum," he said, and laughed, but not as if anything was funny. "A guitar pick. This is my old one. I used it for years when I was a kid, playing for coins in the station." He closed his hand around it. "I gave it to someone once."

"To whom?" said Ruth.

"I don't know. That's the thing. I never knew."

Priya found a round tin of lemon sweets, the kind with a hinged lid, still half full. The label on the lid, she said, was from the pharmacy where she had worked when she first qualified, the little one inside the main hall of Calderbury Central station. It had closed long ago.

Desmond's parcel held a key ring with a yellow plastic star on it, the paint worn away at the points. He stared at it, and for the first time all evening he did not say anything at all.

That left Ruth.

The paper came away easily. Inside was a puzzle book, the cheap kind sold at station kiosks, with a bent cover and a coffee ring on the back. She knew it before she opened it. She had bought it herself, a very long time ago, for the train home.

Most of the puzzles inside had been done in somebody else's pencil, in large, uncertain capitals. But on the last two pages, which were meant to be blank, someone had drawn a small crossword grid by hand, and written the clues beneath it. Ruth recognised the hand. It was her own.

Every clue had been answered in the same childish capitals, except one. The last clue was still empty. It read: The first thing you ever said to me. Six letters.

### [p08]
For a while nobody spoke. The candles burned. Outside, the snow went on falling into the lake as though it intended to fill it.

It was Bev who broke the silence, because of course it was. She had been holding the bus ticket close to a candle, turning it this way and that.

"There's a date on it," she said. "You can just about read it. The fourteenth of January." She looked up. "Fourteen years ago. That was the night of the big storm. I remember, because they stopped all the trains and we had to run the buses till two in the morning."

"The fourteenth," said Priya slowly. "That was the last night I worked at the station pharmacy. I gave a tin of sweets to..." She stopped.

"I was at the station that night," said Theo. "Playing. Nobody was listening, except..."

"I lent somebody my coat," said Ambrose. "I remember it very well, because I had to walk home without it."

Felix was staring at his glove. Desmond was staring at his star. Agnes turned her library card over at last, and Ruth saw that the blue cardboard had gone soft at the edges from being carried, for a long time, in somebody's pocket.

What Ruth felt then was not surprise, exactly. It was the feeling she used to get at three in the morning, when the last word of a puzzle slid into place and every other answer suddenly made sense. For a moment she saw a red woolly hat, very clearly, and then it was gone again.

Eight strangers. Eight small, forgotten things. And every one of them led back to the same stormy night, fourteen years ago, at Calderbury Central station.

"Not strangers," said Agnes, very softly. "Not quite."

## [c3] Suspicion

### [p09]
It is often said that nothing brings people together like a shared mystery. Whoever said it had clearly never spent an evening snowed in with seven strangers, a nervous manager and nine plates.

Within ten minutes of the parcels being opened, the dining room had divided into theories, and every theory had a suspect.

Desmond's suspect was Ambrose. "Look at him," he murmured to Ruth, not quietly enough. "Writes letters, doesn't he? Proper ones, with ink. I bet he's got a cupboard full of cream-coloured card at home."

Ambrose's suspect was Desmond. "A taxi driver," he observed, to nobody in particular, "would know a great many addresses."

Theo suspected Felix, on the grounds that vets were used to keeping secrets from animals. Felix suspected nobody, but he kept sneezing, and he apologised so often for the sneezing that Bev began to suspect him too. Priya suspected everyone equally and said so, in the calm tone of someone reading out the side effects on a packet of pills.

"It would seem," she said, "that someone has gone to considerable trouble to find eight people who have never met. That suggests either a great deal of time or a great deal of money."

"Or both," said Bev.

"Or both," Priya agreed.

Only Agnes had no suspect. She sat with her hands folded on the blue library card, watching each speaker in turn, the way Ruth imagined she had once watched readers at the library desk: patiently, and missing nothing.

Ruth said very little herself. She was watching too. It had taken her less than an hour to realise that every person at that table was hiding something small, and that none of those small things was the answer.

### [p10]
Desmond, for example, kept saying that he drove the taxi every day, but his hands on the wheel had been careful and slightly surprised, like a man greeting an old friend he has not seen for a while. Ruth would have bet her best pencil that he had retired and borrowed the car for the night. Theo said he was a musician, but he had not once opened his guitar case, and when Bev asked him to play, he said he had a sore finger and then forgot which one. Felix claimed his sneezing was a cold. It was not a cold. Ruth had seen him look at the grey hairs on the sleeve of Mr Crane's waistcoat, and then sneeze, and then look again.

None of this made anyone a host. People hide small things for small reasons: pride, mostly, or embarrassment, or simply habit. What she needed was the one thing that did not fit.

And the one thing that did not fit, as everyone soon agreed, was Mr Crane.

He kept disappearing. Every few minutes he would murmur something about the boiler and slip out of the room, and every time he came back, he looked a little more anxious and a little more scratched. There were three thin red lines on the back of his left hand that had not been there when he served the soup. His office, at the end of the corridor, was locked, and from behind its door, when Theo walked past, there came a small, high, complaining noise.

"That," said Desmond darkly, "is not a boiler."

"Is there anybody else in the hotel, Mr Crane?" asked Ambrose, when the manager next appeared.

"Nobody," said Mr Crane, much too fast. "Absolutely nobody at all."

### [p11]
"Mr Crane," said Ambrose, rising slowly to his full and considerable height, "I spent forty-one years listening to boys explain why their homework had been eaten by the dog. I know what a lie sounds like. With respect, it is high time you told us the truth."

Mr Crane went pink. "I can assure you, sir..."

"You needn't assure us of anything," said Priya. "You need only answer. Do you know who Daryl Brie is?"

"I... I have been asked not to say."

"So you do know," said Bev.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't need to, mate," said Desmond, kindly enough. "It's written all over your face."

Mr Crane looked at the door, at the ceiling, at the ninth chair, and finally at his own scratched hand, as though it might help him. Then he muttered something about the boiler again and left the room at a speed which was not quite running.

"Well," said Theo. "That settles it, then. He's Mr Brie."

"He might well be," said Agnes. "Then again, he might not."

It was Felix who suggested more bread, mostly, Ruth suspected, to give everyone something to do with their hands. Desmond and Theo volunteered to fetch it, and went off down the corridor towards a pair of swinging doors with a small brass sign on them that simply said Kitchen.

They came back without any bread.

"Locked," said Theo. "The kitchen's locked. And there's no light under the door."

"Crane's got a key on his belt," said Desmond. "I saw it. Big old brass one. But when I asked him, he said he hadn't got one."

"Of course he did," said Bev, and laughed. Nobody else did.

### [p12]
Ruth was not, by nature, a woman who went creeping along hotel corridors in the dark. She was a woman who sat in warm rooms working things out on paper. But she had been sitting in a warm room for two hours now, and the thing she was trying to work out refused to go on paper.

So she excused herself, took one of the candles, and went to look for herself.

The corridor was long and cold and lined with photographs of the hotel in summer: boats on the lake, ladies in hats, a long-ago wedding on the lawn. At the end were the two swinging doors. Each had a small round window at head height, like a porthole on a ship.

The doors were indeed locked. Ruth pushed, gently, and they did not move. There was no light beneath them. There was no sound.

She raised her candle to the little window and looked through.

The kitchen beyond was dark and very clean. Copper pans hung in rows from a rail, catching her candlelight. Bunches of herbs were drying from a beam. On the long steel counter, a loaf of bread sat on a board, half cut, with the knife lying beside it.

And on the big black stove at the back, on the lowest possible flame, stood a large pot. A thin thread of steam was rising from it, steady and unhurried, into the cold air.

Ruth stood very still.

Mr Crane had told them that the cook had gone home hours ago. Mr Crane had told them that dinner had been prepared earlier. But a soup left alone on a stove for two hours would have burned to the bottom of the pot long ago. Somebody had been stirring it.

The kitchen door was locked, and the soup was still hot.

## [c4] That Night

### [p13]
Ruth knocked. She did not expect an answer, which is perhaps why she nearly dropped the candle when she got one.

"Just a moment," said a voice behind the door. A key turned. One of the doors swung open, and a young woman looked out at her.

She was perhaps twenty-three, small and quick, with dark hair tied back under a white cloth and a white cook's jacket with the sleeves pushed up to the elbow. There was flour on her arms and a pencil behind her ear. Her face, in the candlelight, was the face of someone who has been caught doing something and has been expecting to be caught for some time.

"I'm Maren," she said. "The cook. I'm so sorry. I'm the one who was supposed to have gone home."

"So I gather," said Ruth.

"The road closed before I could leave. Not that it matters; I start at five, and bread doesn't wait. And then Mr Crane said you'd all be... and I thought... well." She gave a small, helpless shrug. "I'm not very good with people. I'm better with onions."

Behind her, a light came on, and Mr Crane appeared from a side door, looking more scratched than ever and very relieved to have been found out.

"She's shy," he said. "She asked me not to make a fuss. I didn't think anyone would notice."

"Somebody always notices," said Ruth.

Ten minutes later, Maren was serving the main course herself: chicken cooked with lemon and thyme, and small golden potatoes. She moved round the table quickly and quietly, and when she reached Desmond, she put a cup of tea beside his plate with three sugars already in it, although he had not asked for tea at all.

It was Agnes who said what everybody had been thinking.

"We each remember a little piece of that night," she said. "Perhaps, if we put the pieces together, we shall see the picture."

### [p14]
"I'll start," said Bev, "seeing as I've got the ticket."

She leaned her elbows on the table, which made Ambrose wince.

"Right. So. The storm came in about six. By seven, there wasn't a train moving anywhere in the north. Calderbury Central was packed: people sitting on the floor, kids crying, the lot. Then about eleven, the station people said they had to close the building, because part of the roof was letting the snow in. The library across the square had said they'd open up for anybody who was stuck, but the square was pure ice, so they had me run the bus across it. Back and forth, back and forth. Two minutes each way."

"And the ticket?" said Ruth.

"There was a little girl," said Bev. "Nine, maybe ten. Red woolly hat. Waiting for my bus with some woman who'd been looking after her. The kid looked frozen. I didn't charge anybody that night, obviously, but she wanted a ticket. Said she'd never had one of her own. So I punched her one, with my little star, and told her she was my most important passenger." She shrugged. "That's all."

Felix put down his fork. "A red hat," he said. "Yes. At the bus stop. Her hands were blue. I'd just come off a twelve-hour shift at the animal hospital, and I was waiting for the same bus, and she was sitting there with her hands up her sleeves, trying to warm them. So I gave her my gloves." He looked at the single glove beside his plate. "I was supposed to be at my sister's for dinner. I never got there. I spent the night in the library instead, and I never saw the gloves again."

### [p15]
"She had hurt her knee," said Priya. "Earlier. About half past six." She said it carefully, as though she were reading the dose from a label. "She slipped on the ice by the platform and came into the pharmacy with blood on her tights. She didn't cry. That's what I remember. She was trying very hard not to cry. I cleaned the cut and put a plaster on it, and I gave her a tin of lemon sweets, because..." She paused. "Because I would rather she had something to hold."

"Her coat was wet through," said Ambrose. "Quite soaked. It must have been about seven o'clock. She was standing under the departures board, looking up at it as though it might change its mind. I was a schoolteacher, you understand; one notices a child who is cold. I lent her my coat. It reached to her ankles." He smiled faintly. "It was returned to me a few weeks later, in a parcel, without a note. Clean, pressed, and missing one red pencil."

Theo had been turning the green plectrum over and over in his fingers.

"I was sixteen," he said. "I used to play in the station hall on Friday nights, because the echo made me sound better than I was. That night everybody was too fed up to listen. Except her. She was sitting on a bench eating soup out of a paper cup, next to some woman who was doing a crossword." He did not look at Ruth. "So I played to her. Just to her. A song I'd written that afternoon. Never played it again, actually. I gave her the pick afterwards, because she asked what it was."

"Do you remember the song?" asked Agnes.

"Every note," said Theo. "Funny, that."

### [p16]
Agnes pushed the blue library card gently across the table, so that everyone could see her own name typed across the top.

"The library was full," she said. "Ninety-three people, sleeping on the floor between the shelves. I made the children a bed of cushions in the reading corner. The girl in the red hat wanted a book, and she couldn't borrow one without a card, so I lent her mine." She looked at Ruth. "There was a woman with her. A woman who didn't sleep at all. She sat at the big table under the window all night, writing in a little book with a pencil, and every so often she got up to check on the child. I made her two cups of tea. She didn't drink either of them."

Ruth's face felt warm, which she put down to the candles.

"It was me," she said. "I'm sorry. I'd forgotten your face. I found her on the bench at about eight. I had some idea of buying her a sandwich and then going home. I stayed until it got light." She touched the puzzle book. "I left this beside her while she was asleep. I had a train to catch."

"And I took her home in the morning," said Desmond, quietly for once. "To her auntie's, on the other side of town. Wouldn't take a penny. She sat in the front and asked me how many sugars I had in my tea, and I said three, and she said that was too many." He looked at the cup by his elbow. "I gave her my lucky star."

Maren came round the table then, collecting the plates. As she leaned past Felix to take his, something slipped half out of the pocket of her apron and hung there for a moment before she pushed it back.

It was a brown leather glove, lined with wool and worn smooth at the fingertips. It was the twin of the one lying beside Felix's plate.

## [c5] Candles

### [p17]
Felix saw it too. Ruth watched him see it. He half rose from his chair and said, "Excuse me, but I think that's..."

No sooner had he opened his mouth than every light in the Glasswater Hotel went out.

There was a moment of perfect darkness, followed by the kind of noise eight adults make when they discover that they are not as calm as they had believed. Bev shrieked and then laughed at herself. Desmond knocked over his tea. Ambrose said "Good heavens" three times in exactly the same tone. Somewhere in the corridor, Mr Crane could be heard crashing into a table and apologising to it.

Then, one by one, the candles took over. They had been there all along, of course, burning in their tall golden holders down the middle of the table, but in the electric light nobody had needed them. Now the room shrank to their small trembling circles. The mirrors filled with tiny flames. The green walls went almost black. Faces floated above the white tablecloth like portraits in an old painting.

"The storm," said Mr Crane, reappearing in the doorway with a torch that did not work. "The line from the village. It happens every winter. There is a generator, but it is, er, in a shed. Outside."

"Then somebody had better go and start it," said Priya.

"I'll come," said Theo. "I've got a torch on my phone."

"So have I," said Bev, and the three of them went out into the dark, followed, after a moment's hesitation, by Desmond.

Ruth looked round for Maren. Maren had gone. Ruth had not heard her go.

Left alone with the candles and the others, Ruth picked one up and went after her.

### [p18]
The kitchen, by candlelight, was a different place. The copper pans glowed like a row of small suns. Maren was standing at the counter with her back to the door, chopping something very fine and very fast by the light of a single candle, as though the dark made no difference to her at all.

She did not turn round. "You followed me."

"I did," said Ruth. "It's a habit. I spent my whole working life following words to see where they went."

"I know," said Maren, and then, a little too quickly, "I mean, I can imagine."

Ruth put her candle down on the counter. On the wall above it, held up with small silver pins, were rows and rows of crosswords cut out of newspapers. Dozens of them. Perhaps hundreds. The paper of the oldest had gone the colour of toast, and every one of them had been completed in pencil. Ruth leaned closer, but the light was poor and her glasses were in the dining room.

"Do you do them?" she asked.

"Every week." Maren's knife did not stop. "Somebody gave me a taste for them a long time ago."

"So did I, once," said Ruth. "Do them, I mean. I haven't finished one in six years."

"Why not?"

It was a question that nobody had ever asked her, and Ruth was surprised to find that she had an answer. "Because when I set them, I always knew there was somebody at the other end. Some stranger with a pencil, on a Saturday morning, trying to understand me. When I stopped, it felt like talking to an empty room."

Maren was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "It wasn't empty," and moved the candle further along the counter, so that the wall of crosswords sank back into the dark.

### [p19]
"Felix thinks that glove in your pocket is his," said Ruth.

"Does he?"

"So do I."

Maren put down her knife at last. She wiped her hands on a cloth, slowly, the way people do when they are deciding how much to say.

"Gloves are like socks," she said. "Everybody has one that doesn't match."

"That," said Ruth, "is not an answer. That is a very well-made sentence pretending to be one."

In spite of everything, Maren laughed, a short, surprised laugh that seemed to have escaped without permission. Then, under her breath, she began to hum. It was only a few notes, a small falling tune that went up at the end like a question, and she stopped almost as soon as she had started. But Ruth had heard it, and she suspected that Theo would have recognised it at once.

"What's that tune?"

"I don't know. Something I heard once." Maren picked up a tray. "I'd rather not talk about it, if you don't mind. Not yet."

"Not yet," Ruth repeated. "So there will be a later?"

"I hope so."

From the dining room came a burst of noise: Bev's laugh, Felix sneezing violently, and Ambrose saying "Good heavens" for what must have been the fourth time. It turned out, when Ruth got back, that Bev and Desmond had taken a wrong turn on the way to the generator and opened the door of Mr Crane's office by mistake. Inside, in a cardboard box lined with an old hotel towel, lay a thin grey cat and four very small kittens.

"She came in out of the snow a week ago," said Mr Crane miserably. "The owner doesn't allow animals. I've been hiding her. I'm terribly sorry. I'm afraid I'm a very bad liar."

"We noticed," said Ambrose. "Never have I met a worse one."

Felix, still sneezing, was already on his knees beside the box.

### [p20]
It was Theo, in the end, who found the generator, and it was Mr Crane who knew how to start it. Everyone else drifted back to the dining room in ones and twos, carrying candles and still talking about the kittens. Felix had declared the mother cat thin but perfectly healthy, and himself allergic but perfectly happy. Mr Crane had been forgiven by everyone except, possibly, himself.

Having found her way back to her place beside the empty chair, Ruth sat down and looked at the invitation again in the candlelight. Daryl Brie. The letters stared back at her and refused to rearrange themselves. She had the strong and unpleasant feeling that she had already been given every piece of the answer, and had simply not been looking at the pieces in the right order.

Somewhere beneath the floor, an engine coughed, caught, and began to hum. The lamps shaped like tulips flickered, went out again, and then came on all together, flooding the room with light.

Everyone blinked. Bev clapped. Desmond said, "There we are, then." Ambrose began to say something about the reliability of modern engineering and stopped halfway through the sentence.

He was looking at the end of the table. So, a moment later, was everyone else.

The ninth chair was still there, pushed neatly in. The ninth card still said Reserved. But the ninth place was bare. The white plate, the silver, the crystal glass, even the folded napkin: all of it was gone, as though no one had ever meant to sit there at all.

## [c6] The Anagram

### [p21]
"Right," said Bev. "Who's got it?"

Nobody had it. Everybody turned out their pockets anyway, because Bev insisted, and Ambrose, after a short and dignified protest, produced from his jacket four red pencils, a handkerchief and a train timetable from nineteen ninety-eight, but no plate.

"Somebody must have taken it while the lights were out," said Priya. "Plates do not walk."

"It is said that this hotel has a ghost," offered Mr Crane, who seemed to feel that he owed them something after the kittens. "A lady in a green dress. She is believed to have danced in this very room every night of the summer of nineteen twenty-six." He caught Ambrose's eye. "Well. It is said. By guests. Mostly after dinner. Shall I tell you the rest?"

"I would rather you didn't," said Ambrose.

"Ghosts don't need plates, love," said Desmond. "That's the whole point of being a ghost."

The argument went round the table twice more, faster each time, like a dog chasing its tail. Theo thought it was Mr Crane again. Mr Crane thought it was the ghost. Felix, who had a kitten asleep inside his jumper and seemed to have stopped caring about anything else, thought it might be best to wait and see. Agnes, as before, said nothing. But she was looking down the corridor towards the kitchen doors, and so, Ruth noticed, was Desmond.

Ruth was not looking at the doors. She had taken the pencil from behind her own ear, where she had kept one for thirty years out of habit, and she was writing on the back of the invitation, very small.

Nine letters. She wrote them out in a circle, the way she had taught herself to do at nineteen, so that no letter had a beginning or an end.

### [p22]
It is a strange fact, well known to anyone who has ever set a crossword, that the hardest answers to see are the ones closest to you. Ruth had known it for forty years. She had built a whole career on it. And yet she had carried this signature around for a week as though it were somebody else's puzzle.

Nine letters in a circle. She looked at them and let her eyes go soft, the way you look at the pattern in a carpet until it begins to move.

Crane, she thought first. A crane was a bird. Perhaps the bird was the point. But there was no letter C in the circle, and in any case Mr Crane was at that moment sitting on the floor of his office, feeding a kitten from a teaspoon, and nobody who looked like that had ever planned anything in his life. A red herring, then. A very kind, very anxious red herring, but a red herring all the same.

Then she thought of the train, and of her own silly joke. Bridal Rye. The same nine letters. She had found that one in minutes, on purpose, to make herself laugh. What had she not found?

Not only had she been looking at the wrong words, she realised; she had been looking at them the wrong way round.

Night Owl, she thought. That was what they had called her on Saturday mornings for twenty-six years. A creature that stays awake while everybody else is asleep. And the opposite of a night owl, as every child knows, is...

The pencil moved almost by itself, picking the letters out of the circle one by one, and when it stopped, there were two words written on the card.

Early Bird.

Only then did she understand what the signature had been saying all along. It was not a name. It was a reply.

### [p23]
Somebody had known that she was Night Owl. Somebody had known it fourteen years ago, when almost nobody else did, and had signed this invitation with its opposite.

And Ruth knew exactly whom she had told. She had told a nine-year-old in a red woolly hat, at about midnight, at a big table under a library window, because the child could not sleep and had asked her why she was not sleeping either. "I'm a night owl," Ruth had said. "It's my job. Don't tell anyone; it's a secret." And the girl had whispered back, very seriously, that she would never tell anybody as long as she lived.

She had kept her word, apparently. The only person she had ever told it to was Ruth herself.

It did not take a crossword setter to put two and two together after that. Who in a hotel is an early bird? A cook. Someone who starts at five, because bread doesn't wait. Someone who puts three sugars in a taxi driver's tea before he has asked for it. Someone who keeps a pencil behind her ear. Someone who hums a tune that a sixteen-year-old wrote one afternoon and played only once. Someone with a wall of Saturday crosswords, every one of them finished. Someone with a glove in her pocket that she had never quite been able to give back.

And the soup. Above all, the soup. Thick and dark red, with cream and black pepper, exactly like the soup in a paper cup from the café at Calderbury Central: the soup Ruth had bought fourteen years ago because a small girl on a bench had looked up at her and said one word.

What that word had been, Ruth still could not remember. It was the one piece that would not fit.

### [p24]
She looked up. Around the table, the argument had stopped. One by one, the others had noticed her face, and one by one they had gone quiet, the way people do in a theatre when the lights go down.

"Ruth?" said Agnes. "You've solved it, haven't you?"

"I think so," said Ruth. "I should have solved it a week ago. If I weren't so vain, I would have seen it on the very first day."

"Well?" said Bev. "Don't keep us waiting. Who is he?"

"It isn't a he," said Ruth. "And it isn't Daryl Brie. It never was."

She turned the invitation round so that they could see the two words she had written under the circle. Ambrose put on his glasses to read them. Priya read them aloud. Theo said, "Early bird?" and then, slowly, "Oh," and then, much more slowly, "Oh."

Behind them, at the end of the corridor, the kitchen doors creaked.

Nobody turned round at once. It was as if nobody quite dared to. Then they heard footsteps, light and quick, coming down the corridor towards the dining room, and Maren came in carrying a tray.

On the tray were a white plate, a set of silver, a crystal glass and a folded napkin. She did not look at any of them. She walked to the end of the table and laid the ninth place again, neatly and exactly, with the care of somebody who had practised it many times in an empty room.

Then she pulled out the ninth chair, and sat down in it.

## [c7] The Ninth Guest

### [p25]
For a long moment, the only sounds in the room were the snow against the windows and, very faintly, a kitten complaining somewhere down the corridor.

"My name is Maren," she said at last. "But you know that. What you don't know is that fourteen years ago tonight, I was nine years old, and I was sitting on a suitcase at Calderbury Central station in a red hat, and I had no idea where I was."

Nobody said anything. Desmond's hand had found the yellow star and closed around it.

"I was supposed to be going to my aunt's," Maren went on. "My mother put me on the train, and my aunt was going to meet me. Then the storm came, and the train stopped, and my aunt's car got stuck in the snow somewhere on the other side of the city, and the phone lines were down. I didn't know any of that. I just knew that nobody came."

She looked round the table, at each of them in turn.

"And then everybody came. One after another. A lady put a plaster on my knee and gave me sweets to hold. A gentleman lent me his coat. A boy played me a song. A woman sat with me all night and never once let me feel lost. A man gave me his gloves. A driver let me ride for nothing and gave me a ticket anyway. A librarian gave me her own card. And in the morning, a taxi driver took me home and gave me his lucky star." She smiled, a little unsteadily. "None of you knew about the others. None of you did anything enormous. But never in my whole life have I been looked after so well by so many people who owed me nothing at all."

### [p26]
"How on earth did you find us?" asked Priya. It was, Ruth thought, very like Priya to need the method before the feeling.

"Slowly," said Maren. "It took me six years. Agnes's name was on the card, so that was easy. The bus company still had a list of which driver had used the star punch. Felix's mother had sewn his name into the glove. Mr Hale's name was inside the collar of his coat, which is how I sent it back. Desmond's taxi number was scratched on the back of the star. Theo was the hardest. In the end, I asked at every music shop in Calderbury about a red-haired boy who used to play in the station, and an old man in the last one laughed and said he still owed him money for strings."

"I do," said Theo. "I'd forgotten."

"And me?" said Ruth.

Maren turned to her. "You told me your secret. Do you remember? You said you were a night owl. When you retired, your newspaper printed a little piece about you, with your real name in it for the first time, and a photograph. I'd been doing your puzzles every Saturday since I was eleven. I cut it out. It's on my wall, in the middle of all the others."

"And the hotel?" said Ambrose. "One assumes that you did not buy it."

Maren laughed. "I'm the cook here, nine months of the year. When I told the owner what I wanted to do, she gave me the whole hotel for one night in the closed season, and Mr Crane to help." She glanced at the door. "He was sworn to secrecy. He's been terrible at it."

"You needn't have gone to all this trouble, you know," said Bev. "You could have just written."

"I know," said Maren. "I wanted to cook."

### [p27]
"But the plate," said Theo. "Why nine? And why did you take it away?"

Maren looked down at the empty white plate in front of her.

"It isn't mine," she said. "That's what I wanted to explain, and I couldn't, so when the lights went out, I panicked and took it back to the kitchen. The ninth place isn't for the host. It's for her. For the girl that night." She stood up. "Wait."

She went to the kitchen and came back with a covered dish, which she set down on the ninth plate. When she lifted the cover, there was a cheese sandwich cut into four triangles, and a paper cup of tomato soup with a swirl of cream on top.

Ruth put her hand over her mouth.

"You bought me that," Maren said to her. "On the bench. It was the first thing I'd eaten since breakfast. It was the first thing I ever learned to cook, too, when I was twelve. Everything I've cooked since then started with that soup." She hesitated. "If you hadn't all stopped that night, I wouldn't be a cook now. I'm not sure I'd be anything very much."

"Nonsense," said Ambrose gently. "You would have become something remarkable whatever happened. But I am very glad that we were part of it."

"So am I," said Bev, wiping her eyes with a napkin and not pretending otherwise.

"So am I," said Felix, into his jumper.

Ruth picked up the puzzle book and opened it at the last page. The empty squares looked up at her. Six of them.

"I can't remember," she said. "I've tried all night. I wrote that clue at dawn, while you were asleep, and I knew the answer then. I don't now."

"I know," said Maren. "I didn't either, for years. I thought it was sorry. It isn't."

### [p28]
"Then what was it?"

Maren sat down again in the ninth chair. For a moment she looked exactly nine years old, sitting on a suitcase under a departures board that would not change its mind.

"You walked up to me," she said, "and you didn't ask me my name, or where my mum was, or any of the things the grown-ups kept asking. You just sat down on the bench, and you looked at me, and you asked me how I was. And I said..."

"Hungry," said Ruth.

The word arrived in her mouth before it arrived in her mind, the way the best answers always had. Hungry. A small, cold, very serious voice under a red hat. Not lost, not frightened, not sorry. Hungry. And Ruth, who had only meant to ask whether the child was all right, had gone straight to the café and bought a sandwich and a soup, and had not gone home until the morning.

"Six letters," said Maren.

Ruth took the pencil from behind her ear and wrote the word into the last six squares, slowly, one letter at a time. Then she closed the book. It was, she realised, the first puzzle she had finished in six years.

Nobody went to bed that night. At about four, Theo opened his guitar case at last and played a small falling tune that went up at the end like a question, and Maren hummed it with him. At five, she went into the kitchen, because bread doesn't wait.

Ruth followed her, as she had followed her before.

"Good morning," said the early bird.

"Good night," said the night owl, and sat down to watch the bread rise.
