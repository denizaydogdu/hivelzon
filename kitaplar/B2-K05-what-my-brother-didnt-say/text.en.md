## [c1] Two Bags

### [p01]
My brother came home from university with two bags and no explanation.

It was a Tuesday evening in late April, and the rain had been falling since lunchtime. I was doing my history homework at the kitchen table when I heard a car stop outside. Then the front door opened, and there he was: Daniel, wet hair, an old green jacket, one bag on each shoulder.

"Hi, Soph," he said, as if he had only gone to the shop for milk.

Mum came out of the living room and stopped in the doorway. For a second, nobody moved. Then she smiled a very big smile, the kind of smile she uses at parents' evenings.

"Daniel! What a lovely surprise! Are you hungry? You must be hungry. Sit down, sit down."

He sat down. He didn't say why he was home. Mum didn't ask, which was strange, because Mum asks about everything. She asks the postman about his knees.

I looked at the bags by the door. They were full. Not home-for-the-weekend full. Home-for-good full.

Our town is called Kilbrannock, and it is small enough that everyone notices whose car is parked outside whose house. By the morning, the whole town would know that the Byrne boy was back.

### [p02]
Dad came home at half past six, the way he always did. He works in an insurance office in the next town, and he has made the same journey every day for twenty years. He put his keys in the bowl by the door, and then he saw the bags.

"Daniel," he said. Just that.

"Hi, Dad."

Dinner was the quietest meal in the history of our family. Mum talks when she is nervous, whereas Dad goes silent. So Mum talked about the weather, the neighbour's new dog and the price of petrol, and Dad ate his potatoes slowly and looked at Daniel the way you look at a bill you don't understand. Daniel looked at his plate.

Finally, Dad put down his fork. "Are you going to tell us what's going on?"

"I've left the course," Daniel said.

"You've what?"

"I've left. I'm not going back."

"You're in your third year of engineering. You have one year left." Dad's voice was calm, but his ears had gone red, which is always a bad sign. "Why?"

Daniel opened his mouth, and for a moment I thought he was finally going to explain. Then he closed it again.

"I'd rather not talk about it yet," he said.

Dad stood up, took his plate to the sink and went out into the garden, although it was still raining.

### [p03]
Later that night, I knocked on Daniel's door. His room still had the same posters on the wall from when he was sixteen, and it smelled of dust, because nobody had slept there since Christmas.

He was sitting on the bed, looking at his phone without really reading it.

"Are you ill?" I asked. It was the first thing I could think of.

"No."

"Did you fail your exams?"

"No, Soph. My marks were fine."

"Then what? Did something happen? Did someone do something to you?"

"Nobody did anything to me." He gave me a tired smile. "I'm fine. Honestly. I just need a bit of time."

He told me he was fine, but people who are fine don't come home in the middle of the term with two bags. I asked him whether he had a girlfriend, whether he owed anyone money, whether he had joined a band. He said no to everything and then asked me to turn the light off on my way out.

In bed, I lay awake and listened to the rain. Through the wall, I could hear Mum and Dad talking in low voices in their room. I couldn't hear the words, only the shape of them: Dad's short and hard, Mum's long and soft, like waves on a stone.

### [p04]
The next morning, Daniel had already gone when I came downstairs at half seven. His bike, which had been in the garden shed since he left for university, wasn't there either.

"Where's Daniel?" I asked.

"Out," Mum said brightly. "He must have left very early. Isn't that nice? Getting some fresh air."

Mum was being far too cheerful. When things go wrong in our house, she cleans. That morning, she had already cleaned the oven, and it was not even eight o'clock.

I ate my toast in the living room, in front of the old photograph above the fireplace. I had seen it a thousand times, but that morning I looked at it properly.

It was a picture of Dad when he was about twenty, the same age as Daniel was now. He was standing in a big wooden shed, in a dirty shirt with the sleeves rolled up. Beside him was half a boat. It had no top yet, just the curved bones of its sides, like the skeleton of a whale. Dad had one hand on it, and he was laughing at the camera.

I had never seen him laugh like that in real life. On the shelf under the photo stood three little wooden fish, which had been there for as long as I could remember.

"Mum," I said, "whose boat was that?"

"Hmm? Oh, that. Nobody's, really. It was never finished." She turned on the hoover before I could ask anything else.

### [p05]
Daniel came home that evening at seven. He said hello, went straight upstairs and had a shower before dinner, which was not like him at all. When he sat down at the table, I noticed his hands.

They were covered in tiny cuts, some new and some already healing. There was a plaster on his thumb. And on the sleeve of his jumper, which he must have forgotten to change, there was a thin line of something pale and soft, like dust. I leaned closer. It smelled of wood. Sawdust.

"What have you done to your hands?" Mum asked.

"Nothing. I've been helping a friend with something."

"What friend?" Dad asked. "What something?"

"Just a project."

Dad put down his knife. "Daniel, you have left a university course that we have been paying for for three years. You are twenty-one years old. If you want to live in this house, you could at least tell us what you are doing all day."

"I will," said Daniel quietly. "Soon. Not yet."

"That's not good enough."

"I know," Daniel said. "I'm sorry."

Then he did something strange. He looked up at the photo above the fireplace, just for a second, and looked away again.

Nobody else saw it. But I did.

## [c2] The Boatyard

### [p06]
I decided to follow him.

I know that sounds dramatic. If I had been a more patient person, I would have waited for him to tell me. But I am seventeen, and I had been waiting for two days, which felt like two years.

So on Thursday, I set my alarm for half five. It was still dark when I heard his door open. I got dressed quickly, put on my running shoes and waited at the window until I saw his bike light go down the hill towards the harbour.

He can't have been going to a lecture, I thought. Maybe he had a job. He might have found work at the fish market.

I told Mum I was going for an early run. It was nearly true. I ran.

Kilbrannock wakes up slowly. At that time of the morning, the only people in the streets were the man who delivers bread to the shop and a few fishermen walking down to their boats. The sea was grey and flat, and the gulls were already complaining about something.

Daniel didn't stop at the fish market. He rode past it and along the old road by the water, the one that goes to the end of the bay. Nobody uses that road much any more. At the end of it, there is nothing except rocks, long grass and the old boatyard, which closed years ago. Or at least, I thought it had.

### [p07]
The boatyard was a long wooden shed with a roof that came down almost to the ground on one side. Some of the windows were broken, and the paint on the doors had been washed away by years of rain and wind. In front of it, a narrow stone ramp went down into the water.

I had always thought it was empty. But that morning, there was smoke coming out of a metal pipe on the roof, and Daniel's bike was leaning against the wall.

I hid behind a pile of old wooden boxes and waited. My heart was beating so loudly that I was sure someone would hear it. From inside the shed came a sound I didn't recognise at first: a long, smooth, scraping sound, again and again, like someone breathing slowly. Then the short knock of a hammer. Then the scraping again.

I moved closer to one of the broken windows and looked inside.

The shed was full of soft yellow light. There were tools on every wall, hundreds of them, hanging in neat rows. The floor was covered in sawdust and curly pieces of wood. And in the middle of the shed, resting on wooden blocks, was a boat.

It wasn't finished. But it was beautiful.

### [p08]
Daniel was standing next to the boat with a flat tool in his hands, pushing it along the edge of a long piece of wood. Each time he pushed, a thin curl of wood came off and fell to the floor. He looked completely calm. I hadn't seen him look like that for a long time, maybe for years.

Next to him was an old man in a blue work coat. He was small and thin, with white hair that stood up on his head as if it had just had a shock. He was holding a piece of wood up to the light and looking along it with one eye closed.

"That's better," the old man said. "Feel it now."

Daniel ran his hand along the wood. "It's smooth."

"Smooth isn't enough. It has to be right. The sea doesn't care about smooth. The sea cares about right."

I must have moved, because a piece of glass cracked under my shoe. Both of them turned towards the window.

There was no point in hiding. I stood up, with my face burning, and walked round to the door.

Daniel didn't look angry. He didn't even look surprised. He just sighed, like someone who has lost a game he always knew he was going to lose.

### [p09]
"Sophie," he said. "Of course."

The old man put down his piece of wood and looked at me for a long moment. Then he smiled, and his whole face folded into lines, like a map.

"You'll be the sister," he said. "Come in, come in. You're letting the cold in. He said you'd come."

I looked at Daniel. "You told him I'd follow you?"

"I told him you'd find out within a week," Daniel said. "You've been watching me like a detective since Tuesday. This is Mr Doyle. He built boats here for fifty years. This was his boatyard. It still is, actually."

"I thought it was closed," I said.

"It was closed to the public," said Mr Doyle. "That's not the same as closed. I've been coming here every day since my wife died, and that was eleven years ago. A man needs somewhere to go in the morning."

I walked slowly around the boat. Up close, it was bigger than I had expected, long enough for three or four people. The top part of the sides looked new and pale. The bottom part, however, was darker, almost brown, as if it had been waiting there much longer than the rest.

Near the back of the boat, on the inside, someone had cut a tiny fish into the wood, no bigger than my thumb.

"So," I said, turning to Daniel. "What is this?"

## [c3] Apprentice

### [p10]
We sat on boxes next to the stove, and Mr Doyle made tea in a pot that looked older than our house. Then Daniel told me everything. Or I thought it was everything.

He had been unhappy at university since the first year. Not because the course was too difficult, but because it felt like the wrong life. He had spent three years sitting in front of screens, designing things he would never touch.

"I kept thinking, in twenty years I'll be sitting in an office somewhere, and I'll have forgotten what anything feels like," he said. "Wood, rope, water. Real things."

Last summer, while he was home, he had come down to the old boatyard, looking for a quiet place to read. He found Mr Doyle, who was trying to fix a window on his own and failing. Daniel helped him. Then he came back the next day, and the next.

"By September, I didn't want to go back," he said. "But I went, because of Dad. I tried for another seven months. And then one day in April, I just couldn't do it any more."

"So now you're..."

"An apprentice," said Mr Doyle proudly. "My first apprentice in over twenty years. And the best one I've ever had, although I'd never say that in front of him."

"You just did," said Daniel.

### [p11]
"So why haven't you told Mum and Dad?" I asked. "It's not a crime. You're building a boat, not robbing a bank."

Daniel looked into his tea. Mr Doyle suddenly became very interested in a tool on the wall.

"It's complicated," Daniel said.

"Dad thinks you've been sitting in a café all day doing nothing. If you told him the truth, he would at least know you were working."

"If I told him now, he'd come down here and tell me I was wasting my life. You know he would. And then he'd never see it properly." He put his cup down. "I want to show him when it's finished. Not before. When he can see the whole thing, not half of it."

"See what?"

"The boat, Soph."

It felt like a very small answer to a very big question, and I was sure there was something he wasn't telling me. Maybe it was the way Mr Doyle kept looking at Daniel, as if he were waiting for him to say more. Or maybe it was the little fish. I didn't know why, but I kept thinking about it.

"When will it be finished?" I asked.

"Saturday week," said Mr Doyle. "We'll be putting her in the water at ten in the morning, if the weather is kind."

"By then, I'll have finished my first real boat," Daniel said quietly.

### [p12]
That evening, things got worse at home.

Dad had met Mrs Kelly from the post office at the petrol station, and Mrs Kelly had told him that she had seen Daniel cycling along the old bay road every morning. Mrs Kelly sees everything. People say that if a cat crosses the road in Kilbrannock, Mrs Kelly will know its name by lunchtime.

"The bay road," Dad said at dinner. "There's nothing down there."

"There's the sea," said Daniel.

"Don't be clever with me." Dad's voice was quiet, which was worse than shouting. "Your mother and I have been worrying about you all week. We have been lying awake at night. And you're out looking at the sea?"

"I'm not just looking at it."

"Then what are you doing? I'm asking you a simple question."

Daniel said nothing. Under the table, I saw his hands close and then open again.

"I gave up a lot," Dad said slowly, "so that you two would have choices I didn't have. Good choices. Safe ones. And you have thrown one away without even talking to me."

"I know you gave up a lot," Daniel said. "I know more than you think."

Dad looked at him strangely. Then he stood up and left the room, and a minute later we heard the car start.

Mum began to clean the cooker, which was already clean.

### [p13]
I went back to the boatyard the next afternoon, straight after school. Daniel was out buying screws in the next town, so Mr Doyle was alone, sitting by the stove with a newspaper he wasn't reading.

"He's had a hard night, your brother," he said. "He came in at six with eyes like a sad dog."

"Dad's really angry," I said. "I don't understand why Daniel won't just tell him. It would make everything so much easier."

Mr Doyle folded his newspaper carefully. "Easier isn't always better. Some things should be said with words, and some things should be said another way."

I walked over to the boat again and put my hand on the dark wood at the bottom. It was hard and cool, and very smooth.

"This part is older," I said. "Isn't it? It's a different colour."

He looked at me over his glasses. "You've got good eyes. Yes. That part was built a long time ago. The boat was started by someone else, and then it was left here. It has been sitting under a sheet in the corner of this shed for longer than you've been alive."

"Who started it?"

"Ah," said Mr Doyle. "That's your brother's story to tell, not mine. I promised him." He stood up and picked up a brush. "But I'll tell you this much. Wood remembers who touched it."

### [p14]
When Daniel came back, I was holding a brush too. Mr Doyle had decided that if I was going to stand around asking questions, I could at least make myself useful. He showed me how to put oil on the wood, thin and even, always in the direction of the lines.

We worked together for an hour without saying much. It was the nicest hour I had spent with my brother since he left for university. The radio was playing old songs, the stove was warm, and outside the sea was coming in slowly over the ramp.

When it was time to go home, Daniel walked with me to the door.

"Soph," he said. "I need you to do something for me, and I know it's not fair."

"What?"

"Don't tell them. Not Mum, not Dad. Not until Saturday week." He looked at me seriously. "Please. I've been planning this for months. If they find out now, the whole thing will be ruined."

"Ruined how? Daniel, what is this really about?"

He was quiet for a moment. "You'll see on Saturday. I promise. Just keep it for me. Can you do that?"

I should have said no. I should have told him that secrets in our family were like water in a boat: they always find a way out. Instead, I said yes.

## [c4] Keeping It

### [p15]
Keeping a secret is harder than it sounds, especially when you live with someone who cleans the oven every time she is worried.

For the next few days, I felt as if I had a small, hot stone in my pocket. Every time Dad looked at Daniel with that tired, disappointed face, I wanted to shout the truth across the table. Every time Mum asked me whether I knew anything, I looked at my plate and said, "No idea." I hated how easy the lie was.

If Daniel had told them the truth in April, none of this would have happened. But then again, if he had told them, Dad would probably have stopped him.

Daniel was out from six in the morning until after dark. He came home smelling of wood, oil and the sea, and he was so tired that he sometimes fell asleep on the sofa before dinner. Despite everything, he looked happier than I had seen him in years. He even started singing in the shower.

Dad noticed this too, and it seemed to make him angrier.

"He's enjoying himself," he said to Mum one night, when he thought I was upstairs. "He's thrown away his future, and he's enjoying himself."

"Maybe he's found something," Mum said.

"There's nothing to find in this town. I should know."

I wished I could tell him he was wrong. But I didn't know yet how wrong he was.

### [p16]
On Wednesday, Mum drove me to the dentist in the next town. Halfway there, she turned the radio off.

"I've been washing your brother's clothes for twenty-one years, Sophie," she said, with her eyes on the road. "I know what sawdust is. And yesterday you came home with oil on your hands. You've been with him, haven't you?"

"I promised him," I said quietly. "I can't tell you where he goes. I'm sorry."

To my surprise, Mum didn't get angry. She was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "You don't need to tell me. There's only one place in Kilbrannock that smells like that."

"The boatyard," I said, before I could stop myself.

Mum nodded slowly. "Tommy Doyle's place. I didn't know he was still going down there."

"You know Mr Doyle?"

"Everyone knows Tommy Doyle. Your dad worked for him once, you know. A long time ago, before you were born. Before Daniel was born, even."

I stared at her. "Dad? Dad worked with boats?"

"He was very good at it. Better than good." She smiled, but it was a sad smile. "Then Daniel was on the way, and boats don't pay for baby clothes. So he took the job at the insurance office. It was meant to be just for a year or two."

"And he never went back."

"No," said Mum. "He never went back."

### [p17]
That night, I stood in front of the photograph again for a long time.

Dad, at twenty, laughing. The half-built boat beside him. The big wooden shed behind him, which I now realised I had seen before. The same long roof. The same tools hanging in neat rows on the wall. The photo had been taken in Mr Doyle's boatyard.

I picked up one of the three little wooden fish from the shelf. It fitted perfectly in my hand, smooth and light. I had always thought Dad had bought them at a market somewhere.

Something was moving in the back of my head, like a fish under dark water, but I couldn't quite catch it.

Mum had promised not to say anything to Dad until Saturday. "If your brother wants to tell him himself, he should be allowed to," she said. "Although I wish he'd hurry up. I can't clean this house any more than I already have."

On Friday evening, Daniel finally spoke at dinner.

"There's something I'd like you to come and see," he said. "Tomorrow at ten. Down at the old boatyard."

Dad went very still. "The boatyard," he said. "Doyle's?"

"Yes."

"Is that where you've been going?" His voice was strange, not angry exactly. "Every day?"

"Yes. Will you come?"

### [p18]
Dad looked at Mum. Mum looked at the salt. I looked at Dad's hands, which were holding the edge of the table very hard.

"No," he said.

"Dad..."

"I'm not going down there, Daniel. I know what you're doing, and I won't be part of it. I'm not going to stand on that ramp and clap while you make the same mistake I..." He stopped.

"The same mistake you what?" Daniel asked softly.

Dad didn't answer. He stood up, pushed his chair back under the table and went upstairs. We heard the bedroom door close, not loudly. That was the worst part. If he had banged it, it would have felt more normal.

Daniel sat without moving for a long time. Then he said, "Well. I thought that might happen."

"He'll come," said Mum, although she didn't sound sure.

"He won't," said Daniel. "But I'm doing it anyway. The boat will be put in the water tomorrow, with him or without him. Mr Doyle has had the ramp cleared, and the life jackets have been checked twice."

Later, I found him in the garden, sitting on the wet steps in the dark.

"Are you OK?" I asked.

"Not really." He looked up at the lit window of Mum and Dad's room. "If only he'd come just once. Just to look. That's all I want."

## [c5] Launch

### [p19]
Saturday morning was cold and bright, with a light wind coming off the sea. By half nine, there were more people at the old boatyard than there had been in twenty years.

I don't know how they found out. Actually, I do. Mrs Kelly from the post office was standing at the front with a flask of tea, so the whole town must have heard about it by Friday night. There were fishermen, neighbours, two of my teachers and a lot of children who had been told that there might be cake.

Mr Doyle had put on a jacket and tie for the occasion. The boat sat on a wooden frame with wheels at the top of the ramp, ready to roll down into the water. In the morning light, she looked even more beautiful than she had in the shed. The new wood shone like honey, and the old wood at the bottom looked dark and deep, like the root of a strong tree.

Daniel was standing next to her in a bright orange life jacket, checking the ropes for the third time. He kept looking up at the road.

Mum stood next to me, holding my arm. "He'll come," she said quietly, more to herself than to me.

At five to ten, the road was still empty.

At ten o'clock, it was still empty.

Mr Doyle looked at Daniel. Daniel looked at the road one more time, and then he nodded.

### [p20]
"Right," said Mr Doyle in a loud voice. "Let's get this lady wet."

Everyone laughed. Two fishermen took the ropes at the back, and slowly, carefully, the boat began to move down the ramp.

That was when I heard the car.

It came along the bay road much too fast and stopped suddenly at the end of it. The door opened, and Dad got out. He was wearing his office trousers and an old jumper, and his hair was standing up as if he had been pushing his hands through it all night. He must have changed his mind at the very last minute.

"Wait," he called. "Tommy, wait."

The fishermen stopped. The whole crowd turned round. Dad walked down to the ramp slowly, as if the ground might move under his feet. He didn't look at Daniel. He didn't look at anyone. He was looking at the boat.

He stopped beside her and put one hand on the dark wood at the bottom. Then he walked along her side, all the way to the back, touching the wood as he went. At the back, he leaned over and looked inside.

I knew what he had found before he said anything. The tiny fish in the wood, no bigger than a thumb.

Dad stood up. His face had gone completely white.

"This is mine," he said. "This is my boat."

### [p21]
Nobody said anything. Even the gulls seemed to stop.

"You started her the year before Daniel was born," said Mr Doyle gently. "You built the bottom and half the sides. Then you came in one Monday and told me you were finishing, and you never came back. You asked me to get rid of her. I didn't. I put a sheet over her and waited."

"Twenty-two years," said Dad.

"Twenty-two years," Mr Doyle agreed. "Wood is patient. More patient than people."

Daniel came and stood on the other side of the boat. "I found her last summer," he said. "I knew her from the photo, and from the fish. Then I thought, if you could see her finished, you might understand why I had to leave university. I didn't want to explain it with words. I wanted to show you."

Dad looked at him across the boat. "Why didn't you just tell me?"

"Because you'd have told me to stop. And I'd probably have stopped. And then neither of us would ever have seen her in the water."

Dad's eyes were wet. He tried to say something, and couldn't. He looked at the boat, then at Daniel's hands, which were covered in small cuts, just like his own hands in the photo.

Then he laughed. It was a strange, broken laugh, but it was the laugh from the photograph. I had finally heard it in real life.

### [p22]
"Well," said Dad, wiping his eyes with his sleeve. "Are we putting her in the water or not?"

"Not without a life jacket, you're not," said Mr Doyle, and he threw him one.

So they did it together. Dad and Daniel, side by side, held the ropes while the boat rolled slowly down the ramp and into the sea. She went in with a soft splash and sat on the water as if she had always been there, light and straight and proud. The crowd cheered. Mrs Kelly cried into her tea. Mum held my arm so tightly that I had marks on it for two days.

Then Dad climbed in, and Daniel climbed in after him, and they rowed slowly out into the bay, both wearing bright orange life jackets, which made them look less like heroes and more like two large carrots. I have never loved them more.

Afterwards, people asked me if I had known all along. I always say no, because it's true. I knew where my brother went every morning. I didn't know why.

He came home with two bags and no explanation. He was wrong about one thing, though. He did explain. He just didn't use words.

By next summer, Dad will have learned to use his old tools again, and Daniel will be building his second boat. This one, they are building together.
