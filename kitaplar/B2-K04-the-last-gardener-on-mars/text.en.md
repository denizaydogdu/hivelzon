## [c1] Yellow Leaves

### [p01]
On the colony's two-hundredth day of spring, every plant in the greenhouse began to die at once.

Kofi Asante noticed it first, although at the time he did not understand what he was seeing. He had been working in the greenhouse since six o'clock that morning, checking the water pipes in the bean rows, and he had almost finished when he saw the leaf.

It was yellow. Not the bright yellow of a lemon, but the tired, pale yellow of old paper. He touched it, and it came away in his hand.

Kofi was sixteen, and he had been born on Mars. He had never seen a forest, a field or a river. Everything green that he had ever known was inside this greenhouse, a long, curved building of glass and steel on the edge of Kestrel Colony, where four hundred people lived under a pink and orange sky. For most of them, the greenhouse was simply the place where food came from. For Kofi, it was the only place on the planet that smelled alive.

He looked along the row. Another yellow leaf. Then another. By the time he had reached the end of the row, he had counted forty-one.

"That's strange," he said to nobody.

Then he walked to the next row, and the next, and his heart began to beat faster.

### [p02]
Life in Kestrel Colony was quiet, organised and careful, because on Mars careless people did not usually get a second chance. The water was taken from ice deep under the ground. The walls had been built thick enough to keep out the cold and the dangerous part of the sunlight.

The colony even had its own calendar, which had been copied from Earth's: four seasons of about ninety days each. Outside, the real Martian seasons were almost twice as long, but nobody paid much attention to them. Inside the walls, it was spring because the calendar said it was spring. In a way, it was always spring.

The greenhouse produced beans, wheat, potatoes, tomatoes, herbs and a few small, stubborn apple trees. Some of the plants had grown from seeds that had been brought from Earth in frozen boxes. Others came from seeds that had been collected here, generation after generation, from plants that had lived and died on Mars. The colony called these the Mars-born lines.

The person who had started those lines was Dr Lindqvist, who was seventy-one and had arrived with the first ship. People called her the last gardener, because all the other gardeners from that ship had retired or gone home. These days she rarely came to the greenhouse. Kofi was the only trainee left.

### [p03]
Over the next week, the yellow spread. The tomato plants stopped producing flowers. The wheat, which had been tall and green only a month earlier, turned the colour of dry sand.

The greenhouse computer, however, insisted that everything was normal. Soil, water, temperature, light: every number on the screen was exactly where it should be.

The adults decided that it must be a disease. A team in white suits took samples from every row and had them tested in the colony's small laboratory. Nothing was found.

"It might be something we can't measure yet," said Ms Okafor, who was in charge of the laboratory. "Or it could be something in the seeds themselves."

Ten days after the first yellow leaf, the colony council held a meeting in the main hall. Kofi stood at the back with his mother, who worked on the water systems and who had told him twice not to say anything.

"We have a duty to everyone in this colony," said Councillor Hale, a tall, careful man who never raised his voice. "If the greenhouse fails, we will need another way to eat. Fortunately, we have the food printers. They are not exciting, but they work."

### [p04]
The food printers were kept in a white room next to the kitchen. They produced something that looked like bread and tasted of nothing at all, from powders that were delivered by the supply ship every two years. Everyone had eaten printed food in an emergency. Nobody had ever chosen it.

"I propose that we give the greenhouse twenty-one days," Councillor Hale went on. "If the plants haven't recovered by then, we will close the living garden, store the soil and switch completely to the printers. The power we save will be used for the new homes that have been planned for years."

There was a long silence. Then the council voted, and seven hands out of nine went up.

Kofi felt as if someone had opened a door to the outside and let all the air out of the room.

"They can't do that," he whispered.

"They can," his mother said quietly. "And honestly, Kofi, if I were in their position, I might do the same. People need to eat."

"People eat from the greenhouse."

"Not if it's dying."

He did not answer. He was thinking about the forty-one yellow leaves, and about the moment he had touched the first one. Something about it had been bothering him all week, and he still could not say what it was.

### [p05]
That night he could not sleep, so he went back to the greenhouse. The lights were low, and the only sound was the soft noise of the air machines. He walked slowly along the rows with a torch, looking at each plant as if it might tell him something.

At the end of the third row, he stopped.

In front of him were two bean plants, side by side, in the same soil, under the same roof, given the same water by the same pipe. One of them was clearly dying. Its leaves hung down, yellow and dry. The other was perfectly green and covered in small white flowers.

Kofi checked the labels on the pots. Then he checked them again.

The green plant had been grown from Earth seed, which had arrived on the last supply ship. The yellow plant came from a Mars-born line, from seeds that had been saved here for almost thirty years.

He checked every row in the greenhouse. By the time he had finished, he was out of breath, but he was sure.

Every sick plant in the greenhouse was Mars-born. Every plant from Earth seed was healthy.

Whatever was happening, it was not happening to all the plants. It was only happening to the ones that had grown up here, like him.

## [c2] Normal

### [p06]
The next morning, Kofi told Ms Okafor what he had found. She listened carefully and asked him whether he was absolutely certain.

"Every single one," he said.

She ran the tests again, this time comparing the two kinds of plants. The soil was the same. The water was the same. The light was the same, too. The computer measured exactly the right amount of light every day, as it had done ever since the new glass roof had been built, a little more than a year earlier.

Before that, the greenhouse had only had lamps, which were switched on at six in the morning and off at eight in the evening, every day, all year. The new roof let in the real sun, and the lamps were now only used to help it when the light was weak. The roof had saved a huge amount of power, and the plants had loved it. The harvests since then had been the best that the greenhouse had ever produced.

"So what do I tell the council?" asked Ms Okafor, more to herself than to Kofi. "That the plants miss a planet they've never seen?"

She said that his observation was interesting, but that it did not explain anything. If anything, she added, it suggested that the problem was in the seeds, which would be even worse.

"You can't repair a seed, Kofi," she said.

### [p07]
She promised that she would pass his notes on to the council. Kofi suspected that she would not.

He spent the afternoon in the greenhouse office, staring at the screen, which was still showing the same calm green numbers. Normal. Normal. Normal. He had started to hate that word.

At five o'clock, Yara Haddad put her head round the door. She was seventeen, a year ahead of him at school, and she was training to be an engineer.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thanks."

"I heard about the vote. My dad said they'll be taking the soil out by the end of the month." She sat down on the edge of the desk. "What are you going to do?"

Kofi told her about the two bean plants. Yara listened without interrupting, which was unusual for her.

"So it's something the Earth plants don't have," she said slowly, "or something the Mars plants have learned."

"Plants don't learn."

"Don't they? You're the gardener." She shrugged. "If I were you, I'd ask the person who made the Mars-born lines in the first place."

Kofi made a face. "Dr Lindqvist doesn't talk to anyone."

"Then she's been waiting for someone interesting," said Yara.

### [p08]
Dr Lindqvist lived in one of the oldest homes in the colony, close to the main air station. Kofi pressed the bell twice before the door opened.

She was small and thin, with short white hair and very clear blue eyes. Despite her age, she stood perfectly straight. She looked at him for a long moment, as if he were a plant that she had not decided whether to keep.

"You're the trainee," she said.

"Yes. Kofi Asante. I've been working in the greenhouse for two years. I'm sorry to bother you, but the plants..."

"I know about the plants. Everyone knows about the plants." She did not move from the doorway. "What do you want from me?"

He explained as quickly as he could: the yellow leaves, the tests, the Mars-born lines and the healthy Earth plants. When he had finished, something changed in her face, although it was gone almost before he had seen it.

"Interesting," she said. Then she shook her head. "But I'm retired, Mr Asante. I haven't been inside that greenhouse for six years. Ask your computer."

"The computer says everything's normal."

"Then perhaps it is," she said, and closed the door.

Kofi stood there for a full minute. He could have knocked again. Instead, he turned round and walked back to the greenhouse, more angry than disappointed.

### [p09]
As a trainee, Kofi had a key to the seed store, a cold, narrow room at the back of the greenhouse where thousands of seeds were kept in small metal boxes. Each box had a number and a date. Usually he only came here to fetch seeds. Tonight, however, he was looking for something else.

On the highest shelf, behind a row of empty boxes, there was a pile of old paper notebooks. He had noticed them on his first day and had been told not to touch them. They belonged to Dr Lindqvist, and they had been there since the first years of the colony, when paper was still being brought from Earth.

He took the first one down and opened it carefully.

The writing was small and neat. Every page recorded a planting: the date, the seed, the row, how many plants had grown and how many seeds had been saved from them. Next to some of the entries, there were short notes. Saved seeds from the strongest plants only. Kept the slow ones, too, just in case. The beans in row four seemed to wait before flowering. Why?

The notebooks were like a diary of the greenhouse, written by someone who had been watching the plants more closely than any computer ever could.

### [p10]
There was something else that surprised him. On every page, next to the colony date, Dr Lindqvist had written a second date. It took him a while to understand what it was. It was not an Earth date at all. It was the real date outside, on the Martian calendar, with its long, slow seasons that nobody inside the colony paid any attention to.

Why would anyone bother to write that down, Kofi wondered, when the plants had never seen the outside?

He turned to the very first notebook, to the first page of the first year. The handwriting there was younger and faster, and the notes were longer. She must have been about forty when she wrote them, Kofi thought, and very excited.

Near the bottom of the page, separated from everything else, there was a single line. It had been underlined twice.

They will learn the long year.

Kofi read it once, then again. But he had the strange feeling that it had been written for someone exactly like him, standing exactly where he was standing now, with yellow leaves outside the door and twenty days left.

He took a photo of the page. Then he put the notebooks back, exactly as he had found them, and switched off the light.

## [c3] The Long Year

### [p11]
The long year. Kofi thought about the words all through the next morning, while he was cleaning the water filters, and all through his maths class, while he was supposed to be solving equations.

The obvious meaning was the Martian year. Every child in the colony had learned about it at primary school. Mars takes longer than Earth to go round the sun, so its year is longer: 687 Earth days, almost twice as long. Its seasons are longer, too. But as far as Kofi was concerned, that had always been a fact for a test, not something that mattered. Inside Kestrel Colony, nobody lived by the Martian year. The calendar on every wall was an Earth calendar.

Nobody, that is, except Dr Lindqvist, who had been writing down the Martian date for thirty years.

At lunchtime, he went to the colony's small library and asked the computer for the outside calendar. When he finally saw it, he sat very still.

Outside, the long Martian summer was over. It had ended a few weeks ago. The real season now was autumn, and every day was a little shorter than the one before. In about four months, the long, dark Martian winter would begin.

### [p12]
That evening he found Yara in the engineering workshop, where she was taking apart an old pump.

"I need the data from the greenhouse roof," he said. "Not the amount of light. The length of the day. How many hours of sunlight the plants have been getting since the roof was built."

"Hello to you, too," said Yara. But she put down her tools.

The roof sensors had been recording everything, although nobody had ever looked at that particular number, because the computer was only interested in the total amount of light. Yara turned the data into a simple line on her screen.

For most of the year, the line went up. The days had been getting longer and longer through the Martian spring and summer. Then, about sixty days ago, it had stopped rising and slowly started to fall. The total light had stayed the same, because the lamps had been switching on to help on darker days. But the days themselves were getting shorter. A few minutes shorter every week.

Kofi put his finger on the point where the line turned.

"When did the first leaves go yellow?" asked Yara quietly.

He checked his notes, although he already knew the answer.

"A few weeks after that," he said.

### [p13]
They looked at the line together for a long time.

"I don't understand," said Yara at last. "Why would a plant care how long the day is?"

"Because on Earth, that's how plants know what season it is," Kofi said. He was remembering something now, a lesson from years ago, with pictures of a planet he had never visited. "Temperature can change from day to day. But the length of the day doesn't lie. When the days start getting shorter, a lot of plants on Earth get ready for winter. Trees drop their leaves. Some plants stop growing and go to sleep until spring."

"And the lamps..."

"For almost thirty years, the lamps gave every plant exactly the same day. Fourteen hours, all year. They never felt a season. Then the roof was built, and suddenly they could feel the real sun. This is the first autumn they have ever had."

Yara looked puzzled. "But then why aren't the Earth plants doing the same thing?"

"Because most of those seeds were made for greenhouses on Earth. They flower whatever the length of the day is." He took a breath. "But the Mars-born lines came from old garden plants. Dr Lindqvist has been saving their seeds for thirty years. If she always kept the ones that did best here..."

"Then they've been learning the long year," said Yara slowly. "Without anyone noticing."

### [p14]
This time, Dr Lindqvist opened the door after the first ring. She looked at Kofi, and then at Yara, and then at the screen that Kofi was holding up in front of him like a shield.

"The day length," he said, before she could speak. "The plants aren't dying. They're getting ready for winter. That's what you meant, isn't it? They will learn the long year."

For a moment, she looked as if he had hit her. Then her face closed again.

"Where did you read that?"

"In your notebook. I'm sorry. I know I shouldn't have. But we have nineteen days."

When she finally spoke, her voice was tired.

"Twenty years ago, I told the council almost exactly what you're telling me now. I said that the Mars-born lines were changing, and that one day they might follow the Martian seasons instead of ours. I asked them to give part of the greenhouse real sunlight, so that we could find out." She laughed, without any humour. "They told me to stop wasting their time with romantic theories. They said that a greenhouse was a factory, not a poem. And they made it clear that if I continued, I would no longer be welcome in it."

"But you were right," said Yara.

"Being right isn't always enough, young lady. I wish it were."

### [p15]
"Then help us prove it," said Kofi. "Come to the council with us."

Dr Lindqvist shook her head. "They'll see an old woman who has been telling the same story for twenty years. If I'd been more careful back then, if I'd collected better evidence, they might have listened. I didn't, and they didn't. I'm not going to stand in front of them and be laughed at again."

"Then what should we do?"

She looked at him for a long moment, the way she had looked at him the first time, as if she were deciding whether to keep him.

"If you want to change a council's mind," she said finally, "don't bring them a theory. Bring them a plant."

And she closed the door.

They walked back in silence. Through a small window, they could see the red ground outside and the sun going down early behind the hills, a little earlier than the day before.

"A plant," said Yara at last. "What kind of plant?"

Kofi had been thinking about the same question.

"One that knows winter is over," he said. "If the plants are really asleep, they should wake up when the days get longer again. So we give them a spring."

Yara stopped walking and stared at him. "You want to build a spring?"

"In nineteen days," said Kofi.

## [c4] The Test Chamber

### [p16]
They built it in the drying room behind the seed store, where nobody had worked for years. It was Yara's design. She took two old storage cupboards, lined them with silver sheets to reflect the light, and had a set of lamps fitted inside each one by a friend from the workshop who owed her a favour. Then she wrote a small program to control the lamps by the minute.

"This one is the summer cupboard," she explained. "Sixteen hours of light, every day. The other one is the winter cupboard: a whole Martian year in fast motion. A few short autumn days, then a few days of real winter, cold and almost dark, and then spring, with the days getting longer quickly."

"Will it be enough?" asked Kofi.

"I have no idea," said Yara cheerfully. "I'm an engineer, not a gardener. That's your problem."

Kofi chose the plants himself. For each cupboard, he took one of the Mars-born bean plants from row three, the same row where he had first noticed the yellow leaves. Both were in a bad state. Their lower leaves had already dropped, and the rest were turning pale. However, when he scratched the stems very gently with his fingernail, the skin underneath was still green and wet.

Not dead, he thought. Waiting.

### [p17]
For the first three days, nothing happened. Kofi checked the cupboards before school, after school and once more before bed. He measured the leaves and wrote everything down in a new notebook, using the same neat columns that he had seen in Dr Lindqvist's.

Then, on the next morning, he opened the summer cupboard and stopped.

The bean plant that had been living in a permanent summer had stopped going yellow. Two new leaves had appeared at the top of the stem, small and bright green, like flags.

Yara grinned when she saw the photos at lunch.

"That's it, then. Long days, green leaves. Short days, yellow leaves. We've proved it."

"We haven't," said Kofi. "Councillor Hale will just say that we've found a way to keep plants alive with more lamps. And more lamps mean more power, which is exactly what they want to save." He shook his head. "We need the winter cupboard. We need to show them that the plants come back by themselves after a winter. That they're not broken. They're just following a different clock."

Meanwhile, the greenhouse was getting worse. By the end of the week, more than half the Mars-born rows had lost most of their leaves. A team from the council had been sent to measure the soil, which would be taken out and stored if the vote went against the garden.

### [p18]
Ten days into the experiment, Kofi's mother found out.

She came into the drying room late one evening and stood in the doorway with her arms folded, looking at the cupboards, the cables and the two sad bean plants.

"Is this why you've been missing dinner?" she asked.

He explained everything: the notebooks, the long year, the line on Yara's screen and the two cupboards. He had expected her to be angry. Instead, she sat down on an old box and listened until he had finished.

"You should have told me," she said at last.

"You told me not to say anything at the meeting."

"At the meeting, yes. Not for the rest of your life." She sighed. "Kofi, I need you to understand something. If the council closes the greenhouse, it won't be because they hate plants. It'll be because they're frightened. Four hundred people depend on them. They can't take risks on a feeling."

"It isn't a feeling. It's data."

"Then show them the data." She stood up and looked into the winter cupboard, where the second bean plant was standing in near darkness. "When will this one be finished?"

"Spring starts tomorrow," said Kofi. "By the meeting, it'll have had a week of spring."

His mother looked at him with an expression he had not seen for a long time.

"Then I'll be sitting in the front row," she said.

### [p19]
The spring days in the winter cupboard began the next morning. Every few hours, Yara's program made the day a little longer, so that in less than a week the plant would live through a whole Martian spring.

At first, it made no difference at all. The bean plant in the winter cupboard looked worse than ever. It had lost all but three of its leaves during its short, cold winter, and those three hung down like old pieces of cloth.

"It might just be dead," said Yara two days later, very gently. "We can't be sure that it survived the winter."

"I know," said Kofi.

But he could not stop checking. He went before school, after school, during lunch and late at night. At night, he dreamed about the cupboard.

Twice he walked past Dr Lindqvist's home. Twice he nearly pressed the bell. Both times he walked on, remembering what she had said. Don't bring them a theory. Bring them a plant.

The night before the vote, the whole colony seemed quieter than usual. People spoke in low voices in the corridors. In the kitchen, someone had already started testing the food printers again, and the smell of warm, empty bread had reached the corridors.

### [p20]
Kofi went to the drying room one last time just before midnight. He did not expect to find anything. He only wanted to be with the plants for a while.

He opened the winter cupboard and switched on his torch.

The bean plant had not changed much. The three tired leaves were still hanging there. The stem was thin and brown at the bottom, as it had been for days. He was just about to close the door when the light from his torch caught something near the top of the stem.

He bent closer.

Between the last leaf and the stem, there was a small swelling, no bigger than a grain of rice. It was pale green and perfectly smooth. It can't have been there that morning.

Kofi held his breath. It could be anything. It might be a sign of disease, something new and worse that they had not seen before. It might be nothing at all.

But it did not look like nothing. It looked like something that was about to happen.

He took a photograph with shaking hands. Then he sat down on the floor of the drying room with his back against the wall and watched the cupboard until the lamps came on for the next spring morning.

By then, the swelling was slightly bigger.

## [c5] Buds

### [p21]
The council met in the main hall at ten o'clock. It was fuller than Kofi had ever seen it. His mother was in the front row, as she had promised. Yara sat next to her with a computer on her knees, and on the table in front of them, under a clear cover, stood the bean plant from the winter cupboard.

Councillor Hale opened the meeting. He reminded everyone that twenty-one days had been given to the greenhouse and that, unfortunately, the plants had not recovered. He said that the council would be voting on the closure of the living garden that morning. Then, as if he had almost forgotten, he asked whether anyone wished to speak first.

Kofi stood up. His legs felt like water.

He simply told them what had happened: the two bean plants, the notebooks, the Martian calendar, the line on Yara's screen and the two cupboards. Yara put the graph and the photographs up on the big screen behind him as he spoke.

"The plants aren't dying," he finished. "They're doing what plants on Earth have done for millions of years. They've noticed that the days are getting shorter, and they're preparing for winter. By the time the Martian winter begins, they'll have gone completely to sleep. And when spring comes, they'll wake up again."

### [p22]
There was a long silence. Then Councillor Hale spoke, politely but firmly.

"It's an interesting theory, Mr Asante. However, you've shown us one plant in a cupboard. How do we know that it isn't simply sick in a different way? What's that thing on the stem?"

Kofi opened his mouth, but before he could answer, a voice came from the back of the hall.

"It's a bud."

Everyone turned. Dr Lindqvist was standing in the doorway. She was wearing an old green greenhouse jacket that nobody had seen for six years, and she walked slowly down to the table. She bent over the plant for a long time.

"A flower bud," she said at last. "Unless I've forgotten everything I ever knew, it'll open in four or five days." She looked up at the council. "You have been watching a winter, Councillor, not a disease. If you had let me test this twenty years ago, you would have known." She turned to the hall. "These plants have been changing for thirty years, generation by generation. Every time we saved the seeds of the strongest plants, we chose the ones that suited this place a little better. They couldn't show us what they had learned, because we kept them under lamps. Now they have felt the real sun for the first time, and they've remembered the long year. This isn't the end of the greenhouse. It's the first Martian autumn."

### [p23]
Nobody spoke. Then, from the front row, Kofi's mother began to clap. Yara joined her, then the people around them, until the whole hall was clapping and the councillors were looking at each other uncomfortably.

The vote was taken an hour later. The living garden would not be closed. Instead, the Earth-seed rows would be moved under the lamps for the winter, so that the colony would not go hungry, whereas the Mars-born rows would be allowed to sleep under the real sky until spring. Both Dr Lindqvist and Kofi were asked to write a plan for the long year. Councillor Hale, who was nothing if not fair, voted with the others.

Afterwards, when the hall was empty, Dr Lindqvist sat down on the steps next to Kofi.

"I owe you an apology," she said. "I shouldn't have closed the door on you. Twice."

"You said 'bring them a plant'," Kofi said. "That helped."

"Hmm." She looked at the bean plant, which Yara was carefully packing for the walk back to the seed store. "Do you understand what that bud is, Kofi? It's not just a flower. In a few weeks, there'll be seeds inside it. And they'll be the first seeds in history that come from a plant which has lived through a whole Martian year. The first real Mars seeds."

### [p24]
Kofi did not answer immediately. He was thinking about the seed store, with its thousands of small metal boxes, and about one new box, with a new number and a date written on it in two calendars.

"People call you the last gardener," he said finally.

"I know they do." She smiled for the first time since he had met her. "But they're wrong, aren't they? I was never really a gardener on Mars. I was a gardener from Earth who happened to be working here. I've spent my whole life trying to grow Earth in a place that isn't Earth." She took his hand and turned it over, looking at the soil under his fingernails. "You're something different. You were born here, like those beans. You know this place in a way that I never will."

"I'll bring the notebooks to the greenhouse tomorrow," she said. "All of them. You'll need them. By the time you're my age, you'll have filled a hundred more."

Four days later, in the winter cupboard, the bud opened. It was a small white flower, not very different from the flowers on the Earth beans.

Then Kofi opened a new notebook, wrote two dates at the top of the first page, and underneath them, in his best handwriting, one short line.

The first Martian gardener. Year one.
