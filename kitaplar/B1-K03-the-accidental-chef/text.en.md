## [c1] The Interview

### [p01]
Priya couldn't cook, but her new job title said "Head Chef".

It was a mistake, of course. Priya was twenty-four, and she was a graphic designer. For two years she had worked in a small design studio in the city. Then the studio closed, and suddenly she had no job and a lot of bills.

She sent her CV to every company she could find. Nobody answered. So her cousin Meera, who worked in restaurants, gave her some advice.

"Your CV is boring," Meera said. "Use mine. It has a lovely design. Just change the words."

Priya changed the name, the address and the dates. She changed almost everything. But she didn't notice one small line at the bottom. It said: "Experienced chef. Can run a busy kitchen."

She sent forty CVs that night. Only one company phoned her back.

### [p02]
It was the Seagull Hotel, a small white hotel by the sea, three hours from the city.

"We were very excited when we saw your CV," said the woman on the phone. "Can you come tomorrow?"

Priya thought it was a design job. Hotels need menus, signs and websites, she told herself. Maybe they wanted a new logo.

The next day she sat in a sunny office in front of Mrs Ferris, the hotel manager. Mrs Ferris was a tall woman who talked very fast and never waited for answers.

"You've worked in busy kitchens, so you know the job," she said. "Long days, hot rooms, angry guests. Yes?"

Priya didn't really understand the question, so she nodded.

"Have you ever cooked for a hundred people?"

Priya nodded again. She had once made sandwiches for a birthday party. There had been at least twenty people there.

"Wonderful," said Mrs Ferris. "You can start on Monday."

### [p03]
On the train home, Priya read the email from the hotel three times. Her job title was in the first line: Head Chef.

She laughed. Then she stopped laughing.

"I'll just tell them," she said to her own face in the dark window. But then she thought about her bills. The first pay day was in one week. If she worked for one week, she could pay her rent. One week. How hard could it be?

That evening she phoned her mother.

"Amma, how do you make dal?"

Her mother was very surprised. "Why? You've never asked me that before. You haven't cooked anything since you left home."

"I've decided to learn," said Priya, which was true.

Her mother explained it slowly: the lentils, the onions, the spices and the long, patient cooking. Priya wrote it all down. It was the only recipe that she knew.

### [p04]
On Monday morning the kitchen was already hot. Pans were hanging on the walls, and something was boiling in a huge pot.

Chef Bruno was waiting for her. He was a big, cheerful man who had cooked at the Seagull Hotel for thirty years. He was leaving soon to open a small café with his wife, so the hotel needed a new head chef.

"Welcome!" he shouted. "Today I'll show you everything. Tomorrow, you'll show me everything!"

He walked quickly round the kitchen, pointing at fridges and ovens. Priya was taking notes when she heard a loud noise behind her.

Bruno was lying on the floor next to a piece of butter. He was holding his wrist.

"It's nothing," he said, although his face was white. "But I can't use this hand for a week. Maybe two."

He looked up at Priya and smiled bravely.

"You're in charge, Chef."

## [c2] One Dish, Five Names

### [p05]
At eleven o'clock, the first orders arrived.

Priya looked at the menu on the wall. It was full of dishes that she had never heard of. Lemon fish with green sauce. Chicken in cream with three kinds of mushroom. Something with a French name that she couldn't even say.

There were two young cooks in the kitchen, Tom and Ellie. They were both looking at her and waiting.

"Right," said Priya. "Today we're going to try something new."

She found pasta, tomatoes, onions and cheese. Pasta was safe. She used to eat a lot of pasta when she was a student.

"Chop the onions," she told Tom, because she had heard chefs say it on television. "Ellie, boil the water."

Twenty minutes later, a big pot of tomato pasta was ready. It was not bad. It was not good either. It was just pasta.

### [p06]
The problem was the orders. Table four wanted fish. Table seven wanted chicken. Table nine wanted the French thing.

So Priya gave them all pasta, and she gave the pasta five different names.

For table four it was "Pasta of the Sea", with a slice of lemon on top. For table seven it was "Farmhouse Pasta", with some chicken from the fridge. Then came "Garden Pasta", which had a few green leaves on it, and "Sunset Pasta", which had extra pepper. Last of all came "The Chef's Surprise", which was the biggest surprise of all.

The waiters carried the plates out. Priya waited by the door, and her heart was beating fast.

Nobody complained. One man even asked for the recipe.

"Well," said Ellie quietly, "that was interesting."

Behind them, somebody laughed softly. It was the man who washed the dishes.

### [p07]
His name was Gus. He was sixty-three, with grey hair, round glasses and very clean hands. He had started at the hotel only two weeks before, and he hardly ever spoke.

But he noticed everything.

While Priya was cleaning up, he tasted the last of the sauce with a small silver spoon. He always carried that spoon in his shirt pocket.

"Too much salt," he said. "And the onions were cooked too fast. Next time, fry them slowly until they're soft and golden. Ten minutes, not two."

Priya stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"I've washed a lot of plates," said Gus. "You learn things."

Then he took out a little black notebook and wrote something in it. He did that all day. He tasted everything that came back from the dining room, even the cold vegetables, and wrote a few words.

Ellie asked him once, "What are you writing?"

"My shopping list," said Gus.

### [p08]
That evening Priya was sitting on a box behind the kitchen, too tired to move, when Mrs Ferris ran out of the back door.

"Chef! Have you heard the news?"

Priya hadn't heard anything except her own feet, which were complaining loudly.

"The Silent Fork is here!" said Mrs Ferris. Her eyes were shining.

"The what?"

"The most famous food writer in the country! Nobody has ever seen the Silent Fork's face. Nobody knows if it's a man or a woman. The Silent Fork visits a restaurant secretly, eats, and then writes about it in the Sunday newspaper. A good review can make a hotel famous. A bad review…" She didn't finish the sentence.

"And how do you know they're here?"

"My friend at the newspaper told me. The Silent Fork is staying at our hotel this week. If the food is good, we'll be full for years!"

Priya looked at the dark sea and thought about pasta.

## [c3] Burnt but Proud

### [p09]
For the next three days, Priya tried. She really did.

On Tuesday she tried to cook fish. She put it in a very hot pan and went to answer the phone. When she came back, the kitchen was full of smoke and the fish looked like a piece of an old shoe.

On Wednesday she made a chocolate cake for the dessert menu. She was feeling quite proud until Tom tasted it. She had used salt instead of sugar. Tom drank three glasses of water and didn't speak to her for an hour.

But there were small successes too. Her soup was popular. Her rice was not bad. And a little girl at table two said that Priya's chips were "the best in the world", which made her surprisingly happy.

Meanwhile, everyone in the hotel was looking for the Silent Fork.

### [p10]
The waiters had a list of suspects.

There was a man in a grey hat who ordered everything on the menu and ate very slowly. There was a young couple who took photos of every plate. And there was a woman with a big bag who asked a lot of questions about the potatoes.

"It's the man in the hat," said Ellie. "Definitely. Look at him. He's thinking."

"He might be the Silent Fork," said Tom. "Or he might just be hungry."

Priya didn't want to think about it. Every time a plate went out, she imagined the Silent Fork tasting it and writing "terrible" in a little book.

Gus washed the plates, tasted the sauces and wrote in his notebook. Sometimes he said a quiet word to Priya. "Less butter." "Hotter pan." "Taste it before you serve it."

His advice was always right.

### [p11]
On Thursday night, after everyone else had gone home, Priya sat down next to the sink.

"Gus," she said, "I have to tell somebody. I'm not a chef. I'm a graphic designer. There was a mistake on my CV, and I've been too frightened to say anything."

Gus kept washing a pan. "I know," he said.

"You know?"

"Nobody who has worked in a kitchen holds a knife like that." He dried his hands. "Come here. If you're going to do this, you should do it properly."

For two hours he taught her. He showed her how to hold a knife so that she didn't cut her fingers. He showed her how to chop an onion quickly, how to stir a sauce so that it didn't stick, and how to fry fish without burning it.

"And if you do burn it," said Gus, "call it smoked."

Priya laughed for the first time in four days.

### [p12]
On Friday everything went better. The fish was golden, not black. The sauces were smooth. Tom and Ellie looked at her with something like respect.

On Saturday the hotel was full. Mrs Ferris walked round the dining room and smiled at everyone, especially the man in the grey hat.

At eight o'clock a waiter came into the kitchen with a strange order.

"Table twelve," he said. "An old lady. She's eating alone. She doesn't want anything from the menu."

"What does she want?"

The waiter looked at the paper in his hand. "She says, 'Please bring me the dish which the chef cooks best.'"

The kitchen went quiet. Tom looked at Ellie. Ellie looked at Priya. Even Gus stopped washing plates.

Priya looked through the little round window in the door. A small, grey-haired woman was sitting by the window and waiting patiently.

The dish which the chef cooks best. Priya only had one.

## [c4] Dal

### [p13]
"Do the fish," whispered Tom. "Your fish is good now."

"Do the chicken," said Ellie. "Everyone likes chicken."

Priya didn't answer. She was thinking about the week. She had served pasta with five names. She had pretended to know words that she didn't know. She had nodded at everything. She was tired of nodding.

"No," she said. "I'm going to make dal."

"What's dal?" asked Tom.

"Lentils, with onions and spices. My mother has made it every Sunday since I was a baby. It's the only thing that I really know how to cook."

"For the Silent Fork?" said Ellie. "She might be the Silent Fork! You can't give the Silent Fork a bowl of lentils!"

"If she wants the dish which I cook best," said Priya, "she'll get it."

In the corner, Gus smiled and wrote something in his notebook.

### [p14]
Priya found some lentils in a cupboard at the back. They had probably been there for years.

She washed them carefully, just as her mother used to do. She chopped the onions slowly and fried them until they were soft and golden. Ten minutes, not two. Then she added garlic and the spices, a little of this and a little of that. The kitchen began to smell like home.

While the dal was cooking, she remembered Sunday mornings in her mother's small kitchen. She used to sit on the floor and watch. Her mother never measured anything.

"You don't need a spoon," she used to say. "You need a nose."

Priya stirred, tasted and added a little salt. She tasted again. It wasn't exactly like her mother's dal. But it was close, and it was honest.

She put it in a white bowl with rice and some warm bread, and she carried it out herself.

### [p15]
The old lady looked at the bowl for a long moment. She didn't say anything. Then she picked up her spoon.

Priya went back to the kitchen and watched through the round window. Tom and Ellie watched over her shoulder. Even Gus came and stood behind them, drying a glass.

The lady ate slowly. She closed her eyes once. She ate everything, and then she used the bread to clean the bowl.

"Is that good or bad?" whispered Tom.

"In my family," said Priya, "that's very good."

A few minutes later, the waiter came back. "Table twelve," he said. "She'd like to meet the chef."

Priya's heart was beating so fast that she could hear it. She took off her apron, then put it back on. She walked across the dining room. The man in the grey hat was watching her. So was Mrs Ferris.

### [p16]
"Sit down, please," said the old lady. Her voice was soft, but her eyes were bright and sharp.

Priya sat. Her legs were shaking under the table, so sitting down seemed like a good idea.

"This was very good," said the lady. "The onions were perfect. Perhaps a little too much salt."

"Thank you," said Priya. "I'm sorry. I mean, thank you."

The lady smiled. Then she leaned forward and looked closely at Priya's face, as if she was looking for something.

"Where did you learn this?" she asked.

"From my mother. It's her recipe."

"Is it?" said the lady quietly. "How interesting."

She touched the side of the empty bowl with one finger.

"Because I know this recipe," she said. "I have known it for fifty years."

## [c5] The Review

### [p17]
Priya didn't know what to say. Was this the Silent Fork? Was it some kind of test?

"Your mother," said the lady. "Is her name Sunita? Did she use to live in a blue house on Mill Street, with a lemon tree in the garden?"

Priya's mouth fell open. "Yes. When she was young. How do you…?"

"I lived next door," said the lady. "My name is Kamala. Your mother was eighteen, and she couldn't cook at all. She used to burn everything, even bread. So I taught her my dal. Every Sunday, in my kitchen, for a whole year."

She laughed. "And now her daughter has cooked it for me. Fifty years later, in a hotel by the sea. Life is surprising, isn't it?"

Priya laughed too, and then, to her own surprise, she nearly cried.

"So you aren't the Silent Fork?"

"The what?" said Kamala.

### [p18]
On Monday, Chef Bruno came back with his arm in a white cloth. Priya was waiting for him with a cup of tea and the truth.

"I know," said Bruno. "Gus told me on Tuesday. He said you were terrible, but that you were learning fast." He looked round the clean kitchen. "He was right."

"Where is Gus?" asked Priya. She hadn't seen him all morning.

Nobody knew. His apron was hanging on the hook by the sink. His small silver spoon was gone.

Then Mrs Ferris ran in with yesterday's Sunday newspaper. Her face was pink.

"The Silent Fork!" she cried. "The review! It's about us!"

Everyone crowded round her. The title said, "Three Weeks at the Sink".

Mrs Ferris began to read it aloud, and her voice was shaking.

### [p19]
"For years I have eaten in the best restaurants in the country. This time I tried something different. For three weeks I worked as a dishwasher in a small hotel by the sea. I wanted to see a kitchen from the inside, from the sink, where nobody looks at you.

"I saw a lot. I saw pasta with five names. I saw a cake that was made with salt. I saw a young chef who had clearly never cooked a fish before.

"But on Saturday night, I saw something that I haven't seen for a long time. An old lady asked for the chef's best dish, and the young chef didn't try to be clever. She cooked the one thing that she really knew. It was a simple bowl of dal, made honestly and with care.

"It was the best dish I have eaten this year."

Nobody spoke. Ellie was the first to understand.

"The notebook," she said. "It wasn't a shopping list."

### [p20]
That afternoon, Priya sat down at the computer in Mrs Ferris's office and opened her CV. She found the line at the bottom about the experienced chef and the busy kitchen, and she deleted it.

Then she wrote a new line: "Can make one very good dal. Learning everything else."

She was still looking at it when the phone rang. It was a restaurant in the city that she had often read about. They had read the review too.

"We don't need a head chef," said the man on the phone. "But we've been looking for a beginner who wants to learn. Someone who will start at the bottom and work hard. Are you interested?"

Priya looked out at the sea. Somewhere out there, she thought, Gus was washing plates in another kitchen and writing in his little black notebook.

"Yes," she said. "I've never been more interested in anything."

Then she phoned her mother. "Amma," she said, "you'll never guess who I met."
