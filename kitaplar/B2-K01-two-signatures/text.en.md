## [c1] Varnish

### [p01]
Under the varnish of an ordinary harbour painting, Lucía found a second name.

It happened on a grey Tuesday in October, in a small restoration workshop on a narrow street behind the harbour of Marell. The workshop smelled of oil, wood and the chemicals that Lucía had learned to respect during her first weeks there. She was twenty-six, and she had been working as an intern for Mr Castell since the summer. Most days, she cleaned frames, mixed colours and listened to him complain about money.

The painting had arrived that morning in a van, together with three chairs, a box of plates and a broken mirror. It had been found in the attic of an old house on the hill, which was being emptied after its owner had died. Nobody in the family wanted it.

At first, Lucía could understand why. The picture showed the harbour of Marell more than a century ago: a few fishing boats, a stone wall, a row of houses and a grey sky. However, the whole thing was covered in a thick yellow-brown varnish, so dark that the sea looked like soup and the sky looked like cold tea.

In the bottom right corner, a signature could just be seen: a capital A, a dot and the name Serra.

### [p02]
"Andreu Serra," said Mr Castell, putting on his glasses. He was a short, round man of about sixty, who wore a bow tie every day and treated old paintings better than people. "Eighteen-nineties, probably. Not famous. But not completely unknown either."

"Is it worth anything?" Lucía asked.

"It might be, once it's clean." He looked at the painting the way other people look at a menu. "There's a collector who buys this kind of thing, a Ms Vidal. She phoned me last month and asked whether I ever found anything by Serra. I'll call her tonight."

"Shouldn't we clean it first?"

"Of course. That's your job." He gave her a small, satisfied smile. "I'd like it to be ready by Saturday. If she likes it, she'll be taking it home at the weekend, and we'll be paying the rent on time for once."

Lucía spent the rest of the afternoon testing the varnish. It was slow work. You never cleaned a painting all at once. You started in a corner, with a piece of cotton on a stick and the weakest liquid that would do the job, and you watched carefully to see what came off. If any colour appeared on the cotton, you stopped immediately.

The old varnish came away easily. Under it, the colours were surprisingly bright.

### [p03]
By six o'clock, Lucía had cleaned a small square of sky and part of the sea. The grey sky was not grey at all. It was a soft, warm blue, with the pink light of early morning along the edges of the clouds. The sea was green near the wall and deep blue further out. On the left, half hidden in shadow, a small red boat appeared where there had been nothing but brown.

She felt the excitement she always felt at moments like this, as if she were opening a window that had been closed for a hundred years.

Then she moved to the corner.

The signature was painted in dark brown, in quick, confident letters. The paint was covered in a fine net of tiny cracks, as old paint usually is. Lucía cleaned around it very gently. As the varnish disappeared, she noticed something else, just below the signature, half hidden in the shadow of the stone wall.

More letters. They were smaller, thinner and darker than the first ones, as if someone had written them with the tip of a very fine brush.

She bent closer and switched on the big lamp with the magnifying glass.

### [p04]
The letters were hard to read, because most of them were still under the dirt. But the first two were clear: an A and an i. After them came something that might have been an n, and then nothing but shadow.

"Ai," Lucía said quietly to herself. "Ai what?"

She looked at the two names for a long time. There was something strange about them. The cracks that covered the signature were deep and wide, like the lines on a very old hand. The second name had cracks too, but far fewer, and they were much finer. And yet the capital A at the start of each name had exactly the same unusual shape, with a little loop at the top, like a ribbon.

Two names on one painting, she thought. Perhaps Andreu Serra had bought a picture from someone else and put his own name on it. It had happened before. Painters in the nineteenth century were not always honest.

Mr Castell had already gone home. Lucía took a photograph of the corner and sent it to him.

His answer came back ten minutes later. "Lovely. Don't touch the signature. Just finish the sky."

She switched off the lamp. But for a long time, in the dark workshop, the two small letters seemed to stay in front of her eyes: A and i.

## [c2] The Archive

### [p05]
Lucía didn't sleep well that night. In the morning, before work, she went to the city archive.

The archive was kept in an old customs building near the harbour, which had been turned into offices in the 1970s. It was quiet and cold inside, and it smelled of paper. The archivist, Ms Roig, was a thin woman with silver hair who spoke in a whisper even when nobody else was in the room.

"Serra," she repeated. "Andreu Serra. A painter, you said?"

"He showed his work in the city in the 1890s," Lucía said. "That's all I know."

Ms Roig disappeared between the shelves and came back twenty minutes later with three boxes and an expression of mild surprise.

"There isn't much," she said. "Which is strange, for a man who sold paintings."

The boxes contained catalogues from the city's art exhibition, which was held every year. Lucía went through them page by page. Andreu Serra was listed in 1889, 1891, 1892, 1894 and 1896. In each catalogue, the same short line appeared next to his name: Harbour views. Address: 14 Rope Street. In 1894, one of his paintings had been given a prize, and a newspaper had described his work as "strong, honest and surprisingly modern".

After 1896, there was nothing.

### [p06]
"What happened to him?" Lucía asked.

"That's what I've been trying to find out since you sat down," Ms Roig whispered. She had brought another box, full of birth and death records. "There's no Andreu Serra born in this city between 1850 and 1875. No marriage. No death. Of course, he might have come from somewhere else. People did."

"And the address? Rope Street?"

Ms Roig opened a heavy book of population records from 1890 and moved her finger down a page of tiny brown handwriting.

"Number fourteen. Here." She frowned. "That's odd. Only one person lived there. A woman. Aina Serra, aged twenty-one, not married. Job..." She bent closer. "Teacher of drawing."

Lucía felt the back of her neck go cold.

"Aina," she said. "A, i, n, a."

"His sister, perhaps," said Ms Roig. "Or his wife, if the record is wrong. But there's no sign of him in the house at all. If he had lived there, he would have been counted."

Lucía wrote everything down in her notebook. Her hand was shaking slightly. Andreu Serra had painted harbour views, won a prize and then disappeared. And at the same address there had lived a woman whose name began with the same two letters as the second name on the painting.

### [p07]
"Could I have copies of these pages?" Lucía asked.

"Of course. It will take a day or two." Ms Roig wrote some numbers on a card. "We have the old books scanned by an outside company. It isn't fast, but it protects the paper."

Lucía arrived at the workshop forty minutes late. Mr Castell was standing next to the painting with his arms folded.

"You've been to the archive," he said. It wasn't a question.

She told him everything: the exhibitions, the prize, the missing records, the woman at number fourteen. While she was talking, he looked at the painting, not at her.

"It's an interesting story," he said at last. "But stories don't sell paintings, Lucía. Names do. Ms Vidal asked me for an Andreu Serra. She didn't ask me for a mystery."

"But what if he didn't paint it? What if she did?"

"Then you'll need proof, and proof is expensive." He tapped the frame. "A proper analysis of the paint would cost more than the painting is worth. And who would pay for it? Not me."

He told her to finish the sky by Thursday and to leave the signatures alone. Then he went into his office and closed the door.

Lucía picked up a fresh piece of cotton. She tried to concentrate on the clouds, but her eyes kept going back to the corner.

### [p08]
For the rest of the day, Lucía worked on the sky and the sea, exactly as she had been told. Slowly, the harbour came back to life. Ropes appeared on the boats. A cat appeared on the wall. Along the edge of the water, the row of houses turned from brown to yellow, pink and white, and in the house at the end of the row, a single window was open, with a white curtain moving in the wind.

The painter must have been standing on the other side of the harbour, Lucía realised, somewhere near the beginning of Rope Street. Perhaps even at a window of number fourteen.

At half past five, her phone rang. It was Ms Roig.

"Your copies will be ready tomorrow," she whispered. "I'm sorry it's taking so long. I would have had them done today if the machine hadn't broken."

"That's fine. Thank you."

"It's funny, though," Ms Roig went on. "Usually nobody asks about those books for years. Then suddenly, two people in one week."

Lucía stopped with the cotton in the air. "Two people?"

"Oh, yes. I'm sorry, I thought I'd told you. Someone else asked about Serra last week."

## [c3] Ultraviolet

### [p09]
"Who was it?" Lucía asked.

"She didn't leave her name," said Ms Roig. "A woman of about fifty, very well dressed, in a long grey coat. She wanted to see the same books. She was especially interested in the address. When she found the record for number fourteen, she sat looking at it for a long time. Then she thanked me and left."

After the call, Lucía sat on the high stool next to the painting and tried to think clearly.

Someone else knew about Aina Serra. Someone who had been asking about Serra just before a Serra painting appeared in an old attic. And Mr Castell had said that Ms Vidal had phoned him last month, asking whether he ever found anything by Serra.

It might be a coincidence. But Lucía had worked in restoration long enough to know how the art market worked. A pleasant harbour view by a forgotten man was worth a little money. However, a lost painting by a woman artist from the 1890s, a woman who had been forced to hide behind a man's name, would be a very different story. Museums would want it. Newspapers would write about it. Its price would go up ten times.

Unless, of course, someone bought it quietly first, as an ordinary Andreu Serra.

### [p10]
That night, Lucía went back to the workshop.

She had her own key, and she told herself that she was doing nothing wrong. She only wanted to look. In the cupboard under the sink there was an ultraviolet lamp, which Mr Castell used to check for old repairs. Under ultraviolet light, different materials shine in different ways. Old varnish shines green. New paint, added during a repair, stays dark. And sometimes, if you are lucky, the light shows you shapes that the eye can't see.

She turned off all the lights, closed the blinds and switched on the lamp.

The painting changed in front of her. The cleaned sky turned a calm, dark purple. The parts that had not been cleaned yet looked green, like the surface of a strange sea. And on the harbour wall, just above the red boat, something appeared that had not been there in daylight.

A shape. Then a second shape next to it, tall and thin.

Lucía held her breath and moved the lamp closer.

It was a figure, painted and then painted over, so long ago that the top layer had become almost transparent under the lamp. A woman, standing on the harbour wall, in a long skirt and a wide hat. In front of her stood an easel. In her raised hand she held a brush.

### [p11]
For a long time, Lucía did not move.

A woman painting the harbour, inside a painting of the harbour. She thought of the population record: Aina Serra, teacher of drawing. She thought of the second name in the corner, with its fine young cracks. Someone had painted this woman and then covered her with a pile of fishing nets. Why? Because a picture of a woman with a brush might make people ask questions?

She took a dozen photographs with her phone. Then she switched off the lamp and sat in the dark for a while.

If she hadn't come back tonight, she thought, she would never have seen it. If Mr Castell had been here, he would have sent her home. And if the painting had been sold on Saturday, the woman on the wall would have disappeared into a private house, perhaps forever.

The next morning, Lucía arrived early, but Mr Castell was already there. He was not alone.

A woman was standing in front of the painting with her back to the door. She was tall and elegant, with short grey hair and a long grey coat.

"Ah, Lucía," said Mr Castell, in the voice he used for customers. "This is Ms Vidal."

### [p12]
Ms Vidal turned round. She was about fifty, with a calm, intelligent face and pale grey-green eyes that seemed to notice everything. She shook Lucía's hand.

"You must be the one who's been cleaning it," she said. "It's beautiful work. The sky especially."

"Thank you."

Ms Vidal looked back at the painting. "Have you cleaned the little red boat yet? On the left?"

Lucía stared at her. The red boat had appeared only the day before yesterday. Until then, it had been invisible under the brown varnish. The photographs that Mr Castell had sent Ms Vidal had been taken before the cleaning started. She can't have seen the boat in them.

"Yes," Lucía said slowly. "How did you know there was a red boat?"

For a second, something passed across Ms Vidal's face, as if a door had opened and closed. Then she smiled. "A lucky guess. Painters of that time loved a red boat."

Lucía did not believe it was a guess.

Ms Vidal turned to Mr Castell. "I'd like to buy it on Saturday, as we agreed. I have only one condition."

"Of course," said Mr Castell.

"The label, the certificate, the receipt, everything. It must say Andreu Serra." She looked at Lucía for a moment. "Only Andreu Serra. Nothing else."

## [c4] Letters

### [p13]
After Ms Vidal had left, Lucía didn't show Mr Castell the photographs. She wasn't sure why. Perhaps it was because she knew what he would say. Perhaps it was because of the way he had agreed so quickly, without even looking at her.

At lunchtime, she phoned the company that had emptied the house on the hill. A tired man told her that the furniture had been sold, the rubbish had been thrown away and the books and papers had been bought by a second-hand bookseller in the old town, a Mr Pons.

"Were there any letters?" she asked.

"Boxes of them," he said. "Who reads old letters these days?"

Mr Pons's shop was in a narrow street behind the market. It was so full of books that the shelves seemed to be holding up the ceiling. Mr Pons himself was a large, cheerful man with a white beard.

"The house on the hill? Yes, yes. I bought everything." He waved a hand towards the back of the shop. "I haven't had time to look through it yet. If you find anything interesting, I'll be selling it to you at a very special price." He laughed loudly at his own joke.

### [p14]
It took Lucía an hour to find them. At the bottom of the third box there was a bundle of letters tied with a faded blue ribbon. The paper was thin and brown at the edges. They had all been written by the same hand, to the same person, a woman called Teresa, who seemed to live in another city. And they were all signed with a single letter: A.

Lucía sat down on a box and began to read. The letters were full of ordinary life: the price of bread, a cold winter, the children she taught to draw. But again and again, they returned to painting.

"I have been working on the harbour since March," said one, "and I think it is the best thing I have ever done. I painted myself into it, standing on the wall with my brush, as a joke. Of course, I will have to paint myself out again before the exhibition. A woman with a brush would make people ask questions."

Lucía read the sentence twice. Then she read it a third time. Her hands felt cold, despite the heat in the little shop.

### [p15]
Another letter, from 1894, made her laugh out loud in the dusty shop.

"You will be pleased to hear that Andreu has won a prize," A. had written. "The newspaper says that he is strong, honest and surprisingly modern. It also says that he is a shy young man who never comes to exhibitions. That part is true. He has never been seen at an exhibition in his life, and I am afraid he never will be."

Lucía turned the pages carefully. No letter explained who Andreu was. It seemed that Teresa already knew. But there were other clues. The frames, A. wrote, were made by an old man on the corner, who asked no questions. The paintings were taken to the exhibition by a friend's brother. The money was sent to the address on Rope Street.

Then she found the last letter in the bundle. It was dated 1896, and the handwriting was faster and angrier than in the others.

"I went to the gallery myself today," it said. "I told the owner that I had painted every one of Andreu's pictures, and that from now on I wanted to sell them under my own name. He laughed. He said that he had always suspected it, but that it made no difference. He told me that if people knew the truth, nobody would want them."

### [p16]
Lucía read on.

"So I have decided that Andreu will stop painting," the letter continued. "I will not tell lies any longer, and they will not buy the truth. Perhaps one day things will be different. Perhaps one day someone will clean the dirt off these pictures and see who was really holding the brush. I hope so. I am tired, Teresa, and I am only twenty-seven."

Lucía sat very still. Outside, people were walking past with shopping bags, talking about ordinary things.

She paid Mr Pons for the whole bundle, which cost her most of the money she had left for the month. He wrapped the letters in brown paper and gave them to her with surprising care, as if he understood that they were not just paper.

"Good luck with whatever it is," he said.

On the way home, she stopped at the harbour and stood on the old stone wall, more or less where the woman with the brush had once stood. The houses of Rope Street were still there across the water. Some had been painted in new colours, and one had been turned into a hotel, but the harbour itself had hardly changed.

She took out the last letter and read again the line that had stopped her heart in the shop. It was written in the middle of the page and underlined twice.

"They will only buy a man's painting."

## [c5] Evidence

### [p17]
The next morning, Lucía put everything on Mr Castell's desk: the copies from the archive, which she had collected on the way, the ultraviolet photographs and the bundle of letters.

He read in silence for almost half an hour.

"This is very good work," he said at last, taking off his glasses. "But it isn't proof."

"Mr Castell, she painted herself into the picture, and the letter says so. Aina Serra was Andreu Serra. There was never a man at all."

"Perhaps. But think about it the way a museum would." He held up the photograph of the corner. "Who wrote this second name? And when? If Aina painted the picture in 1894 and signed it as Andreu, why would she write her own name underneath? Someone could have added it last year, to make the painting more interesting. People do that, Lucía. I've seen it done."

She hadn't thought of that, and she wished she had.

"Then we test it," she said. "If the second name is new, the paint will tell us."

"And who will pay for the test?"

"My friend Tomás is doing a doctorate in chemistry at the university. He's offered to do it for nothing. Well, for dinner."

Mr Castell looked at her for a long moment. Then, for the first time that week, he almost smiled.

### [p18]
That afternoon, under the microscope, Lucía took two samples smaller than a grain of sand: one from the edge of the first signature, and one from the edge of the second name. Mr Castell watched every movement of her hands, but he didn't say a word.

Tomás worked in a laboratory full of noisy machines on the top floor of the science building. He was tall and untidy, and he had been living on coffee for most of his doctorate.

"You understand that I can't tell you the year," he warned her. "Paint doesn't come with a date on it. But I can tell you what's in it, and that can sometimes be just as useful."

The results arrived on Thursday afternoon. Tomás phoned her instead of writing.

"Your first signature is typical of the late nineteenth century," he said. "Lead white, natural earth colours, nothing unusual. The second name is different. It contains a white made from titanium."

"Which means?"

"Which means it can't have been painted before about 1920. That white wasn't widely sold to painters before then. But it isn't modern either. There's old dirt in the cracks, and the varnish on top of it is decades old. I'd say the second name was added at least eighty or ninety years ago."

Lucía sat down slowly. Around 1930, Aina Serra would have been about sixty.

### [p19]
She carried the news back to the workshop as if it were something that might break.

"So nobody added it last year," she told Mr Castell. "Someone added it around 1930, and then it was varnished over. Who else would write her name on it, so carefully, in the same hand? Look at the A. It's the same A, with the same little loop. She must have done it herself, when she was old."

Mr Castell sat down heavily in his chair. For a while, he didn't say anything.

"Do you know how much Ms Vidal has offered me?" he said finally. "Three times what an Andreu Serra is worth. Three times, Lucía. I've been wondering all week why anyone would pay that much for a forgotten painter."

Lucía opened her mouth and closed it again. It didn't make sense. If Ms Vidal had wanted to cheat them, why would she pay so much? And yet, she had known about the boat. And she had been at the archive. And she wanted only one name on the label.

"We have to tell her," Lucía said.

"We will. She'll be told everything on Saturday, and she can decide what she wants to do." He put his glasses back on. "By then, your friend will have written his report, and you will have cleaned that second name properly. I'd like to see it before anyone else does."

### [p20]
On Friday evening, with Mr Castell's permission, Lucía cleaned the corner.

She worked slowly, while Mr Castell stood behind her and pretended to read a newspaper. The dark varnish came away, and under it, letter by letter, the second name appeared. It was written in the same brown as the harbour wall, in a hand that was slightly less steady than the first, but which was unmistakably the same.

Aina Serra.

And after it, so small that Lucía needed the magnifying glass, a date: 1931.

"There you are," Lucía whispered. She didn't know whether she was speaking to the painting or to the woman who had painted it.

Mr Castell put down his newspaper and came closer. He looked at the two names, one above the other, for a long time. Then he took off his glasses and cleaned them, although they were not dirty.

"Thirty-seven years," he said quietly. "She waited thirty-seven years to put her own name on her own picture."

At that moment, the bell above the workshop door rang.

They both turned round. It was almost eight o'clock. The sign on the door said Closed.

Ms Vidal was standing in the doorway in her long grey coat. She was a day early.

## [c6] The Same Hand

### [p21]
"I'm sorry," said Ms Vidal. "I know it's late, and I know we said Saturday. But I couldn't wait any longer."

Then she saw the painting, and the clean corner under the lamp, and she stopped.

For a moment, nobody spoke. Ms Vidal walked slowly towards the easel, as if she were afraid the name might disappear if she moved too fast. She looked at it for a long time. Then she put a hand over her mouth, and Lucía saw that her eyes were full of tears.

Lucía had been practising a speech in her head all day. Now it came out faster and less politely than she had planned.

"You knew," she said. "You were at the archive last week, asking about Rope Street. You knew about the red boat before anyone could see it. You asked us to call it an Andreu Serra because you knew what it really was, and you wanted to buy it before anyone else found out."

Mr Castell said, "Lucía," in a warning voice, but Ms Vidal raised a hand.

"No. She's right to ask." She wiped her eyes and turned round. "Although she's wrong about the reason. Is that really what you think? That I wanted to hide her?"

### [p22]
Ms Vidal took an old black-and-white photograph out of her bag and held it under the lamp.

It showed an old woman sitting in a high-backed chair in a sunny room. On the wall behind her hung a painting. Lucía recognised it immediately: the harbour, the wall, and the little dark boat on the left that she knew was red. The old woman was holding a very fine brush in her hand, and she was smiling at the camera like someone who had just done something slightly naughty.

"Aina Serra," said Ms Vidal. "My great-great-grandmother. This was taken in 1931, in the house on the hill. Her son had it taken, I think, for her birthday."

Lucía stared at the photograph, and then at the date in the corner of the painting.

"My grandmother used to tell me that Aina had been a famous painter," Ms Vidal went on, "but under a man's name. Nobody in the family believed it. They thought it was just one of her stories. Most of her paintings had been sold long ago. This one stayed in the family until my grandmother died, and the house was sold with everything in it. I was fifteen. I have been looking for it, and for the others, for twelve years."

"The red boat," said Lucía slowly. "You saw it in this photograph."

"Every day, when I was a child. It hung in my grandmother's kitchen."

### [p23]
"Then why the label?" asked Mr Castell. "Why only Andreu?"

Ms Vidal smiled sadly. "Because I had no proof. If I had told you who I was, and what I hoped the painting was, you would have doubled the price, and quite right too. And if I had been wrong, I would have made my family's favourite story look foolish. I wanted to buy it quietly, as a forgotten man's harbour view, and then have it examined properly. I didn't want anyone to pay attention to it until I was sure. When I heard that the old house was being emptied, I phoned every restorer in Marell."

"And the second name?" Lucía asked. "Who varnished over it?"

"Her son, probably. After she died, he had all the paintings in the house cleaned and varnished, to protect them. He can't have looked very closely at the corner." She shook her head. "Or perhaps he didn't understand what it meant."

Lucía went to the desk and came back with the bundle of letters. She put it into Ms Vidal's hands.

"These were in the house too," she said. "She wrote to a friend called Teresa. She explains everything. Andreu, the prize, the gallery. Everything."

Ms Vidal untied the faded ribbon with shaking fingers and began to read. "If only my grandmother could see this," she whispered. After that, nobody spoke. Outside, the boats knocked gently against the harbour wall, exactly as they must have done in 1894.

### [p24]
In the end, Ms Vidal paid the price that she had offered, which meant that the workshop paid its rent on time for the first time in a year. But she did not take the painting home.

Instead, in the spring, it was given to the city museum, together with the letters, Tomás's report and a short history of the cleaning written by Lucía Ferrer, intern. The museum had the frame repaired and the woman on the harbour wall photographed under ultraviolet light, so that visitors could see her too.

On the day the painting went on show, Lucía stood in front of it for a long time. The little label on the wall said: Aina Serra, The Harbour of Marell, 1894. Shown in her lifetime under the name Andreu Serra.

If Lucía had followed Mr Castell's instructions, she sometimes thought, that label would still say Andreu. And if Aina hadn't picked up her brush one last time in 1931, nobody would ever have known.

Ms Vidal has been searching for the other paintings ever since. By next summer, she says, she will have visited every second-hand shop on the coast. Lucía is helping her.

But the painting she visits most is still this one: an ordinary harbour, a red boat, and in the corner, two signatures, thirty-seven years apart, written by the same hand.
