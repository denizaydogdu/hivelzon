## [c1] Nine Hundred

### [p01]
In the city of Halvern, every citizen was allowed nine hundred spoken words a day, and Petra had been saving hers for eleven years.

Nine hundred is more than it sounds. It is also less. A greeting costs two. An apology, done properly, costs eleven. A quarrel can cost a week.

The words arrived every morning at six, with the first trams. You felt them before you saw them: a faint warmth at the throat, where the meter sat. Every adult in Halvern wore one. It was a slim collar of grey metal, closed with a small catch at the back of the neck, and along the front ran a strip of amber light. A full day was a full strip. Each word you spoke took a little of the light away, so that by evening most people's throats glowed no brighter than the last coal in a grate.

There were no numbers on the meter. Numbers, it was felt, would only encourage people to count, and counting led to anxiety, and anxiety led to talking. Halvern had had quite enough of talking. Few people could now say exactly when it had started, only that it had been sensible, and that sensible things should be left alone.

So the city was quiet. Not silent: trams hummed, gulls complained, doors were closed with feeling. But the voices were gone. On the morning trams, people spoke with their hands, in the quick, tidy gestures of the Sign, which every child learned before it learned to read. In the cafés, one ordered by pointing. In the parks, couples sat side by side and held hands, which said a great deal and cost nothing.

Petra Lind spoke perhaps three hundred words on an ordinary day, and most of those at work. The rest she kept.

What she was keeping them for, she had told nobody. That, too, would have cost words.

### [p02]
She was a word-broker, and a good one. Her office was a narrow room on the third floor of the Exchange on Copper Street, with a window, a desk, two chairs and a framed licence that nobody ever read. People came to her when they had words they did not need, or needed words they did not have, and she arranged matters between them for a small percentage.

At nine o'clock on the first Monday of the month, her client was a young man called Osric Pell. He sat on the edge of the chair as if it might bite him, and pushed a folded sheet of paper across the desk.

Petra unfolded it. It was a wedding vow. It was very long.

She raised an eyebrow, which was a question in the Sign and also, conveniently, in ordinary life.

"Hilde's is longer," said Osric. "One thousand four hundred."

Petra counted his. One thousand two hundred and six, not including the names. She signed to him that the wedding was in five weeks, and that a man who saved properly could put aside a great deal in five weeks.

"I talk," Osric said miserably. "At work. I can't help it."

What he needed, it turned out, was a loan of four thousand words, repayable over a year at the usual rate. Not only was this the most common request Petra received; it was also, by some distance, the most sentimental. Brides and grooms of Halvern spent months in near silence so that, for three minutes in front of their families, they might say everything at once. Petra had always found this touching and slightly absurd, in roughly equal measure.

She wrote out the terms. He read them, nodded, and pressed his thumb to the contract.

"Thank you," he said, and then looked appalled, because that was two more.

Petra smiled at him. It was the kind of smile that cost nothing, and she gave it freely.

### [p03]
When Osric had gone, she took her notebook out of the drawer.

It was the eleventh notebook. The first ten were at home, in a box under the bed, held together with a rubber band that had long since lost interest in its work. Each contained drafts of the same speech, in pencil, with the words counted in the margin.

The speech was for her father.

Matthias Lind lived on the other side of the river, in a narrow house in Eastmere, and he had not spoken to his daughter in eleven years. It was said in Eastmere that he had not spoken to anyone in that time. The neighbours called him the Quiet Engineer, which in a city as quiet as Halvern was something of an achievement. He had once worked for the Word Service, in a department called Calibration, though what he had calibrated, and why he had left so suddenly when Petra was four, he had never explained. He had never explained much.

Even before the silence, he had been a man of few words. Petra remembered a childhood of nods, of pointed fingers, of soup placed in front of her without comment. He was not unkind. He simply seemed to believe that most things were better left unsaid, and he left them unsaid with great consistency.

Then, on her eighteenth birthday, she had told him that she was going to be a broker at the Exchange.

He had looked at her for a long moment. Then he had spent six words.

"You'll be selling something that isn't there."

Petra had taken it, at eighteen, to mean that he thought her work would be worthless. She had packed a bag that evening without a word, which at the time had seemed a magnificent gesture, and she had not been back.

She had, however, started saving. If he could be silent, so could she. And one day, when she had enough, she would go back and say everything at once.

### [p04]
The speech had grown over the years. In the first notebook it ran to four pages and was mostly about the birthday. By the fifth, it had acquired her mother's departure to the coast, a school concert he had not attended, and a long passage about soup. By the ninth, she had cut the soup and added a section explaining, with some dignity, that she had done perfectly well without him. The current version would take, by her estimate, a little over six hours to deliver.

Six hours of speaking was, at a careful pace, about forty thousand words. Petra had a great deal more than that saved. She had known for years that she had enough. It was simply that a speech like this needed a cushion, and then the cushion needed a cushion, and somehow there was always a reason to wait another year.

She would rather not have examined this too closely, and so she didn't.

There was one more thing, which she noticed now and then and set aside. It was the matter of the Chime.

Everybody knew about the Chime. If you spent more than your balance, your meter sang: a high, clear, unbearable note that could be heard across a street, and that did not stop until you did. After that, every word you spoke went down as talk-debt, to be repaid out of the following days, with a fine on top. The Chime was the reason parents hushed their children in the street, and the reason brokers existed at all.

Petra had never heard it. In eleven years at the Exchange, she had never once seen a client go below zero. Her mother had never heard it either. It is said that Petra's grandmother heard it once, in a queue at the post office, but then it is said that Petra's grandmother saw a great many things.

### [p05]
At half past eleven, a clerk from the Word Service came up the stairs of the Exchange with a roll of paper under his arm, pinned a notice to the board in the hall, and left without a word. That was how the Service did things. It did not announce; it pinned.

By noon, there was a crowd in front of the board.

Petra had to wait some time before she could read it. It was printed in the tall grey type that the Service used for matters of importance, and it said:

NOTICE OF CONSOLIDATION. In the interests of administrative simplicity and the reduction of record-keeping costs, the Service has approved the cancellation of all accumulated allowances. The expiry of such allowances will take effect at midnight on the last day of next month. Citizens are reminded that failure to make use of accumulated allowances before that date will result in their permanent removal. The Service thanks the public for its continued cooperation.

Brokers do not shout; it would be unprofessional, and expensive. But the hall of the Exchange produced, at that moment, a sound that came very close to it. Hands moved in the Sign so fast that they blurred. Somebody sat down on the stairs. Somebody else laughed, briefly, and then looked as though he regretted it. A great deal of money was about to disappear, and brokers are never more eloquent than when that happens.

Petra did not move. She was doing arithmetic.

The last day of next month was a Tuesday, seven weeks away. Every word she had saved would be gone at midnight. Eleven years of silence would be reduced to nothing at all.

Such was the shock that she spoke aloud, there in the hall, without meaning to.

"Well," said Petra. "That settles it."

Four words. Then she went back upstairs, opened the eleventh notebook, and wrote at the top of a clean page, in capital letters: GO.

## [c2] The Balance

### [p06]
Seven weeks is a long time to prepare for anything, which is precisely what makes it dangerous.

In the first week, Petra reread all eleven notebooks, from the beginning, with a pencil. In the second, she rewrote the opening four times. The first version began with the birthday. The second began with the soup, which she had restored, on reflection, because it explained so much. The third began with a list of everything he had not said at her school concert, and was very long, and very fair. The fourth began with the word "Father", followed by a pause of what she estimated would be four seconds, and she was rather proud of it.

In the third week, she bought a scarf. It was red, of soft wool, and she had no particular reason to buy it except that the scarves in the shop window had all been grey, and she had found herself standing in front of the red one for some time. Halvern dressed in the colours of its pavements. A red scarf in Halvern was not quite a shout, but it was certainly a clearing of the throat.

In the fourth and fifth weeks, she did nothing at all, with great concentration.

It is easy to judge this from outside. Petra judged it herself, at length, usually at about two in the morning. Never had she been so busy and achieved so little. She told herself that the speech was not ready, which was true, and that she was not ready, which was also true, and that there was plenty of time, which was not.

If she had gone five years ago, she would not now be sitting on the edge of her bed in the dark, holding a notebook she could no longer see, counting the words of a speech she knew by heart. That was the plain fact of it, and she looked at it plainly, and then she went back to bed.

### [p07]
At the start of the sixth week, she planned the route.

Eastmere lay on the far side of the river, an hour's walk from her flat, or twenty minutes by tram. She would walk. Walking would give her time to rehearse, and the trams were full of people whose hands could not help commenting on a stranger's red scarf. She would cross the Iron Bridge, turn left along the embankment, and go up the hill past the old water tower, and his street would be the third on the right. She had not been there for eleven years, but she could have walked it with her eyes closed. She had, in fact, walked it several times in her sleep.

Her neighbour, Mrs Varga, met her on the stairs that week and signed a question about the scarf. Petra signed back that it was for an occasion. Mrs Varga signed that it was high time Petra had an occasion, and went on down the stairs with an expression of deep satisfaction, as if she had arranged the whole thing herself.

There remained the question of the balance.

Petra knew her balance. Of course she did; she was a broker. She knew it the way a sailor knows the tide, from long attention, and she recorded it every night in a ledger in her desk drawer: the words received at six, minus the words spent, carried forward. Her own arithmetic said a little over two million four hundred thousand. She had no reason to doubt it.

She had, however, never actually checked it with the Service.

To check one's balance, one went to a Service kiosk and spoke one's full name into a grille, and the kiosk printed a slip. Three words, in Petra's case. Over eleven years, it had never seemed worth three words to be told something she already knew.

### [p08]
On the Saturday before the deadline, it seemed worth it.

She told herself that it was a professional precaution. A broker who arrived at the most important conversation of her life without an official statement of her funds would deserve whatever happened to her. That said, it is also true that she had now rehearsed the opening of the speech some two hundred times, and that going to a kiosk was something to do which was not, strictly speaking, going to Eastmere.

The nearest kiosk stood at the corner of Copper Street, in front of a bakery. It was a grey metal box the height of a man, with a grille at mouth level and a narrow slot beneath it. A pigeon was sitting on top of it, with the air of a creature that had sat there for years and expected to sit there for many more.

Petra stood in front of the grille. It was absurd to feel nervous, and she felt nervous. She looked left and right, as if she were about to cross a road. Then she leaned forward and spoke.

"Petra Anna Lind."

Her voice sounded strange to her, thin and a little too high. The kiosk made a small, satisfied noise, rather like a cat that has been fed. Somewhere inside it, a wheel began to turn.

While she waited, she did the arithmetic again, from memory, out of habit. Two million, four hundred and eleven thousand, give or take a few hundred. Minus three. She had decided some time ago that the speech would cost forty thousand, and that she would set aside another ten thousand for whatever her father said back, if he said anything at all, which was, at best, uncertain.

The slot hummed. A strip of paper began to come out of it, slowly, an inch at a time, as though the machine were reluctant to part with it.

### [p09]
Petra tore it off and read it.

She read it a second time, because the first time had obviously been a mistake. Then she turned it over, in case there was something on the back that would explain the front. There was not. The back was blank, in the way that only official paper can be blank, which is to say firmly.

The slip said that her balance stood at six million, sixty-five thousand, three hundred and two words.

Petra stood very still. The pigeon looked down at her with mild interest.

There was a mistake. That was the first and most comfortable thought, and she held on to it for some time. Kiosks were old. Kiosks were, in her experience, about as reliable as a cat's affection. Somewhere in the depths of the Service, a wheel had slipped, or a clerk had pressed the wrong key, and somebody else in Halvern was at that moment staring at a slip that was more than three and a half million words lighter than it should have been.

Then the broker in her woke up, and the comfortable thought went away.

Three and a half million words did not simply appear. Words were transferred, legally or otherwise; that was her whole profession. And if someone had been transferring words into her account without her knowledge, then there were only two possibilities. Either it was a clerical error, in which case the Service would want them back, with apologies. Or it was not, in which case she was holding in her hand the kind of piece of paper that ended brokers' careers.

Unregistered transfers were the one thing the Exchange did not forgive. Failure to report one was grounds for the immediate withdrawal of a broker's licence.

She folded the slip very small and put it in her pocket. Two million four hundred thousand words she had saved, word by word, for eleven years. The other three and a half million, she had never seen in her life.

## [c3] Joaquim

### [p10]
She did not go to the Exchange on Monday morning. A broker with an unexplained fortune in her pocket should, by every rule she had ever taught an apprentice, have gone straight to the Registrar and declared it. Petra would rather have swallowed the slip.

A fault, on the other hand, was not a crime. If the meter itself was broken, then the numbers were simply wrong, and nobody lost a licence over a broken meter. So, at eight o'clock, wearing the red scarf because she had begun to feel undressed without it, she took the tram to the Glass Arcade, where the Service kept its Meter Repair Office.

The Arcade was a long, high corridor of shops under an iron and glass roof, most of them shut. At the far end, past a closed umbrella shop and a window full of dusty clocks, a door stood open, and from behind the door came a sound she had not heard in years.

Someone was talking. Not a word here and there, as people did at work, but talking, steadily, cheerfully, the way water runs out of a tap.

"No, no, no, you don't get to do that, not after I've cleaned you. Ungrateful. Absolutely ungrateful. Do you know how many people would like to be cleaned with this much care? Hundreds. Thousands, possibly."

Petra stood in the doorway. Behind a counter covered in tools and small grey collars sat a large man of about sixty, with a short white beard, a leather apron and a pair of magnifying glasses pushed up on his forehead. He was addressing a meter, which lay open on the counter in front of him, like a patient.

He looked up and saw her. His face, which seemed built for pleasure, became even more pleased.

"A customer! Come in, come in. Don't mind him, he's being difficult. Joaquim," he added, pointing at himself, as if that settled everything. "What's broken?"

Petra took the slip out of her pocket and put it on the counter.

### [p11]
Joaquim read the slip, whistled, and read it again.

"Well," he said. "That's not broken. That's rich. Congratulations."

Petra signed to him, carefully, that it was more than she had saved; that she believed there might be a fault; that she would like her meter checked.

"You can talk to me, you know," said Joaquim. "I won't tell anyone." But he held out his hand for the meter all the same.

She unfastened the catch at the back of her neck. It was the first time in years that she had taken it off in front of anyone, and her throat felt cold and oddly exposed, as if she had removed a tooth rather than a collar. Joaquim laid the meter in a small cradle wired to a grey cabinet, pulled his magnifying glasses down over his eyes, and turned a handle. The cabinet ticked.

"Meter's fine," he said after a minute. "Clean as a whistle. Counts what you say, keeps what you don't. You've been very good, haven't you? Hardly a word out of you in years." He turned the handle again, and a long strip of paper began to roll out of the side of the cabinet. "Now, then. Here's your problem. Or your present, depending how you look at it."

He ran a thick finger down the paper.

"Nine hundred words. Six minutes past six in the morning. Every morning. Here, and here, and here. Every single day for, let's see," he squinted, "four thousand and sixty days. That's eleven years and a bit. Regular as a clock."

"From whom?" said Petra, before she could stop herself. Two words.

"From an account marked dormant. That means the owner doesn't spend anything. Not a word. Never." Joaquim tapped the paper. "The name's protected, I'm afraid. The sender asked for no notice to be sent. You can do that, if you fill in the right form. Hardly anybody does."

Petra let out a breath she had been holding since Saturday. "So it's registered."

"Every word of it. Perfectly legal. A gift." He smiled. "You needn't have worried."

### [p12]
It would be pleasant to report that Petra immediately understood what she was looking at. She did not. Or rather, a small part of her mind understood it at once and was firmly told to be quiet by the rest.

A dormant account. Nine hundred words a day, every day, from somebody who never spoke. In Halvern, there were perhaps a dozen people known never to speak, and only one of them had a daughter. Petra looked at this thought, decided that it was sentimental, unprofessional and almost certainly wrong, and put it away in the same drawer where she kept the matter of the Chime.

What she noticed instead, because it was easier, was Joaquim's meter.

He wore one, like everybody else: an old model, thicker than hers, with a dent in the side. But the amber strip at his throat was dark. Entirely dark. And he had been talking, by her count, for nearly ten minutes. She had begun counting from habit when she came in, and had stopped at four hundred out of something like embarrassment. The strip had not changed. It had not flickered. Never had she seen a meter so completely indifferent to its owner.

"Technicians are exempt?" she asked. It came out as a question and cost three.

Joaquim laughed, a big, warm laugh that filled the little room and went out into the Arcade, where a pigeon took it personally and left.

"Exempt! That's a good word for it." He took her meter out of the cradle and polished it on his apron. "The man who trained me used to say that a meter is only as honest as the throat it sits on. Very wise man. Said very little, but when he said it, you remembered." He turned to a form on the counter and picked up a pen. "Name, for the paperwork?"

She wrote it for him, to save words. He looked at it for slightly longer than a name requires.

"Lind," he said. "That's a good name. A good name for this trade, as it happens."

### [p13]
"Did you know someone called Lind?" Petra asked. Six words. She was spending more in this little shop than she usually spent in a week, and she noticed that she did not much mind.

"Oh, I knew a great many people, once," said Joaquim, which was not an answer, and they both knew it. He handed her the meter. "Put that back on, or you'll catch cold. Now. Tell me why a woman with six million words in the bank looks as if she's about to sit an examination."

She told him. Not all of it, but more than she had told anyone. She told him about the notice on the board at the Exchange, and the deadline, which was tomorrow at midnight. She told him that she had been saving for one conversation, and that the conversation was with someone across the river, and that she had a speech, and that the speech was forty thousand words long. She did not say who the someone was. She suspected that she did not need to.

Joaquim listened without interrupting, which for him was clearly an effort of heroic proportions. When she had finished, he was quiet for a moment. Then he leaned on the counter and looked at her over the top of his magnifying glasses.

"Forty thousand words," he said. "For one man. And you've been saving for eleven years, so that you could afford to say them."

"Yes."

"And you've been a broker for eleven years. You've counted other people's words, and lent them, and sold them. You know more about meters than anyone who walks in here." He nodded at his own dark throat. "And in all that time, Miss Lind, did it really never occur to you to ask?"

"Ask what?"

He tapped the dented collar gently with one finger.

"Have you never wondered why mine doesn't count?"

## [c4] The Archive

### [p14]
Petra did not answer him. It was not that she lacked the words; she had six million of them. It was that every answer she could think of began with "I assumed", and she had a broker's dislike of that phrase.

"Technicians," she said at last. "Some special arrangement."

"I hope not," said Joaquim cheerfully. "I'd hate to think I was special. Look. I'm not going to tell you anything. If I told you, you wouldn't believe me, and you'd be right not to, because I'm a man who talks to broken meters." He pulled a scrap of paper towards him and wrote on it. "The Service keeps an archive. Reading room on the ground floor of the old building on Lantern Street. Open to the public on Tuesdays, which is why nobody goes."

"Tomorrow's Tuesday," said Petra.

"So it is."

"Tomorrow I'm supposed to be in Eastmere."

"Eastmere will still be there at teatime," said Joaquim. "It's been there for three hundred years. It's a very patient sort of place." He folded the paper and held it out. "Go and read something. You're good at reading. Then go and say whatever it is you need to say."

She would rather not have taken the paper. She took it. At the door, she turned, and he lifted one hand and made a sign she had not seen since she was a child, a small circle with the thumb followed by a flat palm turned downwards. It meant, roughly, go home. Parents made it to children who were dawdling in the street.

That night, she did not sleep. She lay in the dark with the folded paper on the table beside her and the speech running through her head, and between the paragraphs of the speech, quietly and persistently, ran Joaquim's question. Having asked it, he had not waited for an answer. That was the most irritating thing about it.

At nine on Tuesday morning, she put on the red scarf and went to Lantern Street. An hour, she told herself. An hour at most.

### [p15]
The old building of the Word Service was a tall, grey, serious place with columns at the front, built in an age when public buildings were expected to frown. The reading room was on the ground floor, at the end of a corridor lined with portraits of men and women in grey collars, none of whom appeared to have said anything amusing in their lives.

Behind a desk at the entrance sat a thin, elderly clerk with a name badge that said Fenwick. He looked at Petra as if she were a rare bird that had flown in through a window, and he was not entirely sure whether to feed it or report it.

She signed that she wished to see the reports on the spoken allowance. He signed back, with great precision, that they were on the third aisle, the bottom two shelves, and that the room closed at one o'clock, and that food was not permitted. Then he went back to doing nothing, which he did with an expert's calm.

The reports were bound in grey cloth, one volume per year. Petra knelt on the floor and took out the first.

It was forty years old. Its pages had gone the colour of weak tea, and its opening paragraph had been written by someone who was clearly very tired.

The period now generally referred to as the Noise Years, it said, has been characterised by the uncontrolled growth of public and private speech, the multiplication of broadcasts and loudspeakers, and the near-total breakdown of the citizen's ability to hear, think, or sleep. The introduction of a daily spoken allowance is accordingly proposed as a temporary measure, for a period not exceeding three years. It is essential that every adult citizen be fitted with a meter, and that the measure be reviewed annually.

Petra read the sentence about the three years twice. Then she read the date at the top of the page again, and did a small, unwelcome sum.

Three years. And then forty.

### [p16]
She went through the volumes in order, because she did not know how else to do it.

The second report was long and confident. Complaints about noise had fallen dramatically. Hospitals reported that patients slept better. Rates of argument in public places had declined by a figure that someone had evidently worked very hard to calculate. The report recommended the continuation of the measure for a further year, pending review.

The third report recommended the continuation of the measure for a further year, pending review. So did the fourth, and the fifth, and the sixth. By the eighth, the reports had grown noticeably thinner, and the recommendations had moved from the end of the volume to the beginning, as if the writers had grown tired of making the reader wait for them. By the tenth, there was no longer any discussion at all, only tables. By the twelfth, the tables had begun to repeat themselves, word for word, from previous years. Somebody, somewhere in the Service, had clearly stopped believing that anyone would read them.

Granted, Petra thought, that was not a crime. Nobody had read them. She was, quite possibly, the first person to open some of these volumes since the day they were bound. She found this a strangely moving thought, and then a slightly alarming one, because it suggested that she had become the kind of person who was moved by tables.

It was past twelve when she realised that she had not eaten, that her knees hurt, and that Mr Fenwick had been watching her for some time with an expression of polite concern, as one might watch a stranger who has begun to talk to the furniture.

She reached for the next volume, and found that there was only one left.

It was the thinnest of all. It had no year on the spine, only a title in small grey letters: FINAL REPORT ON THE SPOKEN ALLOWANCE.

### [p17]
Petra opened it with a care that would have done credit to a surgeon.

It was dated twenty-five years earlier. There was no year after it. There was nothing after it at all, only the end of the shelf and a little dust.

The first page was headed Summary of Findings, and it was written in a different voice from the others. The writer of the earlier reports had sounded tired. This one sounded careful, like a man walking across a frozen lake.

It would appear, it said, that the objectives of the spoken allowance have been substantially achieved. Levels of public noise have been stable for more than a decade. Arguably, the measure has achieved more than was intended. One might suggest that the habits of quiet are now so firmly established among the population that the measure no longer depends on enforcement for its effect. It is accordingly recommended that…

The sentence went over the page.

Before she could turn it, a bell rang. It was a small bell, of the kind used to summon servants in old houses, and it was being rung by Mr Fenwick, who stood at the end of the aisle with the bell in one hand and his watch in the other. It was one o'clock. The reading room was closed.

Petra signed that she needed one minute. Mr Fenwick signed, with the regret of a man who has enforced this rule for thirty years and intends to enforce it for thirty more, that the room was closed. He held out his hand for the volume.

As she gave it to him, it fell open near the end, and she saw a single line at the bottom of a page, in the same careful prose: should the citizens continue to observe the allowance, they will be observing something that is…

Then the cover closed, and she was in the corridor, and the door was locked behind her.

The last report on the quota had been written twenty-five years ago. Since then, it would appear, nobody had written anything at all.

## [c5] Habit

### [p18]
She sat down on the steps of the old building, because her legs suggested it, and looked at Lantern Street.

Something that is. Something that is what? Something that is necessary. Something that is, on balance, still beneficial to the public. Reports were full of sentences like that; she had read forty years of them. There was no reason at all to supply the obvious ending, the one that had been sitting in her head since half past twelve with its coat on, waiting to be let in.

Twenty-five years ago, the Service had stopped writing reports.

Twenty-five years ago, when Petra was four, her father had left the Service.

She considered these two facts side by side, the way she would consider two signatures on a contract, and concluded, with professional firmness, that they had nothing to do with each other. Many people had left the Service twenty-five years ago. It had been a large organisation. People left large organisations all the time; it was arguably the main thing people did with them.

Having settled this, she found she was hungry. She went into a bakery on the corner and pointed at a bun, and the baker handed it to her without a word, and took her coin without a word, and nodded her out of the shop without a word, and the whole transaction was so smooth, so quiet and so perfectly ordinary that Petra stood on the pavement afterwards holding the bun and feeling, for the first time in her life, faintly unsettled by it.

When had the baker last spoken to a customer? Did he know how? Did he want to?

It was a quarter past two. Eastmere was an hour's walk away. She had nearly ten hours until midnight, and a speech that would take six. On any reasonable calculation, there was time to spare.

Petra had been making reasonable calculations for eleven years. She ate the bun, and began to walk.

### [p19]
She walked slowly, and she looked at the city as if she had just arrived in it.

On the corner of Lantern Street, two old men were playing chess on an upturned box, in complete silence, which was how chess had always been played and so told her nothing. On a bench by the canal, a young couple were having what was clearly an argument entirely in the Sign, their hands flying, their faces furious, their meters glowing full and untouched at their throats. A tram went by, packed with people looking at their shoes.

Outside a school, a small boy ran out of the gate shouting something about a frog. It was a long, joyful, completely unnecessary sentence, and his mother caught him by the shoulder and put a finger to her lips, and he stopped at once, mid-frog, with the guilty face of a child who has been caught spending money that was not his.

Children under twelve did not wear meters. Everybody knew that. Everybody also knew that you hushed them anyway, because it was good practice.

Petra stood and watched the boy and his mother walk away, side by side, silent.

Suppose, she thought, purely as an exercise. Suppose that nothing would happen if the boy finished his sentence about the frog. Why, then, would his mother stop him?

Because her own mother had stopped her. Because it was rude to be loud. Because the neighbours were watching, and the neighbours had stopped their own children, and nobody wished to be the first family in the street to be heard. Because silence, after forty years, was not a rule any longer but a kind of good manners, and good manners, as everyone knows, are far harder to abolish than laws. Such is the comfort of a rule that, after a while, one no longer needs to believe in it to obey it.

And then there were the brokers. There was the Exchange, with its four floors of offices and its framed licences. There were the loans, the wedding vows, the percentages. A whole profession, her profession, built on the careful buying and selling of…

She stopped that thought quite firmly, and walked on.

### [p20]
The point, she told herself, was that it did not matter.

That was the sensible position, and she held it all the way down to the river. Whether or not the meters still counted, whether or not the Chime still sang, she was going to Eastmere tonight to say something she had been preparing for eleven years. The arithmetic of the thing was beside the point. One does not, after all, refuse to attend one's own wedding simply because one has discovered that the cake is made of cardboard.

That said, she was a broker, and brokers do not like discovering that the thing they sell is made of cardboard.

If it were true, then everything she had done since she was eighteen would look rather different. If the meters had stopped mattering twenty-five years ago, she would now be a woman who had spent eleven years selling something that was not there. She would also be a woman who had stayed away from her father for eleven years in order to save up for a conversation that had, all along, been free.

Even so, it was not true. It could not be true. Her grandmother had heard the Chime in the post office. Joaquim was a man who talked to broken meters. The report was simply a report, and the line at the bottom of its page might have ended in any number of ways.

By the time she reached the Iron Bridge, the sun had gone behind the warehouses and the river had turned the colour of old pewter. She stopped in the middle of the bridge, because her legs suggested it again, and she leaned on the cold rail and watched the water.

She told herself she was rehearsing. She went through the opening three times, the pause after Father carefully timed. Then she went through the section about the concert. When she next looked up, the lamps had come on along both banks, and it was nearly nine o'clock.

### [p21]
Eastmere had not changed. That was the first thing, and in some ways the worst.

The embankment still smelled of wet stone and coal smoke. The hill was still too steep, and the old water tower at the top of it still leaned very slightly towards the east, as if it were trying to hear something in the next district. The third street on the right was still narrow, still badly lit, and still lined on both sides with tall, thin houses that seemed to have been pushed together by a giant who had run out of room.

His was the seventh on the left. The door was blue, or had been blue once. The paint had faded to the grey-blue of a winter sky, and the brass number had gone green.

There was a light in the front window. Through the gap in the curtains, Petra could see the corner of a workbench she remembered from childhood, and on the bench, in neat rows, small grey shapes that might have been tools and might have been parts of something larger. He had always mended things. When she was small, she had sat under that bench and handed him screws, and he had nodded his thanks, and it had been, she now realised, one of the happiest arrangements of her life.

She stood across the street, in the shadow of a doorway, and did not move.

The old clock on the water tower had long since stopped, but somewhere in the district a tram bell rang the quarter-hours. She heard it ring the quarter, then the half, then the quarter again. People passed her occasionally and glanced at the red scarf, and one woman signed a polite question about whether she was lost, and Petra signed back that she was not, which was not quite true.

At half past ten, with the speech complete in her head and an hour and a half until midnight, she crossed the road.

### [p22]
She knocked three times. It seemed the right number: not timid, not aggressive, a broker's knock.

For a long moment, nothing happened. Then, from somewhere at the back of the house, there came the scrape of a chair, and footsteps, unhurried, coming down the narrow hall. They were the footsteps of a man who did not need to hurry, because whatever was at the door had either waited this long or would not wait at all. Petra knew those footsteps. She had heard them coming up the stairs every night of her childhood, pausing outside her door, and going on.

The lock turned. The door opened.

Matthias Lind was older. That was absurd, of course; eleven years had passed, and people did not stay the same age out of politeness. But she had carried an image of him for all that time, and the image had not aged, and now the real man was standing in front of it, thinner, slightly stooped, with grey hair that had been brown, in a cardigan she did not recognise and a pair of reading glasses pushed up into his hair, exactly as Joaquim wore his.

At his throat was a meter, an old one, the same thick model as Joaquim's. Its strip was dark.

Of course it was, Petra thought. He gave away his words at six minutes past six every morning. What would he have left to show?

It was the first time she had admitted it to herself in so many words, and it went through her like cold water.

He looked at her. He looked at her face, and at the red scarf, and at the notebook she was holding against her chest like a shield. Something moved in his face, something that came and went too quickly to be named.

She waited for him to speak. She had imagined this moment a thousand times, and in every version he said something, even if it was only her name.

He opened the door wider, stood aside, and did not say a single word.

## [c6] The Speech

### [p23]
The kitchen was at the back of the house, as it had always been. It was smaller than she remembered, which kitchens always are, and warmer, and tidier. There was a table with two chairs. There had always been two chairs. For eleven years, it occurred to her, there had been two chairs.

He filled the kettle and put it on the stove. He took down two cups, without asking, and put tea in a pot, and stood with his back to her while the water heated, looking at the window, in which there was nothing to see but the reflection of the kitchen and of the two of them in it.

Left alone with the back of his cardigan, Petra looked around her. On the wall above the stove hung a round clock with a white face and two black hands. It was twenty to eleven.

Petra did the arithmetic. She did it twice, because she did not like the answer the first time. A speech of six hours. A balance that would vanish at midnight. Eighty minutes, more or less, in which to say what she had spent eleven years preparing.

Had she come at noon, she would have had time for every word. Had she come yesterday, or last month, or five years ago, she would not be sitting in a cold chair calculating which parts of her life she could afford to leave out. It was not a useful thought. She had it anyway.

He put a cup in front of her and sat down on the other side of the table, his hands folded, and waited. It was the way he had always waited, when she was a child, for her to finish telling him about her day: patiently, without prompting, as though there were all the time in the world.

There was not. She opened the notebook, though she did not need it, and took a breath.

"Father," said Petra.

She counted four seconds in her head. One. Two. Three. Four. The clock ticked. He did not move.

"I have come," she said, "to say a number of things that I ought to have said a long time ago."

Nineteen words. Twenty, with Father.

### [p24]
The opening section dealt with the birthday. She had rewritten it many times, and in its final form it was a model of its kind: calm, precise, and almost entirely without adjectives. She described the evening. She quoted his six words exactly. She explained what she had taken them to mean, and how she had felt, and why she had left, and she did it in the language she used at the Exchange, because it was the only language she trusted not to tremble.

"I would like to place on record," she heard herself say, "that the absence of any further communication on your part was experienced by me as a confirmation of that judgement."

It was a terrible sentence. It had taken her four years to write. He listened to it without blinking.

She went on to the soup. She explained, with what she hoped was fairness, that it was not the soup itself that she objected to, which had generally been good, but the silence with which it had been served, night after night, so that she had grown up believing that care was something one did with one's hands and never with one's mouth. Two hundred and forty words. Three hundred. She counted them in the margins of her mind, the way she had counted them in pencil in eleven notebooks.

Then she looked at the clock, and it was eleven.

She was on the fourth page of sixty.

Hardly had she seen it when something in her chest went loose, like a knot coming undone. She turned five pages at once. The concert, she said, and summarised it in a sentence. Her mother's going to the coast: two sentences, and a third that she had not planned, saying that she supposed it had been hard for him too. Her first year at the Exchange, which had once filled half a notebook, she dealt with in a single breath.

She was no longer reading. She was no longer, she realised, entirely counting.

### [p25]
It is difficult to describe what happened to the speech in the next fifty minutes, because the speech, in any form Petra would have recognised, ceased to exist.

What came instead was faster, and plainer, and much less fair. She told him about her office, and the window in it, and the licence that nobody read. She told him about Osric Pell and his vow, and Hilde's longer one, and found to her surprise that she was laughing, and that across the table, very faintly, so was he: not with sound, but with the corners of his eyes, which she had forgotten could do that. She told him about Mrs Varga on the stairs, and about the red scarf, and how she had stood in front of it in the shop for nearly ten minutes because it was the only thing in the window that was not grey, and how she had thought, at the time, absurdly, that he would have liked it.

She told him that she had been to the kiosk, and to the Glass Arcade, and that she knew about the nine hundred words a day, every day, for four thousand and sixty days, from an account that never spent anything, and that she did not understand it, and that she would very much like to.

His hands, folded on the table, tightened.

She told him about the Archive, and the reports, and the three years that had become forty, and the last page she had not been allowed to read, and how she had sat on the steps afterwards with a bun, feeling as if the whole city had been holding its breath for so long that it had forgotten it was allowed to let go.

She did not look at the clock again. She did not need to. She could feel the minutes going, the way you feel sand going through your fingers, and the faster they went, the less she cared about the words that were going with them.

Never in her life had she spent so much so quickly. Never had it seemed so cheap.

### [p26]
At ten minutes to twelve, she arrived, without quite meaning to, at the last page.

The last page of the speech had never been rewritten. It was the one part that had stayed the same from the first notebook to the eleventh, through every draft and every year, because it was not an argument and could not be improved by arguing. It was only a sentence. She had always known that it would come at the end, after everything else, when there was nothing left to say but that.

She closed the notebook and put it face down on the table. Her voice, she noticed, had gone hoarse. She had spoken more in the last hour and a quarter than in the last six months.

"And the thing I came here to say," Petra said, "the thing all of it was for, the only part of the whole speech that I never once had to change, is that for eleven years, every single day, I have…"

Outside, somewhere in the district, the tram bell began to ring midnight.

At her throat, the meter gave a small, cold click. She felt it before she understood it: the faint warmth that had sat beneath her chin all evening, all year, all her adult life, drained away at once, like a candle pinched out. The amber light went dark. Six million words, saved and given, were gone between one heartbeat and the next.

Zero.

Petra stopped. Her mouth was still open on the next word, and the next word did not come. Every habit she possessed, every rule she had learned before she could read, rose up in her throat and held it shut. One more word, and the meter would sing. Everyone knew that. Her grandmother had heard it in a post office.

Across the table, her father had not moved. He was looking at her with an expression she could not read, and the dark collar at his own throat looked exactly like hers.

The kitchen clock ticked. The tram bell fell silent. She waited for the Chime.

## [c7] Zero

### [p27]
Nothing happened.

The clock ticked. A tap dripped once into the sink. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled, the way old houses settle at night, with a small sigh. Ten seconds passed, and then twenty, and then a whole minute, and the meter at her throat sat cold and dark and perfectly silent, like a stone.

Petra sat with her mouth still slightly open and felt, very slowly, the shape of the thing she had refused to look at all day come out of its drawer and sit down beside her.

Joaquim, talking for ten minutes without a flicker. A grandmother's story about a post office. Eleven years at the Exchange, and not one client below zero, not once, not ever; she had thought it was because they were careful. Three years that had become forty. Reports that had stopped, as if somebody had quietly closed a door and gone home. Should the citizens continue to observe the allowance, they will be observing something that is no longer there.

You'll be selling something that isn't there.

Only then did she understand what her father had said to her on her eighteenth birthday. It had not been an insult. It had been, very possibly, the longest and most important sentence he had ever tried to say, compressed into six words by a man who had never in his life learned how to say anything at greater length.

Little had she suspected, sitting under his workbench handing him screws, what it was that he mended.

She looked at him across the table. He was still watching her, still waiting, with that same patience, as if there were all the time in the world. And for the first time, it occurred to her that there was.

She closed her mouth. Then she opened it again, and finished her sentence, without counting, without a meter, and without any sound from her throat but her own voice.

"…missed you."

### [p28]
For a long moment, he did not answer. She thought, with a kind of despair, that perhaps he had forgotten how.

Then Matthias Lind cleared his throat. It took him two attempts. The first produced nothing but a dry rasp, like a door that has not been opened in years. The second produced a voice: low, rough, a little unsteady, and so entirely familiar that she had to hold the edge of the table.

"I know," said her father. "I have missed you too. Every day. Four thousand and sixty of them."

Then, as if the first words had broken something loose, more came.

He had been the Chief Calibration Engineer of the Word Service. It was he who had written the final report, at that same kitchen table, over a whole winter, because he was not a man who found writing easy. It was he who had recommended that the enforcement of the allowance be retired. And it was he who, one evening twenty-five years ago, had gone down to a room beneath Lantern Street with a screwdriver and disconnected the Chime from every meter in Halvern.

"It took eleven minutes," he said. "I had expected it to take longer. It seemed a great deal of silence to undo in eleven minutes."

"Why didn't anyone know?"

"We announced it." He almost smiled. "In the Service Bulletin. Page forty, under Minor Adjustments. I believe it is generally agreed that nobody has read the Service Bulletin since it was founded."

The meters, he explained, had gone on counting, because nobody had asked them to stop, and counting did no harm. It was not the counting that had kept the city quiet. It was the minding. And the minding, it turned out, did not need a wire.

He had waited for the city to notice. The city had not noticed. A year went by, and then five, and then ten, and Halvern went on whispering in its sleep, hushing its children, saving for its weddings, building a whole Exchange on top of a hole in the ground.

"And you," he said. "You went and became a broker."

### [p29]
"You could have told me," said Petra. "In plain words. You could have explained."

"I tried." He looked down at his hands. "I am not good at explaining. You will have noticed. I said it in six words, and they were the wrong six, and you heard something I did not mean. I could see it in your face. By the time I had found better words, you had packed your bag."

"You might well have come after me."

"I might." He did not argue. "I would rather have done anything than say it badly a second time. So I decided that I had said enough. That I would say nothing more until you came back. And I wanted you to come back," he said slowly, choosing each word as if it were heavy, "not because you had saved enough to afford me. Not because some notice on a board told you that your words were about to expire. I wanted you to come because you had something to say to me, and wanted to say it."

"So you gave me yours."

"Every morning. All nine hundred." His mouth moved at one corner. "It is not a sacrifice, you understand, to give away something worthless. But I thought that if you ever looked, you would know that somebody was keeping the line open. And by the same token, you would never be able to tell yourself that you could not afford to come."

Petra laughed. It came out of her like something escaping from a box, too loud for the small kitchen, and she did not care in the least.

"And Joaquim?"

"Joaquim was my apprentice. He came round on Monday evening." Her father looked, for a moment, almost embarrassed. "He talks, you know."

"I know."

"He said you might come. I have been sitting in that chair since six o'clock. I was wondering whether you would knock, or simply stand across the street until morning."

"I did stand across the street."

"I saw." He nodded at the window. "I would rather you hadn't. It was cold. Were it not for Joaquim, I would not even have known to look."

### [p30]
They talked all night.

They talked as people talk who have been saving up, which is to say badly, and out of order, and all at once, interrupting each other and apologising and starting again; about the soup, which he said had been perfectly good soup and she agreed had been perfectly good soup; about the concert, which he had attended, it turned out, standing at the back of the hall in a coat, and had left before the end because he had not trusted his face; about her mother and the coast and the letters, which he still had, in a box under his own bed, held together with a rubber band that had long since lost interest in its work; about Mrs Varga, and Osric Pell, to whom Petra decided she would have to explain, very gently, that he needn't have borrowed a single word; about the red scarf, which he said, a little shyly, was the best thing he had seen in Halvern in twenty-five years.

So freely did the words come that neither of them noticed when the clock passed three, and then four, and then five, and the window over the sink began to turn from black to grey.

At six o'clock, Petra felt a faint warmth at her throat.

It was the new day's nine hundred, arriving with the first trams, as it always had. Out of habit, she lifted her hand towards the meter. Then she let it fall, and left it unread, and went on talking.

Across the river, the trams were beginning to run. In a few hours, the city would wake and go to work and point at buns in bakeries and hush its children in the street, and it might well go on doing so for years, because habits are patient things, and Halvern was a patient city. Even so, Petra thought, somebody would have to tell it. Somebody who knew how meters worked, and how people worked, and how to explain a difficult thing in plain words to a room full of strangers. A broker, perhaps.

But that was for tomorrow. Today, in a warm kitchen in Eastmere, with the kettle on for the fourth time and her father saying, apropos of nothing, that he had been meaning to paint the front door for eleven years, she was busy. It was, by any reasonable calculation, the most expensive conversation in the history of Halvern, and it did not cost either of them a single word.
