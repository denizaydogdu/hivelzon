## [c1] Arrival

### [p01]
In the lake at Ardal, Tomás saw himself doing something he had not yet decided to do.

He had arrived the evening before, on the last bus of the day, with two cases of instruments, one small suitcase and a firm intention to leave within a fortnight. The road up from the valley had taken nearly three hours. It climbed through pine forest, then through a grey landscape of loose stone, and finally came out above a long, narrow lake lying between two mountains like a blade that someone had put down and forgotten.

The village sat at the northern end of the water: thirty or forty stone houses, a closed school, a single shop that was also the post office, and a guesthouse with blue shutters. Smoke rose straight up from the chimneys. There was no wind at all.

"You're the man from the Water Office," said the woman who opened the guesthouse door. She was in her fifties, broad-shouldered, with flour on her sleeves. "I'm Petra. You'll want the room at the front. It has the best view of the lake."

"I'd rather have a quiet one," Tomás said.

"They're all quiet," said Petra. "This is Ardal."

The truth was that he would rather not have come at all. Nobody at the office had wanted the job. A small village had written a long, polite letter objecting to a plan to draw water from its lake, and among the reasons it gave was one that had made the whole department laugh. The lake, the letter said, remembered tomorrow. Anyone who looked into it at first light could see themselves as they would be the following day.

It was Tomás who had been chosen to go and prove otherwise. Dr Hallam, his director, had made it clear that the post of senior hydrologist was his if the report was clean, quick and convincing.

### [p02]
He did not intend to look. That was the irony he would return to later, many times. He went down to the shore at six the next morning only to choose a place for his equipment, and he was thinking about cable lengths and battery life, not about tomorrow.

The lake was perfectly still. Mist lay on it in thin layers, and the far mountains were the colour of a bruise slowly healing, purple fading into grey. Then the sun touched the highest peak, and a line of gold ran down the rock and across the water towards him.

He crouched at the edge to test the stones, and there he was.

It was his reflection, certainly. The same dark hair, too long at the back; the same thin face that his sister said looked permanently disappointed. But the man in the water was not crouching. He was standing beside a low stone wall, and he was taking off his coat, the grey wool coat that Tomás was wearing at that very moment, and putting it around the shoulders of a small boy.

Tomás stood up so fast that he almost slipped.

When he looked again, the water showed exactly what it should have shown: a tired man in a grey coat, crouching on wet stones, with mist behind him.

He stayed there for a long time, breathing carefully, the way he had been taught to breathe before exams. Then he did what any sensible scientist would do. He took out his notebook and wrote down what he had seen, in plain language, with the time.

Observation one, he wrote. Twelve minutes past six. Possible visual anomaly on the water surface, lasting approximately three seconds. The most likely explanation is fatigue combined with suggestion. Further observation is required.

Underneath, in smaller letters, he added: You are tired, and you read that letter too many times.

### [p03]
At breakfast Petra put a bowl of porridge in front of him and a pot of honey beside it, and asked nothing. That was somehow worse than questions.

A boy of about eight was sitting at the other end of the kitchen table, swinging his legs and eating toast with great concentration. He had a pointed chin and a cloud of fair hair that no comb had ever defeated.

"This is my grandson, Nico," Petra said. "His mother works in the valley during the week. Nico, this is Mr Arrieta. He's come to measure the lake."

"Why?" asked Nico.

"To find out how deep it is, how cold it is and what's in the water," said Tomás.

"It's very deep, it's very cold and there are fish in it," Nico said. "You needn't have come all this way. You could have just asked me."

Tomás laughed for the first time since he had arrived. "That's a fair point. Unfortunately, my director won't accept you as a source."

"Why not?"

"Because you're eight."

"Eight and a half," said Nico, deeply offended, and went back to his toast.

Tomás spent the rest of the day setting up his equipment on a flat rock at the southern end of the shore. It was work he enjoyed: practical, exact and free of opinions. By the evening he had a temperature line running down into the dark water, a small camera fixed on a tripod facing the lake, and a list of tasks for the morning.

He had almost persuaded himself that the reflection had been nothing. Almost. Twice during the afternoon he caught himself standing at the water's edge, looking down, and twice he walked away before the water could show him anything other than his own face.

That night it turned cold. He slept badly and dreamt of nothing he could remember.

### [p04]
The next morning was the coldest yet. Frost covered the low walls, and the mist over the lake had frozen into tiny bright crystals that hung in the air like dust in a sunbeam. Tomás left the guesthouse at a quarter to seven, his head full of numbers, and took the path along the shore.

The school bus to the valley stopped at the end of the village, beside a low stone wall and an old fountain that no longer worked. A single child was waiting there, jumping from foot to foot. It was Nico, in a thin red jumper, with his school bag on his back and no coat at all.

"Where's your coat?" asked Tomás.

"I left it at school on Friday," Nico said through chattering teeth. "Grandma doesn't know. Please don't tell her."

What happened next took perhaps five seconds. Tomás did not think about it, did not weigh it up, did not decide. He simply took off his grey wool coat and put it around the boy's shoulders, as if his hands had been told what to do by someone else. Nico disappeared inside it almost completely.

"You'll give it back to me this afternoon," said Tomás. "Don't lose this one."

The yellow bus came round the bend, and Nico climbed in, waving an enormous grey sleeve. Only when the bus had disappeared did Tomás understand what he had done. He was standing beside a low stone wall, without his coat, in exactly the position he had seen in the water the day before.

Never had he felt so cold, and the frost was the least of it.

### [p05]
He walked back to the guesthouse slowly, trying to be sensible about it. People gave children their coats every day. It was the kind of thing anyone might well have done on such a morning, and he had been primed, as psychologists say, by the picture he had imagined the day before. The memory of the reflection had probably shaped his behaviour, not the other way round. That was the obvious explanation, and obvious explanations were usually right.

And yet. What disturbed him was not that he had given away his coat. It was that he had not chosen to give it away. He had watched himself do it, as one watches a stranger in the street.

In the shop that afternoon, buying a cheap jacket to wear until Nico came home, he mentioned the lake to the old man behind the counter, in a light, careless voice.

"People say you can see things in it," he said.

"People say a lot of things," the old man agreed.

"Have you ever seen anything?"

The old man smiled, wrapped the jacket in brown paper and said nothing at all.

It was the same everywhere. The woman at the shop door smiled at him. Two old men mending a boat on the shore smiled at him. Even Petra, when Nico came home at four o'clock in an enormous grey coat and told her the whole story, only smiled, and gave Tomás an extra slice of cake at dinner.

It is said that mountain villages are slow to trust strangers. In Ardal, it seemed to Tomás, the problem was the opposite: everyone appeared to know something about him that he did not yet know himself.

That night he wrote in his notebook, in his best scientific style: The coincidence of the reflection and the subsequent event is noted. No conclusions should be drawn from a single case.

Then he crossed out the word coincidence, and could not think what to put in its place.

## [c2] Instruments

### [p06]
For the next three days Tomás measured everything that could be measured.

He took the temperature of the lake at every metre down to forty metres, where the cable ended and the darkness did not. He measured how clearly light travelled through the water, how much iron and calcium it carried, and how quickly it moved from the streams in the north to the narrow outflow in the south, where it left the lake under an old stone bridge and fell away towards the valley. He collected samples in small glass bottles, labelled them neatly and packed them in a cool box for the laboratory.

The results were, without exception, normal. Not only were they normal; they were so typical that they could have been used to teach students what an ordinary mountain lake looked like. The water was cold, clean and slightly acidic. The surface was unusually still in the early morning, which was explained by the shape of the valley: the two mountains sheltered the lake from the wind until the sun had warmed the air. Even the famous mist behaved exactly as mist should.

The camera was the part he had been waiting for. He had set it to take a picture every ten seconds from five in the morning until nine. Whatever he saw with his own eyes, the camera would see too, and the camera had no imagination.

On the third evening he sat in his room and looked through four thousand photographs of the lake at dawn. They showed water, mist, mountains and light. Twice they showed Tomás himself, crouching at the edge and looking down. In both pictures his reflection was crouching too, as reflections are supposed to do.

He ought to have felt relieved. Instead, he felt as if he had been handed a beautifully wrapped box with nothing inside.

### [p07]
Because he had seen it again. Twice.

On the second morning the man in the water had been carrying the cool box up the path to the bus stop in heavy rain, which was absurd, because the sky was cloudless and the forecast promised a week of sunshine. The following morning it rained without warning from nine until noon, and the samples went down to the valley a day early because the driver warned him that the road might close.

On the third morning the reflection had shown him sitting on the end of Nico's bed, reading aloud from a book about volcanoes. That evening Petra had to go down to the valley because her sister was ill, and Nico, who was not used to strangers, refused to go to sleep unless the lake man read to him. The book about volcanoes had been his own choice.

Each time, Tomás had not decided anything. Things had simply arrived, and he had moved along with them, like a leaf carried by a stream.

So he tried a test. On the fourth morning, standing on the shore, he decided firmly that whatever the reflection showed, he would do the opposite. Then he looked down.

The water showed him his own face, a little pale, with the purple mountains behind him. Nothing else. He waited for a quarter of an hour, until his knees ached and the sun had fully risen, and the lake showed him nothing but himself and the sky.

He wrote: Observation seven. No anomaly. It is possible that the earlier anomalies were related to fatigue, which has now been reduced.

It was a reasonable sentence. He did not believe a word of it.

### [p08]
That afternoon Dr Hallam telephoned. The signal in Ardal was so weak that Tomás had to stand at the top of the hill behind the guesthouse, holding the phone out in front of him like a compass.

"Well?" said Dr Hallam. She never said hello. She believed it wasted everyone's time.

"The lake is completely ordinary," Tomás said. "Temperature, chemistry, flow. Everything is within the normal range."

"Excellent. Then you can write it up and come home."

"There are one or two observations I'd rather check first."

There was a pause. He could picture her behind her very clean desk in the city, taking off her glasses. "What sort of observations?"

"Visual ones. Probably nothing."

"Tomás." She said his name as if it were a warning. "The board meets in three weeks. They want to approve the water plan, and they want a report that tells them the objection has no scientific basis. I want the same thing. So, I imagine, do you." She let that sit for a moment. "You'd make an excellent senior hydrologist. Don't make it complicated."

"I won't," he said.

"Good." She was gone.

He walked down the hill feeling as if he had agreed to something he had not quite heard. Halfway down, he met a woman of about sixty coming up, carrying a basket of firewood on her back with the ease of someone who had done it all her life. She was tall and straight, with short grey hair and very dark, very attentive eyes.

"You'll be the hydrologist," she said. "I'm Oriana. When you've finished counting the fish, come and see me. I've got something you might well find more interesting."

Then she continued up the hill, and did not look back.

### [p09]
Hardly had he reached the shore the next morning when he heard voices.

Two old men were standing side by side at the water's edge, near the place where the boats were kept. Tomás recognised them as the men he had seen mending a boat on his first afternoon. One was tall and stooped, with a large white moustache; the other was short and round, and wore a hat that had clearly been older than he was for some time.

"That's Aurel and Constantin," Petra had told him. "They've owned that boat together for forty years. They argue about it every day. It's the happiest marriage in the village."

This morning they were not arguing. They were looking down into the water, very seriously, as if reading a letter.

Tomás walked towards them slowly. He did not want to interrupt, but he could not help listening.

"I'm selling," said Aurel, the tall one. "That's what I saw. Tomorrow I'm standing on this shore, shaking hands with some young man from the valley, and he's giving me money, and you're nowhere to be seen."

"Rubbish," said Constantin. "I saw us painting her. Tomorrow. Both of us. Green, the same as every year."

"Green," said Aurel with contempt. "It was never green. It's blue."

"It is green, you old fool, and tomorrow we are painting it."

Tomás stood a few metres behind them and looked down at the water beyond their shoulders. He saw two old men in it, which was what he ought to have seen. Then, for a moment, the surface seemed to divide like a page torn down the middle. On the left, Aurel was shaking hands with a stranger in a city jacket. On the right, Constantin was holding a paintbrush and laughing.

The same water, at the same moment, was showing two different tomorrows.

## [c3] The Ledger

### [p10]
Tomás did not sleep much that night. He lay on his back listening to the silence, which in Ardal was so complete that he could hear the blood in his own ears, and tried to find a physical explanation for what he had seen. Light could be bent by layers of warm and cold air; he knew that. Mirages were real. But no mirage he had ever read about showed two different futures at once.

The next morning, at about ten, he went down to the boats.

Aurel was standing on the shore, shaking hands with a young man in a city jacket. Money changed hands, and a sheet of paper was signed against the side of the boat. Constantin was nowhere to be seen.

So the tall man's reflection had come true, and the short man's had not. Tomás felt a strange, cold satisfaction, like a student who has guessed the answer to a question he does not understand.

It was not until the afternoon that he heard the rest of the story, from Petra, who heard everything. Aurel had not been selling his half of the boat to a stranger, it turned out. He had been selling it to Constantin, who had sent the young man as his agent because the two of them could not stand in the same place without arguing about colour.

"Aurel's son-in-law needs money for his roof, and he's been pushing Aurel to sell for months," Petra said, rolling out pastry. "Constantin sat up all night thinking about it. He told me this morning that he'd made up his mind. If someone had to buy Aurel's half, it would be him. He'd keep the boat, and paint it whatever colour he liked."

"And this morning? Did he look in the lake?"

"He did." Petra smiled her village smile. "He said there was nothing there. Only sky."

Tomás wrote that down too. He did not yet know why it mattered.

### [p11]
Oriana lived in the last house of the village, where the road became a path and the path became a goat track. Her kitchen was warm and smelt of woodsmoke and apples. Along one wall, from floor to ceiling, stood shelves of thick books bound in dark cloth.

"My grandmother started it," she said, before he could ask. "Eighty years ago this spring. She was a schoolteacher, and she didn't like stories that couldn't be checked. So she began to write them down."

She took one of the books from the shelf and opened it on the table in front of him. The pages were divided into neat columns, in faded brown ink: the date, the name of the person, what they had seen in the water, and, in a different hand, what had actually happened the next day.

"Anyone who looks can come and tell me," Oriana said. "Most people do. Not all. I write it down, and the following evening I write down what happened. My mother did it before me, and her mother before her. Nobody is obliged to tell me anything. It isn't a law. It's a habit."

Tomás turned the pages slowly. Some entries were tiny. Saw herself burning the bread. Burnt the bread. Others were longer, and more painful. Saw himself leaving for the city without saying goodbye to his father. Left without saying goodbye. Then, a few lines further down: Saw herself refusing the offer. Accepted it.

"Not everything comes true, then," he said.

"No," said Oriana. "Not everything."

"How much does?"

She looked at him with something that might have been amusement. "I've never counted," she said. "I'm not a scientist. I'd rather you did it. You'll trust the number more if it's yours."

### [p12]
It took him three nights.

He photographed every page, nine thousand four hundred and twelve entries in all, and began typing them into a table on his laptop, with columns for the date, the kind of event, whether the reflection had come true, and any comments. It was slow, careful, satisfying work, the kind of work he was good at, and for long stretches he forgot what the numbers were about.

On the first evening Nico came to sit beside him, supposedly to do his homework and actually to watch the screen.

"Is my name in there?" he asked.

"I don't know. Have you ever told Oriana what you saw?"

"I've never seen anything," Nico said, with deep disappointment. "Not once. Everyone in my class has seen something except me. Grandma says it's because I'm too bossy. I always know what I'm going to do. I decide everything at breakfast."

"What did you decide this morning?"

"That I'd beat Luka at football, and I'd have two pieces of cake, and I'd ask you about volcanoes again." Nico counted on his fingers. "I've done all three."

"You've had two pieces of cake?"

"I'm going to have the second one now," said Nico, and went to find it.

Tomás smiled and returned to his table. He did not write down what Nico had said. It did not seem like data. Only much later did he realise that it was perhaps the most important observation he made in Ardal, and that he had made it while thinking about something else.

Outside, the wind finally found its way into the valley and moved the shutters gently, as if someone were trying to get in without waking anyone.

### [p13]
Some entries did not fit into any column. On two hundred and twelve mornings, a person had looked into the lake and seen nothing at all. Oriana's grandmother had marked these with a single word in the margin: blank. Constantin was among them, on the most recent page, in Oriana's firm, upright handwriting. Tomás decided that they were failed observations and put them aside. A scientist cannot measure what is not there. That, at any rate, was what he told himself at two in the morning.

There were other comments too, in the column for what had happened. Changed her mind. Talked it over with his brother and decided not to. Sat by the water all night and chose. He noticed them the way you notice the pattern on a carpet you have crossed a thousand times, and paid them no more attention than that. Had he looked at them more closely, he might have saved himself a great deal of trouble.

On the third night he finished. He sat back, rubbed his eyes and looked at the number on the screen.

Leaving the blank mornings aside, nine thousand two hundred reflections had been recorded in eighty years. Five thousand six hundred and twelve of them had come true. The rest had not.

Sixty-one per cent.

It was a terrible number. That was his first thought, and it was a scientist's thought. A lake that showed the future would be right every time. A lake that showed only imagination would be right by chance, and for events as specific as burnt bread or a handshake on a particular morning, chance would be close to zero. Sixty-one per cent was neither. It was too high to be luck and too low to be prophecy.

It belonged nowhere at all.

## [c4] What You Want

### [p14]
He walked up to Oriana's house in the morning with his laptop under his arm, like a doctor bringing difficult news.

"Sixty-one," he said.

Oriana poured him a cup of coffee. "Is that a lot?"

"It's impossible," he said. "It's a number with no explanation. If the lake predicted the future, it would be a hundred. If it were all imagination, it would be almost nothing. What the lake does, apparently, is predict the future slightly more often than it doesn't."

"Perhaps it isn't predicting anything."

"Then what is it doing?"

Oriana sat down opposite him and folded her hands. "It is said in Ardal," she said, "that the lake remembers tomorrow for those who forget to choose it. My grandmother used to say that. I don't know exactly what she meant. I suspect she did."

"That isn't an explanation. It's a proverb."

"So it is," Oriana agreed calmly. "But you're the one with the impossible number, not me."

Walking back along the shore, Tomás stopped at the water, as he now did without thinking. The lake was grey and quiet under a low sky. There was nobody in it but himself.

What he needed, he decided, was a theory. Any theory was better than a number that belonged nowhere.

### [p15]
He built the theory over two days, the way he had been taught to build anything: carefully, from the bottom up, testing each stone before putting the next one on top.

The reflections that came true, he noticed, were rarely surprising to the people who saw them. A woman saw herself leaving her husband's family business to open a bakery, and did. A young man saw himself telling a girl at the summer dance that he loved her, and did, rather badly, according to Oriana's grandmother, who is said to have been at the dance herself. Aurel had wanted to sell his half of the boat for years; everyone in the village knew it. Tomás himself, if he was honest, had wanted to give his coat to the shivering child the moment he saw him.

The reflections that did not come true were different. People saw themselves doing things they claimed not to want, and then did not do them.

So perhaps the lake did not show the future at all. Perhaps it showed desire: whatever a person wanted most, whether they admitted it or not. And desire turned into action, he reasoned, only some of the time. People were cowardly, or busy, or tired. Sixty-one per cent seemed, if anything, a rather generous estimate of human courage.

That evening he wrote the theory down in his best professional language.

It would appear that the reported images do not represent future events as such, but rather the observer's strongest current desire. The observed rate of fulfilment, sixty-one per cent, might plausibly reflect the proportion of desires that are acted upon. Further investigation is clearly required.

He read it twice. It was elegant. It explained the number. It was arguably the best idea he had had in years.

There was only one way to test it, and he had been avoiding it for a week.

### [p16]
He went down to the shore the next morning before anyone else was awake.

The air was very still and very cold. The mist lay low, and the mountains on the far side were the deep purple of the hour before sunrise. He stood at the edge of the water with his hands in the pockets of his grey coat and told himself that this was an experiment like any other. He would look, he would observe, he would record. If the lake showed what he wanted most, then he would at last find out what that was. It was strange, he thought, that a man of thirty-four should need a lake to tell him.

What he expected to see, he realised, was the city. His office, perhaps, with the new title on the door. Dr Hallam shaking his hand. The quiet satisfaction of a job properly finished.

The sun reached the top of the mountain. Gold ran down the rock and across the water. He looked down.

The man in the lake was sitting on the bench in front of Petra's guesthouse, where the garden ran down to the water, in warm sunshine. There was a cup of coffee beside him, and Nico was at his feet, drawing something with chalk on the stones. He looked rested. He looked, Tomás thought with a shock, like someone who had lived in Ardal all his life.

Then the man in the water picked up a thick bundle of papers. Tomás knew at once what it was, even from above, even upside down. It was his report.

The man in the lake tore it in half, and then in half again, and dropped the pieces, one by one, into the water.

### [p17]
For a long time Tomás did not move.

So that was it. That was what he wanted most. Not the promotion, not the city, not the neat office with his name on the door. He wanted to stay here, in a village with one shop and almost no signal, and he wanted to throw away the very report he had come to write. If his theory was right, the lake had just read him more clearly than he had ever read himself.

He sat down on a cold rock and put his head in his hands.

It was ridiculous. He had known this place for less than a fortnight. He had no friends here, no work and no future. He had a career in the city that he had spent twelve years building, a flat with a view of the river, a sister who rang him on Sundays. If he had been the kind of man who threw things away, he would not be a candidate for senior hydrologist now. He would be something else entirely, and he did not know what.

And yet the man in the water had looked happy. Not excited, not triumphant. Simply happy, in the plain way that people are happy when nothing is pulling at them.

He thought about it all day. He thought about it while checking the instruments, while eating Petra's soup, while Nico explained at length the difference between a volcano that was sleeping and one that was dead. He would rather not have thought about it at all, but he could not stop.

That evening he went to his room, opened his laptop and looked at the empty document he had created on his first day.

If the lake was right, tomorrow he would tear his report to pieces. He had not yet written a single word of it.

## [c5] The Report

### [p18]
The phone rang at seven the next morning, which in Ardal was so unusual that Petra came out of the kitchen to stare at it. For once the signal had found its way down the hill, almost as if it had been sent.

"The board has moved the meeting," said Dr Hallam. "It's next Tuesday now. I need your report by Friday."

"Friday is very soon."

"Friday is three days away, and you've had nearly two weeks. You told me yourself that the lake is ordinary. It's high time this whole business was put to rest." There was a short silence on the line. "Your last message mentioned some old books."

"A family in the village has kept records of the reflections for eighty years. They're remarkably careful."

"I'm sure they are. Tomás, listen to me. Write that the physical and chemical measurements are normal. Write that the objection has no scientific foundation. You needn't mention the books at all. In fact, I'd rather you didn't. Should anyone on the board ask about them, you can say they were outside the scope of the study."

"And if there's something I can't explain?"

"Then don't explain it." Her voice softened, which was more alarming than when it was hard. "You're good at this. You're careful, and you don't make trouble. That's why I sent you. Don't make me regret it."

When she had rung off, he stood in the hall for a while, looking at the phone as if it might ring again and take something back.

Petra was still watching him from the kitchen door. She did not ask. She only said, "You'll want breakfast," and went to make it.

### [p19]
He did not mean to write the report that Dr Hallam wanted. He simply began with it, because it was the easiest place to begin.

His room was too warm, so he took a pad of paper and a pen and sat on the bench in front of the guesthouse, in the thin autumn sunshine. Petra brought him coffee. Nico, whose teacher was ill that day, settled on the stones at his feet with a box of coloured chalk and began to draw an enormous volcano.

The sentences came easily, as false sentences often do. All measured parameters fall within the expected range for a high mountain lake. No physical mechanism has been identified that could account for the reported phenomena. The objection would therefore appear to be without scientific foundation. By noon he had eight neat pages. They were clear, confident and professional. Reading them back, he felt slightly sick.

He looked up. The sun was warm on his face. There was coffee beside him and a volcano at his feet, and his hands were holding a thick bundle of papers. His fingers had already taken hold of the top edge, ready to tear.

He went very still.

It was exactly the scene from the water. He had not chosen any of it: not the bench, not the paper, not the sunshine. He had drifted into it, one small easy step after another, the way he had drifted into giving away his coat. In another moment he would have torn the pages, dropped them into the lake and felt, for an afternoon, as if he had done something brave. But it would not have been brave. It would have been the same as doing nothing. No report at all would only mean that someone else wrote the convenient one.

Slowly, he took his hands off the edge of the paper.

### [p20]
"Aren't you going to do it?" asked Nico, without looking up from his volcano.

Tomás stared at him. "Do what?"

"Whatever you're going to do. You've got a face like you're about to jump into the lake."

"I nearly did," Tomás said. "I've decided not to."

"Are you going to get into trouble?"

"I hope not. I expect so."

He went upstairs, opened the laptop and began again from the beginning. This time he did not write the easy sentences. He wrote the true ones, and the true ones took all night.

He reported every measurement, every temperature and chemical value, exactly as he had found them. He described the camera and its four thousand ordinary photographs. He described his own observations, in the first person and without apology, including the coat and the volcanoes. Then he described the ledger: its age, its method, the eighty years of entries kept by three generations of the same family, the number of reflections, the sixty-one per cent.

His final paragraph was the hardest to write, and he rewrote it eleven times.

The physical and chemical characteristics of the lake are unremarkable. However, the consistency of the reports over a period of eighty years, together with the author's own direct observations, does not permit the classification of the objection as unfounded. In the author's view, the dismissal of these reports without further study would be premature. It is recommended that no decision on the water plan be taken until a full season of observation has been completed.

He read it once more at five in the morning. It was not elegant. It would certainly cost him the promotion, and it might well cost him rather more. It was also, he thought, the first thing he had written in years that was entirely his own.

At half past five, he sent it.

### [p21]
Then, because he could think of nothing else to do, he went down to the lake.

The village was asleep. A single light was burning in Oriana's kitchen at the far end of the shore, and nowhere else. The mist was thinner than usual, and the air smelt of snow, though it was only October. Tomás walked to the flat rock where his equipment stood and looked down at the water, as he had every morning for a fortnight.

He saw the sky.

He saw the pale gold edge of the dawn, and the dark purple of the mountains, and one small cloud moving slowly from left to right. He did not see himself at all. Where his face ought to have been, there was only light and air, as if he had stepped out of the picture and left the frame empty.

He knelt down and put his hand in the water. It was so cold that it hurt. When the ripples had cleared, there was still nothing there.

It is generally accepted by physicists that a reflection cannot simply disappear: if light travels from a face to the surface of the water, some of it must come back. Tomás knew the physics very well. He had once taught it to bored first-year students. He knelt on the rock for a long time, with his cold hand pressed against his chest, and found that he was not afraid. He was, if anything, curious, the way he had been as a child, before he had learned that curiosity was something you were supposed to grow out of.

The next morning, the water was empty again. The morning after that, it was the same.

From the moment he sent the report, the lake had stopped showing him anything at all.

## [c6] Storm

### [p22]
Dr Hallam's reply arrived that afternoon, when the signal allowed it. It was four lines long.

Your report has been received. Its conclusions are not what the board was led to expect, and they will need to be discussed. Please return to the office on Thursday next week, once the board has met. We will talk about your position then.

Tomás read it at the top of the hill, with the phone held out like a compass. Your position. Not your promotion; not your future. He might well have no position at all by the end of next week. If he had written the report Dr Hallam wanted, he would be a senior hydrologist by now. He waited to feel frightened, and felt instead a kind of tired lightness, like a man who has carried a heavy bag up a mountain and finally put it down, without knowing yet whether he will have to pick it up again.

He had given a copy of the report to Oriana. She read it at her kitchen table, slowly, all the way through, while he drank her coffee and pretended not to watch.

"You put us in it," she said at last.

"You're the best data I've got."

"My grandmother would have liked that sentence." She closed the folder. "They'll be angry with you in the city."

"I expect so."

"And you'll go back on Thursday."

"That's what I'm supposed to do," he said. He noticed that he had not said yes.

On his way back along the shore he passed Aurel and Constantin, who were painting the boat together. Constantin now owned the whole of it, but this appeared to have changed nothing at all. They were painting it green, and arguing about the blue stripe that Aurel insisted on adding along the side.

Six days later, on a heavy, windless afternoon, the sky above the southern mountain turned the colour of an old bruise.

### [p23]
The storm came over the mountain shortly after dark.

It began politely, with a few heavy drops that left round marks on the stones, and within half an hour it was the loudest thing Tomás had ever heard. The rain did not fall so much as arrive, in great grey sheets that the wind threw against the windows. The shutters banged. Somewhere a gate swung open and shut, open and shut, all night.

At ten o'clock he went downstairs to check the instrument readings on his laptop, and stopped.

The level of the lake was rising. Not slowly, as it would after ordinary rain, but steadily, a centimetre every few minutes. By now he knew the shape of the lake better than the street he lived on in the city. He knew how much water the northern streams could carry, and how quickly it could leave through the narrow outflow under the old stone bridge. Those two numbers had always been in balance. Tonight they were not.

"Something's blocking the outflow," he said aloud.

Petra appeared in the kitchen doorway in her dressing gown, holding a torch. "What does that mean?"

"It means the water can't get out. If it keeps rising at this rate, it will reach the lower lane by two in the morning. Your kitchen is on the lower lane."

Petra did not waste a second on questions. She was already pulling on her boots. "Nico," she called up the stairs, "you're staying in bed, and I mean it. Tomás, go and wake Constantin and Aurel. They've got ropes, and they know the bridge. I'll get Oriana. She'll know who else to call."

Having lived in Ardal all her life, she did not need to be told twice that the lake could be dangerous.

### [p24]
By eleven, a dozen people were standing on the old stone bridge in the rain, with torches, lamps and coils of rope. Under the arch, where the lake should have been pouring out into the valley, a fallen pine lay across the channel, and behind it a mass of branches, leaves and mud had built itself into a wall.

"We can't just pull it all out," Tomás shouted over the noise. "If the whole thing goes at once, the water will come through with enormous force. Nobody goes near the channel. Nobody goes into the water. Is that clear?"

Nobody argued, which in Ardal was itself a kind of miracle.

So they did it the slow way, the way he had once seen engineers do it on a river in the south. Aurel and Constantin tied ropes to the top branches from the safety of the bridge, and the others pulled them free one at a time, from above, so that the water could find many small paths through the wall instead of one large one. Tomás watched the lake level on his phone, which for once had a signal, and called out each time it changed. Oriana stood at the end of the bridge with a lamp, sending tired people home to rest and bringing fresh ones back. Petra made coffee in the house nearest the bridge and carried it out in a large pot, as if this were a festival.

Aurel and Constantin argued the whole time: about knots, about which branch was next, about whether the tree was a pine or a fir. Not once did they disagree about what actually had to be done.

At a quarter past one, the last of the large branches came free. The water under the arch gave a deep, satisfied roar, like an animal that had been holding its breath, and rushed away into the dark valley. On the screen of Tomás's phone, the line that had been climbing all night slowly bent, flattened, and began, very gently, to fall.

### [p25]
Nobody went to bed. There did not seem to be any point.

They sat in Petra's kitchen, which was dry, wrapped in blankets, drinking coffee that had been made and remade so many times that it tasted of smoke. Aurel fell asleep sitting upright, his moustache rising and falling. Constantin told the story of the night three times, and each time the tree got bigger. Nico, who had stayed in bed only in the strictest technical sense, sat on the stairs in his pyjamas and was allowed, for once, to stay up.

"If you hadn't been watching that machine of yours," Petra said to Tomás, "my kitchen would be full of lake now."

"If I hadn't been sent here to prove you all wrong," said Tomás, "I wouldn't have been watching it at all."

Oriana laughed, which he had never heard her do before.

Just before dawn the rain stopped. The silence afterwards was enormous. Tomás went outside alone and walked down to the shore, where the water had already fallen back below the stones, and the air smelt of wet pine and earth.

The mist was rising. The sun touched the top of the mountain. Out of habit, and without expecting anything, he looked down.

His reflection was back.

The man in the water was standing at the end of the village, beside the low stone wall and the old fountain that no longer worked. He had his small suitcase in one hand and his two cases of instruments at his feet. The yellow bus was coming round the bend. The man picked up his cases, climbed the steps and sat down by the window without looking back at the lake even once.

Tomorrow was Thursday.

## [c7] Blank Water

### [p26]
For a while he simply sat on the cold rock and let the idea arrive. He was going home. The lake had said so. Tomorrow at nine he would be on the yellow bus, and by the afternoon he would be sitting in Dr Hallam's office, explaining himself. It was what he was supposed to do. It was, he supposed, what he would have done anyway.

Except that he had not decided it.

He sat up straighter. The thought had come so quietly that he almost missed it, but now that it was there he could not stop looking at it. He had not decided to leave. Nor had he decided to stay. He had not decided anything at all since the night of the report. He had simply been carried, by the storm, by the email, by the timetable of a bus, towards whatever came next.

And when, exactly, had the lake shown him nothing?

He took out his notebook and turned back through the pages, his fingers stiff with cold. Observation seven: no anomaly. That was the morning he had stood on the shore and decided, firmly, to do the opposite of whatever he saw. Then the morning after the report: nothing. And every morning since then, nothing, until a storm and an email had put him back on the road he had been walking all his life.

Constantin, who had sat up all night and made up his mind, had seen only sky. Nico, who decided everything at breakfast, had never seen anything at all.

Tomás stood up so fast that his knees cracked. Then he ran, for the first time in years, all the way along the shore to the last house in the village.

### [p27]
Oriana was already awake, as if she had been expecting him. She said nothing as he took the books down from the shelves and opened his laptop on her kitchen table. She simply put a cup of coffee by his elbow and sat down to wait.

This time he did not look at the reflections that had come true. He looked at the others, the three thousand five hundred and eighty-eight that had not. He read the comments he had ignored, one after another. Changed her mind. Decided not to. Said no to his father at last. Sat by the water all night and chose. Thought it over and went the other way. Not every entry said so, but most of them did, in one form or another. Something had happened between the reflection and the next morning. Somebody had stopped, looked at the road they were on, and turned.

Then he went to the blank mornings, the two hundred and twelve failed observations he had put aside. Beside almost every one of them, the ledger recorded a decision made the night before. A proposal of marriage. A long letter to a brother. A girl who had decided to go to the city to study, and went.

He sat back.

"It doesn't show the future," he said slowly. "It doesn't show what you want, either. It shows where you'll end up if you don't choose. If you just let things happen. The road you're already walking." He looked at the screen. "Sixty-one per cent isn't a measure of the lake. It's a measure of us. It's how often people drift."

"And the blank water?"

"That's what you see when you've decided. There's nothing left to show you. The lake can't predict a choice. It can only predict the current."

Oriana nodded slowly. "It took my grandmother forty years to see that."

### [p28]
"She told my mother, and my mother told me," Oriana said. "I haven't seen my own face in that water for thirty-one years. Not since the night I decided to keep these books. Every morning I look, and every morning there's only sky. It's very restful."

"Why didn't you tell me? On the first day?"

"Would you have believed me?"

He thought about it honestly. "No."

"Neither would I, at your age. Had I told you, you would have written it down as a charming local story and gone home a week early." She smiled. "Some numbers you have to count yourself."

He walked back along the shore slowly, with the sun now fully up and the whole valley shining after the rain. At the top of the hill, where the signal lived, he stopped and wrote a message to Dr Hallam. He wrote it once, without changing a word.

I will not be on the bus on Thursday. My report recommends a full season of observation before any decision is taken, and I intend to carry it out myself. I would rather do it with the support of the office, but if necessary I will do it without, on unpaid leave. I will stay until the spring.

He read it, sent it, and felt the decision settle in him like a stone settling on the bottom of a lake: not heavy, simply there, and his own.

Then he went down to the water's edge in front of Petra's guesthouse, where Nico was already drawing a new volcano on the stones, and he looked down.

In the lake at Ardal, Tomás saw only the sky, and for the first time in his life he knew exactly what he would do tomorrow.
