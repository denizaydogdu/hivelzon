## [c1] The Same Chair

### [p01]
Noah has played second violin for three years, and he wants more.

He wrote that sentence on the first page of a new notebook on a cold Saturday in March. He wrote it about himself, like a line from a book, because it was easier than writing "I".

Then he looked at it for a long time.

It was true. Noah had joined the city youth orchestra when he was twelve. Since then, he had sat in the same chair every Saturday morning: the third chair on the left, in the second violin section. Behind him, the cellos. In front of him, the first violins, who always got the beautiful tunes.

The second violins played the other notes. The quiet ones under the music. The notes that nobody ever sang on the way home.

Noah loved the orchestra. He loved the smell of the old hall and the sound of forty instruments waking up together. But he was fifteen now, and he was tired of the same chair.

### [p02]
That morning, Ms Adeyemi stood at the front of the hall and waited for silence. She was the conductor, a tall woman with grey hair and bright orange glasses. She never shouted. She only waited, and somehow that was worse.

"I have some exciting news," she said. "This summer, our concert will finish with a new piece. It's called Evening Song, and it has a solo for one violin."

Forty young musicians started talking at once.

"The audition is in three weeks," Ms Adeyemi said. "Anyone can try. First violin or second."

Noah felt his heart jump. Anyone. Even the third chair on the left.

On the bus home, he wrote in his notebook again. If I practise every day, I will get the solo. Then he added a second line, smaller. If I don't get nervous.

That was the problem. Noah could play well in his bedroom. On a stage, his hands started to shake.

### [p03]
The next Saturday, there was a new girl in the hall.

She was standing near the door with a violin case in her arms, as if she wanted to protect it. She had short black hair and a serious face, and she was wearing a green coat that was too big for her.

"This is Hana," said Ms Adeyemi. "She has just moved here from another city. She'll sit with the second violins for now."

Hana sat down in the empty chair next to Noah. She didn't say hello. She just opened her case and started to tune her violin.

Then Ms Adeyemi asked her to play something alone, so that everyone could hear her.

Hana stood up. She closed her eyes. And she played.

Noah had never heard anything like it. The sound was warm and clear, and it seemed to fill every corner of the old hall. When she stopped, nobody spoke. Even the cellos were silent.

### [p04]
"Well," said Ms Adeyemi quietly. "Thank you, Hana."

During the break, everyone wanted to talk to the new girl. Ruby, who was the leader of the first violins, asked her where she had studied. A boy from the cellos asked if she had ever played on the radio. Hana answered with one or two words and looked at the floor.

Noah stayed in his chair. He didn't want to talk to her. He felt something heavy and unpleasant in his chest, and he didn't like it.

She is better than me, he thought. She is better than everyone here. She'll get the solo, and I'll stay in the same chair for another three years.

On the bus, he didn't write anything in his notebook. He just looked out of the window at the grey streets and felt disappointed with the whole world.

He was also a little ashamed. Hana hadn't done anything wrong. She had only played well.

### [p05]
After that, Noah started arriving early so that he could practise alone in a small room behind the hall. It was the quietest place in the building.

On the third Saturday, he was walking towards that room when he heard a violin.

Someone was playing Evening Song. The music was slow and soft, and so beautiful that Noah stopped walking. He stood very still and listened.

It was Hana. He knew her sound already. But this was different from her playing in the hall. It was softer, freer, as if she was playing only for herself.

Noah moved closer. The floor under his foot made a small noise.

Suddenly the music stopped.

He knocked and opened the door. Hana was standing by the window with her violin under her arm. Her face was white.

"Sorry," said Noah. "I didn't mean to listen."

Hana didn't answer. She put her violin back in its case quickly, picked up her coat and walked past him without a word.

## [c2] The Audition

### [p06]
On the day of the audition, it was raining. Noah had slept badly, and his hands were cold.

Twelve students were waiting outside the small room. Ms Adeyemi was inside, sitting at a table with a pencil and a cup of tea. They had to go in one by one.

Hana was sitting at the end of the row. She was holding her violin case so tightly that her fingers were pale. Noah was surprised. She looked more frightened than anyone.

Ruby went first. She came out smiling. Then a girl from the first violins went in, and then a boy who played very fast and very loudly.

While Noah was waiting, he tried to remember something his grandpa used to say. Grandpa had played the piano in cafés when he was young. "Don't play for the people who are judging you," he used to tell Noah. "Play for the one person in the room who needs the music."

Then a door opened, and somebody said his name.

### [p07]
Noah's hands shook when he lifted his violin. For a moment, he thought he might not be able to start.

Then he looked at Ms Adeyemi's tea and imagined that the cup needed the music. It was a strange idea, but it helped.

He played. It wasn't perfect. His bow shook a little on the long notes, and he played one note too early. But the tune was there, and in some places it was even beautiful.

When he finished, Ms Adeyemi wrote something down. Then she looked at him over her orange glasses.

"You listen, Noah," she said. "Do you know that? You've always listened better than anyone in this orchestra."

Noah didn't know what to say. It wasn't the kind of thing people said after a solo.

When he came out, the chair at the end of the row was empty.

"Where's Hana?" he asked Ruby.

"She left," said Ruby. "She just stood up and walked out. She didn't even go in."

### [p08]
The next Saturday, Ms Adeyemi put a list on the wall.

Noah read it three times. Then he read it again, because he didn't believe it.

Solo: Hana.

"But she didn't even play!" said the boy from the cellos.

"Ms Adeyemi has heard her play," said Ruby. "We all have."

Noah said nothing. He was disappointed, of course. But he was more confused than disappointed. Why had Ms Adeyemi given the solo to someone who had run away from the audition?

During the practice, Hana sat next to him, as always. Her music for Evening Song was open on the stand that they shared. Noah noticed that it was covered in small pencil notes.

That was normal. All musicians wrote on their music. But these notes were in two colours. Some were in blue pencil, in tidy, round writing. Others were in green, smaller and quicker.

Noah looked at them for a moment. Two people had written on the same piece of music. Who was the second one?

### [p09]
After the practice, Noah put his violin away and was ready to leave. He wanted to get out of the hall as fast as possible.

"Noah."

He turned. Hana was standing behind him. It was the first time that she had said his name.

"Can you stay for a bit?" she asked. "After everyone goes. Please."

Noah was so surprised that he only nodded.

The hall emptied slowly. Ruby waved from the door. The cellos were the last to leave, as always, because their cases were the biggest.

Finally they were alone. The rain was still falling against the high windows.

Hana sat down on the edge of the stage. She didn't look at him. She was turning her pencil, a green one, round and round in her fingers.

"I need to ask you something," she said. "But you might think it's stupid."

"I won't," said Noah.

Hana took a long breath. "Have you ever been so afraid of something that you couldn't move?"

## [c3] Pigeons and Little Kids

### [p10]
"Yes," said Noah. "Every time I play on a stage."

Hana looked up at him. "Really? But today you played. I heard you through the door before I left. You were shaking, but you played."

"I played badly."

"You played," she said again, as if that was the only important thing.

Then she told him. In her old city, she had played a solo at a big concert when she was eleven. In the middle of it, she had forgotten everything. Her mind had gone completely empty. She had stood on the stage in front of five hundred people, and she had not played a single note.

"Since then, I've never played alone in front of people," she said. "Not once. I can play when nobody is there. But when someone opens the door, my hands stop."

Noah remembered the small room and her white face. Now he understood.

"Then why did Ms Adeyemi give you the solo?" he asked.

Hana looked down at her green pencil. "She has a plan," she said. "I'm not sure it will work."

### [p11]
Noah thought about it on the bus home. He was still a little jealous of Hana. But it was difficult to be jealous of someone who was more frightened than you.

The next day, he sent her a message. If you can't play in front of people, start with people who don't care. Park, Sunday, two o'clock?

The park near Noah's house had a fountain, a lot of trees and more pigeons than anywhere else in the city. On Sunday afternoon, the pigeons were walking around the benches, looking for bread.

Hana arrived with her violin. She looked at the pigeons with a worried face.

"This is your first audience," said Noah. "They're not very interested in music. They're only interested in food."

For the first time since he had met her, Hana laughed.

She took out her violin and started to play. After a few notes, a pigeon walked closer to her. Hana stopped.

"It's watching me," she said.

"It's hoping you're a sandwich," said Noah. "Keep going."

### [p12]
The next Sunday, they found a bigger audience. Noah brought his little sister, Ella, who was seven, and two of her friends.

The little kids sat on the grass and ate crisps. They were not a quiet audience. They talked, they laughed and they asked a lot of questions.

"Why is the violin so small?"

"Can you play a song from a cartoon?"

"Why does your face look sad when you play?"

Hana played anyway. Sometimes she stopped in the middle, and her hands began to shake. When that happened, Noah did something simple. He just listened, very carefully, and then told her what he had heard.

"The beginning was lovely," he said. "You were breathing slowly. Then you looked at Ella, and you stopped breathing."

Hana thought about it. "I didn't know that."

"I noticed it because I've listened to the people around me for three years," said Noah. "That's what second violins do. We listen to everyone else."

Ella clapped. "Again!" she shouted.

### [p13]
On the third Sunday, Hana brought a piece of paper with music on it.

"I can't practise the solo in silence," she said. "It's too lonely. Will you play this under it? Just for practice."

Noah looked at the paper. The notes were written in green pencil, small and quick.

He played it slowly at first. It was a second line, a quiet one that moved under the tune. It was not difficult, but it was strangely familiar, as if he had played parts of it before.

Then they played together, Hana's tune on top and his line underneath.

Something happened. Hana stopped shaking. Her sound grew warm and free, like that day in the small room. The pigeons forgot about bread. Ella and her friends stopped eating crisps. Even an old man who was walking his dog stood still to listen.

When they finished, the old man clapped. Hana looked at him, and then at Noah, and she was smiling.

"That was amazing," said Noah.

"That was us," said Hana.

### [p14]
For the next three weeks, they practised in the park every Sunday. The audience grew. Ella brought more friends. A woman who sold ice cream near the fountain started to come closer when she heard them.

Noah began to look forward to Sundays more than Saturdays. He still wanted to play a solo one day. But he had stopped thinking about it every minute.

At the orchestra, Ms Adeyemi watched them sometimes with a small smile. Once she asked Noah, "Have you practised the green music?" Noah was so surprised that he dropped his bow. How did she know about the green music?

"Hana told me," Ms Adeyemi said, and walked away before he could ask anything else.

The concert was on a Friday in July. On the Wednesday before it, Noah's phone rang late in the evening. It was Hana.

Her voice was very small.

"I can't do it," she said. "Noah, I really can't. If I go out on that stage alone, I'll stand there and nothing will happen. Just like before."

## [c4] Duet

### [p15]
Noah didn't sleep much that night. He kept thinking about Hana's voice on the phone.

On Thursday, she didn't come to the last practice. Her chair next to him was empty, and her music wasn't on the stand. Ms Adeyemi didn't seem worried, which Noah found surprising and a little annoying.

"Is Hana going to play tomorrow?" he asked after the practice.

Ms Adeyemi was putting her papers into her bag. "I hope so," she said. "But that may depend on you."

"On me? What do you mean?"

"Bring your violin tomorrow," she said. "And bring your best listening."

That was all. She closed her bag and left.

On Friday evening, the old hall looked different. There were lights everywhere and a lot of chairs for the audience. Noah's parents were sitting in the third row with Ella. She was wearing her best dress and holding a drawing of a pigeon for Hana.

Hana was standing near the stage door in a long dark blue dress. She looked as pale as paper.

### [p16]
The concert went well. The orchestra played three pieces, and the audience clapped loudly after each one. Noah played his second violin part carefully, as he had done for three years.

Then it was time for Evening Song.

Hana walked out to the front of the stage. Her violin was shaking in her hand. Noah could see it from his chair.

Ms Adeyemi turned to the audience.

"Our last piece tonight is new," she said. "It was written for one violin. But a few weeks ago, our soloist came to me with an idea. She had listened to the second violins in our practices, and she had heard something beautiful there. So, with my permission, she changed the piece."

She turned and looked straight at the third chair on the left.

"Evening Song is now a duet," said Ms Adeyemi. "Noah, would you come to the front, please?"

The hall went very quiet. Noah didn't move. For a moment, he thought he might be dreaming.

### [p17]
Noah stood up. His legs felt strange. He walked to the front and stood next to Hana, and she put a piece of music on the stand between them.

It was the green music from the park.

Now he understood everything. The two colours of pencil. The blue was Ms Adeyemi's writing, and the green was Hana's. The quiet line that had seemed so familiar was built from his own part, the second violin part that he had played every Saturday for three years. Hana had listened to it, week after week, and had made it into a song.

"I couldn't do it alone," Hana whispered. "I never wanted to do it alone. I'm sorry we didn't tell you. Ms Adeyemi said you would worry for weeks."

She was right, Noah thought. He would have worried.

"Are you ready?" asked Hana.

He wasn't. But he lifted his violin anyway, and he listened to her breathing, and when she breathed in, he did too.

### [p18]
They began.

Hana's tune rose above the hall, warm and clear. Noah's line moved quietly underneath it, holding it up the way a hand holds a bird. When her sound shook a little, his stayed steady. When he played a note too early, she waited for him. They didn't look at the audience. They listened to each other.

It was the shortest five minutes of Noah's life.

When the last note finished, there was a long silence. Then the applause came, so loud that the old windows seemed to shake. Ella was standing on her chair, waving her pigeon drawing.

Later that night, at home, Noah opened his notebook and read the first page again. Noah has played second violin for three years, and he wants more.

He picked up a pen. He didn't cross anything out. He only added one line underneath.

Second doesn't mean less. Sometimes it means the other half of the song.
