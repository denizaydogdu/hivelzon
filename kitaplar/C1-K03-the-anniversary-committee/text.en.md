## [c1] The Meeting

### [p01]
Gwen joined the anniversary committee by accident: she was the only person who didn't leave the room fast enough.

In fairness to her, nobody had warned her. The email had invited "all interested colleagues" to "a short, informal conversation about the future", and Gwen, who had worked at Brindle Umbrellas for eleven years, had assumed that the future meant the budget. The future usually meant the budget. She had brought a pen, a notebook and three printed spreadsheets, each of them stapled in the top left-hand corner, the way spreadsheets ought to be.

It was only when Howard Brindle, the managing director, stood up at the front of the boardroom and said the word "celebration" that she realised her mistake. Not until he said the word "volunteers" did everyone else realise theirs.

What followed was, in its way, rather beautiful. Twenty-two people remembered urgent phone calls at precisely the same moment. Chairs were pushed back with the soft efficiency of a well-rehearsed fire drill. A man from sales left so quickly that his coffee was still swinging in its cup when the door closed behind him.

Gwen, meanwhile, was writing the word "celebration" in her notebook and underlining it twice, because underlining was what she did when she was alarmed. By the time she looked up, the room appeared to contain nobody but Howard, a plate of biscuits and herself.

"Gwen!" said Howard, as if she had arrived with a present. "Wonderful."

Through the window behind him, she could see the large sign on the factory roof. It said what it had said for as long as anyone could remember: that Brindle Umbrellas had been keeping the town of Wexley Bridge dry since 1976. Nobody, at that point, had thought to check.

### [p02]
It should be explained that Gwen Holloway did not dislike celebrations. She simply preferred them to happen to other people.

She was thirty-eight years old, she lived alone in a tidy flat above a dry cleaner's, and she had one ambition, which she had never said out loud. In the autumn, Derek Pole, the head of finance, would retire after thirty-one years, and somebody would replace him. Gwen intended to be that somebody. Her strategy was simple. She would be so reliable, so accurate and so completely unremarkable that when the moment came, the job would slide into her hands like a coat falling off the back of a chair.

In eleven years, Gwen had noticed that the people who got promoted at Brindle Umbrellas were rarely the people with big ideas. Big ideas, in her experience, cost money, and keeping money was what the finance department was for. The best accountants, she believed, were like good umbrellas: nobody thought about them until they were missing.

So she arrived at eight, left at half past five and ate her sandwiches at her desk. Never once had she spoken at the summer party. She kept a plant on her desk that needed so little attention that it might well have been plastic; she had never checked.

Her colleagues liked her, in the vague way that people like weather that is neither good nor bad. If asked, most of them would have said that Gwen was "very sound", and then, after a pause, that they weren't entirely sure what she did. This suited her perfectly.

Which is why, sitting alone in the boardroom while the managing director beamed at her, Gwen felt very much as a mouse must feel on discovering that the cheese is attached to something.

### [p03]
"Sit, sit," said Howard, although she had not stood up. He pulled out the chair next to hers and turned it round so that he could lean on its back, a habit he believed made him look approachable.

Howard Brindle was a large, cheerful man with silver hair and the permanent expression of someone about to tell you a story you have already heard. He was the grandson of the founder, Albert Brindle, whose portrait hung in reception: a small, fierce man holding a closed umbrella as though it were a sword. Howard loved the company the way some people love an old dog, loudly and without much attention to detail.

"Fifty years, Gwen," he said. "Half a century. My grandfather started this firm with two sewing machines and a borrowed van. And do you know what I always say?"

Gwen did know. Everybody knew.

"The firm and I are practically twins," said Howard. "Born in the same week! Grandad used to say he could never remember which of us kept him awake more."

It was at this point that the door opened and Clive Marsh came back in for his phone. Clive was the head of marketing, a thin, anxious man in very expensive glasses, and he stopped in the doorway like a burglar caught in a torch beam.

"Clive! Excellent. Sit down."

Clive sat down, with the face of a man who had considered faking a phone call and decided it was too late.

Then a voice from the corner said, "Is this the party thing?"

Jasper Brindle, Howard's nephew, had not left the room at all. He had been sitting behind the large plant by the window the whole time, eating biscuits. He had joined the company in January with the job title "Innovation Lead" and no duties that anyone had been able to identify. He was twenty-four, wore trainers to board meetings and described almost everything as either "huge" or "a vibe".

### [p04]
The last to arrive was Nell Okafor, who was nineteen, three weeks into a six-month placement and had been told by her supervisor in human resources to "get involved in things". She had taken this advice literally, as she took most advice. She was carrying a notebook that was already open, and she sat down, uncapped a pen and wrote the date at the top of a clean page, underlining it once.

Gwen, despite herself, approved of the underlining.

"Right," said Howard, rubbing his hands together. "This is the team. I can feel it. Now, what we need first of all is a chair."

There followed the kind of silence that occurs in a lift when someone has done something regrettable.

"Going forward," said Clive carefully, "I think it's essential that the chair be someone with real bandwidth. Someone organised. Someone who," and here he glanced at the three stapled spreadsheets on the table, "really owns the detail."

"I'd rather not, if it's all the same to everyone," said Gwen. "It's the end of the financial year. I'm supposed to be closing the accounts."

"Closing the accounts!" said Howard, delighted, as if she had said she was supposed to be climbing a mountain. "You see? That's exactly the attitude."

"It isn't an attitude. It's a deadline."

"Having seen your spreadsheets, Gwen, I honestly can't think of anyone better. Everything in its little box. Everything adding up." He looked round at the others. "Wouldn't you say?"

"Massively," said Jasper.

"I'd have to check the minutes," said Nell, "but I think so."

There were no minutes. The meeting was eleven minutes old.

Gwen looked at the door. It was eight steps away. She had counted them, the way she counted everything, and she knew with perfect certainty that she would never reach it.

### [p05]
She tried one last time. "Wouldn't it make more sense," she said, "for the chair to come from marketing? Clive has so much experience with," she searched for the word, "events."

Clive looked at her as if she had handed him a live wasp.

"I'd love to," he said. "Genuinely. But I'll be completely focused on the anniversary logo, and I think that piece of work deserves my full attention. We don't want to overload the logo."

"Shall we just vote?" said Jasper. "Voting is huge right now."

Howard thought a vote was a marvellous idea. Gwen did not get the chance to say what she thought about it, because Jasper said, "All those in favour of Gwen," and three hands went up at once. Howard's went up highest.

It was then that Gwen made the mistake that would shape the next seven weeks of her life. She raised her own hand, intending to ask whether a vote could be valid without a proper motion. Nell, who was writing everything down, looked round at the four raised hands and smiled.

"So that's unanimous," she said.

"No, I was only going to ask…"

"Unanimous!" said Howard, and began to clap.

Gwen lowered her hand slowly. In eleven years at Brindle Umbrellas, she had never been elected to anything, never been applauded and never been looked at by the managing director with quite so much affection. She found that she did not care for any of it.

"Nice one," said Jasper. "Madam Chair."

Nell wrote that down too. Gwen watched her do it, the way one watches a parking ticket being placed on one's windscreen.

Somewhere above them, the drizzle had started again, tapping softly on the roof, on the sign and on the date that nobody had ever checked.

The vote was unanimous. Gwen was the chair.

## [c2] Subcommittees

### [p06]
The first official meeting of the Fiftieth Anniversary Committee took place the following Monday in Meeting Room Three, which had no windows and a clock that ran four minutes fast. Gwen had chosen it deliberately. She hoped that the absence of daylight would encourage people to leave.

She had prepared an agenda. It had five items, a column for owners and a column for deadlines, and it fitted on a single page. Not only had she numbered every item, but she had also estimated how long each one would take. The total was forty minutes.

Item one was the logo. It took an hour and ten minutes.

The problem, as Clive explained with the help of a slideshow, was the umbrella. The company logo had always shown a closed umbrella, standing upright like a soldier. For the anniversary, Clive wanted it open.

"Open says celebration," he said. "Open says we're here, we're proud, we're," and he made a wide gesture with both hands, "open."

"Open also says it's raining," said Gwen.

"Which is arguably on brand," said Jasper.

Clive had prepared fourteen versions. In some, the umbrella was tilted at thirty degrees, which he described as "playful". In others, it was tilted at forty-five degrees, which he described as "playful but grounded". One version showed the umbrella upside down and full of confetti. Nobody asked what that one said.

"I think we're nearly there," said Clive, at minute fifty. "Let's circle back to this on Thursday."

They circled back to it on Thursday. They circled back to it the following Monday, and the Thursday after that. By the end of the second week, Gwen had come to understand that circling back was not, in fact, a way of returning to a subject. It was a way of making sure that you never left it.

### [p07]
Item two was the cake.

"Fifty layers," said Jasper. "One for every year. That would be massive."

"It would also be four metres tall," said Gwen, who had done the arithmetic on the back of the agenda while he was speaking. "The warehouse ceiling is three and a half."

"So we take a ceiling tile out."

There was a pause.

"I propose," said Clive, who had sensed an opportunity to avoid a decision, "that we set up a subcommittee to look at cake size. Take it offline. Let the experts drill down."

"Who are the experts?" said Gwen.

Nobody answered, which, she would learn, was how all subcommittees were born. The cake size subcommittee consisted of Jasper and Nell. It met for the first time that afternoon in the canteen and produced, by the end of the week, a document of nine pages, including a diagram.

It was Nell, to her credit, who did most of the work. She had turned out to be the kind of intern who keeps a spreadsheet of everybody's birthdays so that nobody is ever forgotten. She showed it to Gwen with quiet pride.

"Mr Brindle's birthday is the week before the party," she said, pointing at a cell she had coloured yellow. "He'll be forty-nine. Do you think the cake ought to mention it as well? Two celebrations in one?"

"I'd rather it didn't," said Gwen. "One celebration is already more than I can manage."

"Fair enough." Nell made a note. Then she said, rather more quietly, "Ms Holloway, can I ask you something? Is it normal for a company to spend this long talking about a logo?"

Gwen thought of all the meetings she had sat through in eleven years: the hours spent on fonts, on mission statements, on the precise shade of blue for the canteen walls.

"Yes," she said. "I'm afraid it is."

### [p08]
Item three was entertainment, and it was here that Jasper made his announcement.

"Indoor fireworks," he said.

He said it the way other people might announce a cure for the common cold. Then he sat back and waited for the room to appreciate what it had been given.

"No," said Gwen.

"You haven't even heard the concept."

"I needn't hear the concept. The concept is fire, indoors, in a building full of fabric. We make umbrellas, Jasper. The warehouse is essentially a very large box of things that burn."

"Okay," said Jasper, not in the least discouraged. "I hear you. I hear that. Let's park it."

Over the following weeks, Gwen would discover that ideas Jasper had parked had a habit of quietly driving themselves out again when nobody was looking.

By the middle of May, the committee had given birth to four subcommittees: cake size, logo, entertainment and napkins. Clive insisted on calling the logo subcommittee a "working group", because it sounded less likely to end. The napkin subcommittee was the only one that had reached a decision, and the decision was that a second napkin subcommittee would be needed to consider colour.

Meanwhile, the year-end accounts sat on Gwen's desk like an unpaid bill. She began arriving at half past seven and leaving at seven. She started eating her sandwiches while walking between meetings, which she had always regarded as the behaviour of someone whose life had got out of hand.

It was in the corridor, one evening, that Derek Pole stopped her. Derek was a mild, grey man with a cardigan for every day of the week. He was said to have smiled twice in thirty-one years, both times by accident.

"Gwen," he said. "I hear you've become very visible."

He said it with no expression whatsoever. Gwen heard it the way a mouse hears an owl.

### [p09]
It was Howard who changed everything, though he did not mean to. Howard rarely meant to do anything. Things simply happened in the space around him, like weather.

He came into Meeting Room Three on a Thursday afternoon, in the middle of a discussion about the second napkin subcommittee. He was carrying a mug of tea and wore the expression of a man who has just had an idea in the car.

"History," he said.

Everyone waited.

"It's all very well having cake and fireworks and so on…"

"There are no fireworks," said Gwen.

"But what is an anniversary, really? It's history. It's memory. It's who we are." He put his mug down on the table, directly on top of Clive's logo printouts. "I want an exhibition. Fifty years of Brindle Umbrellas. The first umbrella, the first sewing machines, the founding documents, Grandad's things, photographs. People will love it."

"That sounds lovely," said Nell, sincerely.

"And where are all those things now?" said Gwen, although she had a cold feeling that she already knew.

Howard waved a hand at the floor. "The archive. In the basement. It's all down there. Old Mr Penrose knows where everything is."

"Do we still have a Mr Penrose?" said Jasper, with real curiosity.

"He comes in on Tuesdays. Retired years ago, but nobody ever told him to stop, and frankly I'd rather nobody did." Howard picked up his mug, leaving a perfect brown ring on the anniversary logo. "Gwen, you'll handle it, won't you? You're so good with old paperwork."

Clive looked at the ring of tea on his printout with real grief.

"Of course," said Gwen. It was what she always said. It was, she was beginning to realise, the whole problem.

As far as anyone knew, nobody had opened the Brindle archive since the last century.

## [c3] The Basement

### [p10]
The basement of Brindle Umbrellas was reached by a narrow staircase behind the loading bay, through a door whose sign had long ago faded into a pale, polite rectangle.

Gwen went down the following Tuesday morning with Nell, a torch and a packet of tissues, because she suspected there would be dust. She was right. There was dust of a depth and quality that she had previously associated only with documentaries about lost cities.

There was also water. It is often said that every building has one great weakness. The weakness of Brindle Umbrellas, a company that had spent its entire existence protecting the people of Wexley Bridge from the rain, was that it could not keep its own basement dry. A thin, patient drip fell from a pipe in the ceiling and landed, with perfect regularity, in a bucket. Someone had placed the bucket there years ago. Someone had been emptying it ever since.

That someone was Mr Penrose.

He was sitting at a wooden desk under a single lamp, wearing a tweed jacket, a woollen hat and fingerless gloves, and reading what appeared to be an invoice for buttons with every sign of enjoyment. He was eighty-one, very small and very upright, and he looked up at them over the top of his glasses as though they were a late delivery.

"You'll be the committee," he said.

"How did you know?" said Nell.

"Nobody comes down here unless someone upstairs wants something. Last time it was the insurance people. Before that, a man looking for a ladder." He rose, carefully. "Ambrose Penrose. Archivist. Retired, technically. Tea?"

Gwen had been about to refuse. Instead, to her own surprise, she said yes.

### [p11]
Having made the tea, in a kettle that was older than both of his visitors, Mr Penrose explained the system.

There was, it turned out, a system, although it existed almost entirely inside Mr Penrose's head. The boxes on the left were arranged by decade. The boxes on the right were arranged by colour. The boxes in the middle were arranged, as far as Gwen could tell, by mood.

"Mr Albert never threw anything away," said Mr Penrose, "and he never put anything back. Those were his two principles. He'd keep a receipt for a box of buttons for twenty years and then lose the deeds to the factory in a drawer full of string." He sipped his tea. "He's believed to have used a cheque as a bookmark for three months once. The bank was very understanding."

"But you know where everything is," said Nell.

"I know where everything was," said Mr Penrose. "That's not quite the same thing. The flood of ninety-eight moved a few things about. So did the insurance people."

Gwen explained what Howard wanted: the first umbrella, the sewing machines, photographs and the founding documents. Mr Penrose listened with his head on one side, like a bird.

"The founding documents," he said. "Yes. They'd be in the Albert boxes. Somewhere."

"How many Albert boxes are there?"

He pointed. Along the back wall, beneath the dripping pipe, stood a row of cardboard boxes that went on for some time and then turned a corner.

"Forty-one," said Mr Penrose. "Forty-two, if you count the hatbox. I wouldn't count the hatbox, personally. Nobody's opened it since he passed away. It was his good hat, and I'd rather leave a man's good hat alone."

Somewhere upstairs, Gwen knew, the year-end accounts were waiting for her with the patience of a creditor.

"Right," she said, taking off her jacket. "Where do we start?"

### [p12]
They started on the left, with the oldest boxes.

It was slow work. Gwen had expected to be bored, and was disturbed to find that she was not. There was something deeply satisfying about it, about the way a whole company could be put back together from its paperwork, one receipt at a time. She found the first delivery notes, written in a hand so firm that the pen had gone through to the page below. She found a letter from a customer whose umbrella had turned inside out "in an ordinary wind, not even a strong one", and Albert's reply, which said only: "Madam, there is no such thing as an ordinary wind."

What she did not find was a year. Not one of the early papers had a year written on it anywhere. Days, yes. Months, often. Years, never.

"He didn't believe in them," said Mr Penrose, when she asked. "Years. Said they were for wine and pensions. He'd write Tuesday at the top of a letter and expect you to know which one."

"But somebody must have known when the company started," said Nell. "You can't just have a fiftieth anniversary by guessing."

"Oh, everybody knows," said Mr Penrose comfortably. "It's on the wall in reception."

Gwen thought of the brass plaque beside the front door, which announced "Established 1976" in confident capital letters, and which she had walked past perhaps five thousand times without reading.

"Where did the plaque come from?" she said.

"Ninety-four, I think. Same year as the first proper brochure. Marketing did it." Mr Penrose sniffed. "Nobody asked me. Nobody ever asks the archive. They'd rather guess than come down the stairs."

Gwen wrote this in her notebook. She did not underline it. Not yet.

### [p13]
It was Nell who found the first umbrella.

It was lying in a long cardboard tube at the bottom of box nineteen, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. When she unrolled it, the three of them stood in silence for a moment, as if someone had come into the room.

It was black cotton, with a plain wooden handle that had gone dark and smooth where a hand had held it. One of the ribs was slightly bent. It was not, by any modern standard, a good umbrella. It was simply the first.

"He sold it to himself," said Mr Penrose softly. "Paid full price, out of his own pocket, so the first sale would be a proper one. Then he carried it every day until it was too old to open." He touched the handle with one gloved finger. "Number One, he called it. I'd almost forgotten."

Rarely had Gwen felt anything about an object in a cardboard box beyond a mild interest in its value. She was surprised to discover a small, warm ache somewhere below her ribs, and decided, for the moment, to ignore it.

"Were you here at the beginning?" said Nell.

"Me? No. I came in the second spring, when there were six of us and a kettle. Dorothy was the one who was here from the very start. Dorothy Fairweather, on machine one. She started the very first week, just after young Howard was born. Lives by the sea now, in Seaborough. Sends me a card every year with a gull on it." He smiled. "She'd remember. She remembers everything, Dorothy. Whether she gets it right is another matter."

By four o'clock, they had found the sewing machines, two photographs of the opening day and a pair of enormous scissors. They had not found the founding documents.

"Box thirty-seven," said Mr Penrose suddenly, as if a voice had told him. "Try box thirty-seven."

### [p14]
Box thirty-seven contained, among other things, a broken clock, nine pairs of gloves that did not match and a biscuit tin decorated with faded roses. Inside the tin, folded twice and lying on a bed of old receipts, was the certificate.

It was printed on thick, cream-coloured paper, with a round official stamp in one corner and two signatures at the bottom: Albert's, firm and impatient, and that of a witness, which looked like a spider that had fallen into ink. Above the signatures, in neat old-fashioned type, the document stated that Brindle Umbrellas had been formed on the tenth day of June.

Gwen held it under the lamp. Only then did she see the problem.

Exactly where the year should have been, there was a ring. It was a perfect brown circle, slightly darker at the edge, the unmistakable mark of a cup of tea that had been put down on the paper and left there for some time. The ink underneath had dissolved into a soft, hopeless blur. The first two numbers could just be made out. The last two were gone.

"Ah," said Mr Penrose. "Mr Albert did like his tea."

"So does Mr Brindle," said Nell, and they all thought of Clive's logo.

"It doesn't matter," said Gwen, mostly to herself. "We know the year. It's on the plaque. It's on the sign. It's on everything."

She said it twice, the way people do when they are trying to convince a part of themselves that is not listening. Then she folded the certificate carefully and put it back in the tin.

The day and the month were perfectly clear. The year had disappeared into a neat brown circle, as if, long ago, someone had put down a cup and quietly rubbed out history.

## [c4] Escalation

### [p15]
At the next committee meeting, Gwen reported that the exhibition was going well. This was true. She did not mention the tea ring. This was also true, in the sense that she did not mention it.

It hardly mattered, because nobody was listening. Jasper had news.

"So," he said, leaning forward with his elbows on the table. "Two things. Both huge. First: I've booked a brass band."

"A brass band," said Gwen.

"The Wexley Bridge Silver Band. Twenty-six players. They do weddings, openings, the odd retirement, but mostly openings. They were free on the twelfth, which is honestly a sign."

"Second thing," said Jasper. "And this is the big one." He paused for effect. "An umbrella. Fifty metres across. One metre for every year. We put it up in the car park, the factory makes it, everybody sees it from the road. It's a statement. It's a vibe."

No sooner had Gwen opened her mouth to object than the door swung open and Howard came in, as if he had been waiting outside for his cue. Arguably, he had.

"Has he told you about the umbrella?" said Howard, glowing. "Isn't it the most Brindle thing you've ever heard?"

Gwen did a quick calculation. A circle fifty metres across had an area of almost two thousand square metres. That was a great deal of fabric, a great deal of steel and, if she was not mistaken, a planning application.

"It would cost," she began.

"Let's not get bogged down in numbers, Gwen," said Howard kindly. Nobody had ever said this sentence to her before, and she found that she resented it more deeply than she could explain. "It's the fiftieth. We only turn fifty once."

Clive raised his hand. "Will the logo be on the umbrella?"

"Clive," said Howard warmly, "the umbrella will be the logo."

Clive looked as if he might cry.

### [p16]
It was Nell's idea to invite every former employee, and Howard loved it so much that he announced it in the staff newsletter before anyone had worked out how it might be done.

It was done, in the end, by Gwen. Having spent three evenings in the basement going through the personnel files, she had a list of three hundred and twelve names and addresses, some of them very old, some of them almost certainly out of date, and one of them, as far as she could tell, belonging to a dog that had been employed in 1989 as a security measure.

She wrote the letters herself. The official version, which Howard approved, began: "It is with the greatest pleasure that Brindle Umbrellas invites you, as a valued member of our extended family, to join us in celebrating fifty remarkable years." The version in Gwen's head, which nobody approved, began: "You may not remember us, and frankly we have mislaid most of you, but there will be cake."

She posted them on a Wednesday. On Thursday, in the office kitchen, she found herself standing next to Clive at the kettle, and decided, for reasons she could not afterwards explain, to tell him about the certificate.

"The year's covered by a tea stain," she said. "The founding year. You can't read it."

Clive nodded slowly, the way he nodded at slides. "Interesting," he said. "Really interesting. Let's take that offline."

"We are offline, Clive. We're in the kitchen."

"Then let's circle back to it," said Clive, picking up his mug and backing away, "once the logo's been signed off."

Gwen watched him go. It was high time, she thought, that somebody at Brindle Umbrellas actually finished a conversation. It was clearly not going to be Clive.

### [p17]
The indoor fireworks returned the following week, as Gwen had known they would. This time they were called "cold sparks".

"They're not fireworks," Jasper explained patiently. "They're firework-adjacent. Totally different category. It's basically light that happens to be quite fast."

The factory's safety officer, a calm woman who had spent twenty years being asked whether things were really dangerous, was invited to the meeting. She listened to Jasper's presentation in silence and then said, "No." It would seem that the cold sparks were finished. Gwen did not believe it for a moment. She had seen what happened to things Jasper parked.

Meanwhile, the twelfth of June came closer, the way a train does when you are standing on the platform with the wrong ticket.

On Friday afternoon, Derek Pole stopped beside her desk. He was wearing his Friday cardigan, which was brown.

"Just so you know," he said, "the interviews for my job have been put back until after the anniversary. It's understood that Mr Brindle wants to make the decision himself." He paused. "He's been asking about you."

"Asking what?"

"Whether you're the person who's organising the umbrella."

Derek walked away. Gwen sat very still.

For eleven years she had been careful, quiet and correct. She had built her whole career on the principle that the safest place to be was just outside the edge of everybody's attention. And now, through no fault of her own, she was standing in the very centre of it, holding a fifty-metre umbrella.

If she had picked up her pen a little faster on that first morning, she thought, she would now be sitting peacefully behind her plant, with the accounts finished and her reputation for invisibility intact. If she had read the email properly, a small voice inside her pointed out, she wouldn't be in this mess either.

She told the voice to be quiet. It was, on the whole, a very accurate voice, and she would rather it kept its accuracy to itself.

### [p18]
The replies to the invitations began to arrive in the second week of May, and to Gwen's considerable surprise, they were wonderful.

A man who had worked in dispatch for thirty years sent a long letter about Albert, who had once driven forty miles through a storm to deliver a single umbrella to a wedding, "because the bride's mother had ordered it, and an order was an order". A woman sent a photograph of herself at nineteen, laughing beside a sewing machine, with a note on the back that said simply: "Best job I ever had. Don't tell my husband." Someone else sent a pressed flower and no explanation at all.

Gwen read every one of them at her desk, at lunchtime, slowly. She did not tell anyone that she was doing this. She was not sure she could have explained it. It was as if the company, which she had always thought of as a set of accounts with a building attached, had turned out to have a pulse.

Nell made a display of the letters on a long board in the canteen. People stopped to read them while they waited for the microwave.

Then, on the Tuesday of the last week of May, a small blue envelope arrived, addressed in shaky handwriting to the committee. Gwen recognised the gull on the card before she had even opened it.

The handwriting inside was old but determined, the letters leaning forward as if into a strong wind.

"Dear Committee," it said. "How very kind of you to think of me. I shall be there with bells on, as long as my son can drive me. I still have my scissors. Love to Ambrose. Yours, Dorothy Fairweather."

And then, squeezed in underneath, as if added on the way to the post box: "But fifty already? I thought it was next year."

## [c5] Three Days

### [p19]
Gwen did nothing about the card for almost two weeks, which was, she would later admit, about thirteen days longer than she should have waited.

She did nothing because it was only a card, and Dorothy Fairweather was, by Mr Penrose's own account, someone who remembered everything without necessarily getting it right. She did nothing because the plaque, the sign, the website and every brochure since the last century all said the same thing. Above all, she did nothing because doing something would mean being noticed, and being noticed was precisely what she had spent eleven years avoiding.

On the Tuesday before the celebration, three days before the band, the cake and the umbrella, she gave in and rang the number on the card.

Dorothy answered on the second ring, as though she had been sitting by the phone for some years.

"Oh, the committee!" she said. "How lovely. Is it about the scissors? I can bring them, but I won't lend them."

"It isn't about the scissors," said Gwen. "It's about your card. You said you thought it was next year."

"Did I? Well, I do. I started on the Monday after Mr Albert signed his papers, and I was nineteen. Never in my life had I seen so much black cotton. And Mrs Brindle had just had the baby, young Howard, so everyone was walking about with no sleep and a silly smile." She laughed. "I'm sixty-eight now. Sixty-nine in August. So you see, it doesn't quite add up, does it? But my son says my memory's like a sieve, so I wouldn't put any money on it."

Gwen had already done the subtraction. She had done it twice, and then a third time on the back of her hand, where nobody could see.

Sixty-eight minus nineteen was forty-nine.

### [p20]
Gwen spent the rest of Tuesday trying to tell someone.

She started with Clive, because he was nearest. She found him in the print room, holding two identical sheets of paper up to the light.

"Clive," she said, "I think the company might be forty-nine."

"Can we not do this now?" said Clive, without looking round. "They've printed the umbrella logo at forty-five degrees. It was supposed to be thirty. Do you know what forty-five degrees says? It says panic."

She tried Jasper, who was in the car park watching six men from the factory unfold what looked like a small grey field.

"Fifty, forty-nine," said Jasper, shrugging. "Honestly, it's the same vibe."

She tried Howard. Howard, it emerged, was in back-to-back meetings until Friday, then at the celebration, then on holiday. His assistant offered her a slot in the third week of June.

So Gwen went back to her desk and wrote an email. She wrote it seven times. The first version said: "We have got the year wrong." The second said: "I believe we may have got the year wrong." By the seventh version, it said: "It would appear that there may be a small discrepancy in the historical record which, while arguably minor, the committee might wish to consider at some point going forward."

She read it back and was horrified. Without noticing, she had learned to speak fluent meeting. Worse, it had taken her only six weeks.

She sent it anyway. Nobody replied. Clive sent an automatic message saying that he was "heads down on brand" and would "revert in due course".

That evening, alone in the office, Gwen realised that she needed something nobody could circle back from. She needed numbers. And there was only one place in the building where old numbers went to sleep.

### [p21]
The accounts of a company, Gwen had always believed, were the only completely honest thing about it. People exaggerated. Brochures lied with enthusiasm. Plaques simply repeated whatever they had been told. But a set of accounts had to begin on a particular day and end on a particular day, and somebody had to sign them.

She found the old annual accounts in the basement at ten o'clock that night, in a box on the right-hand side, filed under green. Mr Penrose had gone home, but he had left the lamp on and a note on the kettle that said "Help yourself", as though he had known she would come.

The first set of accounts was a thin typed booklet, with Albert's signature on the last page and, beneath it, the signature of an outside accountant whose name meant nothing to her. Albert, naturally, had not written a year anywhere. But accountants always do. It was, Gwen reflected, one of the many quiet virtues of her profession.

The booklet covered "the first period of trading, from the tenth of June to the thirty-first of May". And underneath, in clear type: "Year ended thirty-first of May, 1978."

She sat down on a box marked with a drawing of a raincoat and read it four times.

A first trading period that ended in May 1978 could only have begun in June 1977. Not 1976. There was no other way to read it. The company had not been founded fifty years ago. It had been founded forty-nine years ago, in the same week as Howard, exactly as Howard had been telling everyone for decades without ever once doing the maths.

What Gwen wanted, more than anything, was to put the booklet back in the box and say nothing at all. Had she been the kind of person who could do that, her life would have been considerably simpler. She was not. She was an accountant. And an accountant who finds a mistake and keeps quiet about it, she thought grimly, is not really an accountant at all. She is just a person with a calculator.

### [p22]
On Wednesday, two days before the celebration, she found out where the mistake had come from.

"The ninety-four brochure," said Mr Penrose, without looking up from his invoice, when she showed him the accounts. "I always wondered."

He went to a shelf, moved three boxes and a fern, and came back with a glossy brochure, slightly curled with damp. On the back, under a photograph of the factory, it said: "Established 1976."

"The printers sent the proof up to Mr Albert," said Mr Penrose. "He was very old by then, and he would not wear his glasses, on principle. He looked at it for about a second, signed it and said that nobody reads brochures anyway." He sighed. "He was wrong about that. Everybody read it. Marketing copied the date onto the plaque. Then onto the sign. Then onto the letters, the website, the calendars, the vans. After a few years, it was simply true. Things are, when they're written down often enough."

Gwen took the brochure upstairs to the final committee meeting. She laid it on the table, next to the accounts and the stained certificate, and explained everything, slowly and clearly, as though to a group of intelligent children.

There was a silence.

"Well," said Clive at last. "Mistakes were made." He said it the way people at Brindle Umbrellas always said it, as though mistakes were a kind of weather that simply arrived. "But I think, going forward, the priority has to be the logo."

"The band's already booked," said Jasper. "And they've learned a medley."

The meeting moved on to napkins. Only Nell stayed behind afterwards to help Gwen gather up the papers.

"So what are you going to do?" asked Nell.

"I don't know."

"Would you rather I didn't ask?"

"Yes," said Gwen. "But thank you for asking."

### [p23]
Howard had asked her, some weeks earlier, to give a short speech on the night. "Five minutes, nothing fancy," he had said. "Just thank everyone. You're the chair, after all." At the time, it had seemed like the worst thing that could possibly happen. She now understood that she had simply lacked imagination.

On Thursday evening, the night before the celebration, Gwen sat at her kitchen table with two pieces of paper in front of her.

The first was a speech that thanked everyone, praised the band, admired the umbrella and said nothing whatsoever about dates. It was safe. It was polite. It was exactly the kind of speech that a future head of finance ought to give. It made her feel faintly unwell.

The second speech told the truth. It was shorter.

She read them both several times. She made a cup of tea and was careful not to put it down on either of them. Outside, a light rain began to fall, which seemed to her, under the circumstances, a little unnecessary.

What she needed was proof, real proof, the kind that not even Howard could laugh away. The accounts were strong. Dorothy's memory was charming but, by her own admission, a sieve. The certificate had a tea ring where its most important fact should have been. And she could already hear Clive saying, with great sincerity, that the accounts were "just one data point".

At a quarter past nine, her phone rang. It was a number she did not recognise, from a landline, which was in itself a kind of message.

"Ms Holloway?" said a careful, old-fashioned voice. "Ambrose Penrose. I do apologise for ringing so late."

"Not at all. Is everything all right?"

"I've been thinking about what you said. About proof. So this evening I did something I'd rather not have done." He paused. "I opened the hatbox."

Gwen sat up. "And?"

"Well," said Mr Penrose, "it wasn't his good hat."

## [c6] The Speech

### [p24]
It was his diary.

It was small, black and very battered, with the year printed in faded gold on the cover, because Albert had bought it from a shop that put the year on for him. It was, as far as anyone knew, the only year he had ever owned.

Gwen arrived at the basement at half past seven on Friday morning, before anyone else was in the building, and found Mr Penrose already at his desk with the diary open under the lamp, as if it were a patient.

"I've marked the page," he said. "You needn't have hurried, mind you. It's waited in that hatbox for thirty years; it could easily have waited until nine."

Most of the entries were short. Albert had used his diary the way other people use a shopping list. "Tuesday. Rain. Bought thread." "Thursday. Van broke. Fixed van. Van broke." But on one page in June, the handwriting grew larger and more excited, as though the pen had been running to keep up.

"Tuesday. Margaret has had the boy. Howard. Seven pounds and very loud. Both well."

And three days later: "Friday. Signed the papers at last. Brindle Umbrellas is real. Rained all day. Good sign. The boy and the firm both exhausting already."

Gwen looked at the gold year on the cover. Then she took out her phone and checked something she had been wondering about since Tuesday. In 1976, the tenth of June had been a Thursday. In 1977, it had been a Friday.

"So that's that," she said quietly.

"That's that," agreed Mr Penrose. "Not only is it forty-nine, Ms Holloway, it has never once been fifty. Not for a single day." He closed the diary gently. "What will you do?"

"I'm supposed to give a speech this evening."

"Would you rather someone else gave it?"

"Very much," said Gwen. "But I don't think anyone else is going to."

Mr Penrose nodded, as if this were the answer he had been hoping for. Then he wrapped the diary in a clean handkerchief and handed it to her carefully, like a man handing over the last umbrella in a storm.

### [p25]
By seven o'clock that evening, the warehouse had been transformed.

Strings of warm lights hung from the beams. The exhibition ran the length of one wall: the old sewing machines, the photographs, the scissors, the letters from former employees and, in a glass case at the centre, Number One, with its slightly bent rib, looking modest and a little surprised to be there. The cake, after seven weeks of subcommittee work, had three layers and was exactly one metre tall. The napkins were burgundy. Nobody could remember why.

The Wexley Bridge Silver Band played by the doors, all twenty-six of them, with the shining enthusiasm of people who have learned a medley specially. In place of indoor fireworks, there was a bubble machine, which the safety officer had approved on the grounds that it did not, as far as anyone knew, burn.

And outside, it rained.

It had started at six, steadily, the way it only rains in Wexley Bridge in June, and the guests who arrived from the station hurried across the car park with their coats over their heads. And there, waiting for them, was Jasper's umbrella. All fifty metres of it stood open over the car park like a great grey sky of its own, and underneath it, three hundred people stood dry and astonished, with their faces turned up. For the first and possibly the only time in its history, the committee had made a decision that turned out to be right.

"Told you," said Jasper, to nobody in particular. "Statement."

Dorothy Fairweather arrived at half past seven, in a purple coat, on the arm of her son, carrying a pair of enormous scissors in a velvet case. She walked straight to the glass case, looked at Number One for a long moment and said, "He never did fix that rib." Then she found Mr Penrose, and the two of them stood together by the sewing machines, talking quickly and laughing, as if fifty years, or forty-nine, were no time at all.

At a quarter past eight, Howard tapped a glass and called for the chair of the committee.

### [p26]
Gwen walked to the microphone. It was eight steps from where she had been standing. She counted them.

The warehouse grew quiet. Three hundred faces turned towards her, and she was aware of Clive near the cake, Jasper by the bubble machine, Nell at the front holding her notebook and Derek Pole at the back, in what was presumably his formal cardigan.

"Good evening," she said. "On behalf of the committee, it is my privilege to welcome you all to this celebration, and to thank the many colleagues whose dedication has made tonight possible." The words were smooth and correct and meant nothing, and she heard them slide off the room like rain off a good umbrella.

She folded the first speech and put it away.

"I'd also like to tell you something," she said, "that I found in the basement."

She told them about the tea ring, and the accounts, and the brochure that Albert had signed without his glasses. She told them about Dorothy, who had started work a few days after young Howard was born and had been sixty-eight for some months. She held up the diary and read the two entries aloud: the loud baby and the rainy Friday.

"Mistakes were made," she said. "In this building, we usually say that when we'd rather not say who made them. In this case, I can tell you exactly who made it: a printer in 1994, and a founder who refused to wear his glasses. And then all of us, for more than thirty years, because nobody thought to check." She took a breath. "It would appear that we are a little early. Brindle Umbrellas is forty-nine years old tonight. It is exactly as old as its managing director, which, as he has been telling us for decades, is how it was always supposed to be."

The silence that followed was long enough for Gwen to imagine, in some detail, the rest of her career. She saw Derek's job going to someone with fewer opinions. She saw herself eating sandwiches alone in Meeting Room Three for the next twenty years.

Then, from the middle of the room, came a sound like a small explosion. It was Howard, laughing.

### [p27]
It was the biggest laugh anyone at Brindle Umbrellas had ever heard. It went on for some time. It shook the bubbles. At one point, Howard had to hold on to the cake table.

"Forty-nine!" he said, wiping his eyes as he made his way to the microphone. "The same week as me! I've been saying it for twenty years, and not one of us did the maths. Not even me! If you hadn't gone down to that basement, Gwen, we'd all be celebrating the wrong birthday right now! Grandad would have loved this." He took the microphone from Gwen, patted her on the shoulder as if she had scored a goal, and turned to the room. "Ladies and gentlemen, there has been a slight change to the programme. Welcome, every one of you, to the Fiftieth Anniversary Rehearsal!"

The band, who had not been told about any of this, launched bravely into the medley. Three hundred people cheered. Dorothy raised her scissors. Mr Penrose, at the back, took off his woollen hat for the first time in living memory.

Later, when the cake had been cut and Clive had already begun, very quietly, to redesign the logo with a forty-nine on it, Howard found Gwen by the exhibition.

"Next year," he said, "we do it properly. Same band, same umbrella, the real fiftieth. And there's only one person I'd trust to chair it." He beamed at her. "Wouldn't you agree?"

Gwen looked round for help. Clive had vanished towards the car park. Jasper was suddenly deep in conversation with the bubble machine. Even Nell, loyal Nell, had developed an urgent interest in the napkins. Within seconds, Gwen was standing alone with the managing director, exactly as she had been on the first morning.

She had not, once again, left the room fast enough.

On the subject of the promotion, Howard said nothing at all. Derek Pole, however, passed her on his way to the door, paused and said, "Very visible, Gwen." And then, for the third time in thirty-one years, and quite possibly not by accident, he smiled.

Gwen opened her notebook, wrote the word "fifty" at the top of a clean page and underlined it twice.
