## [c1] Trap Streets

### [p01]
The town of Merrow Cross appeared on every paper map printed since 1931, and on no satellite image ever taken.

Amira Haddad had been staring at it for eleven minutes, which, by the standards of the fourth floor, was a scandalous amount of time to spend on a place with no buildings in it. On her left screen was a scan of the oldest map in the file: a fold in the Corrin Hills, a thin road climbing out of the valley below, a dotted footpath, a river and, tucked into a bend of that river, a small cluster of black squares with a name beside it. On her right screen was the newest satellite image of the same valley. It showed trees. Nothing but trees: a long, unbroken carpet of fir and pine, so even in colour that it looked as if someone had combed it.

She zoomed in until the trees became green blocks and the green blocks became nothing in particular. Then she zoomed out again, as if the town might have been hiding at the edges.

"It's a trap street," said a voice behind her.

Gavin Pell, Head of Operations, had arrived at her shoulder with the silent efficiency of a man who had recently attended a course on walking around offices. He held a coffee in one hand and a tablet in the other, and he was looking at her screens the way people look at a puddle they intend to step over.

"Well," he corrected himself. "A trap town. Somebody made it up nearly a hundred years ago to catch copiers, and we've been copying it ever since. Confirm it, delete it, and then come and look at your new desk. It has a window."

What Amira needed, she reflected, was not a window. What she needed was one more week with mud on her boots.

"Is there anything else in the file?" she asked.

"Ninety-five years of paper saying one thing and every camera in orbit saying another," said Gavin. "I'd call that a verdict."

### [p02]
Mapmakers have always had a complicated relationship with the truth. A map is expensive to make and astonishingly cheap to copy, and for most of history the only way to prove that a rival had copied yours was to hide a small lie in it and wait. A street that led nowhere. A bend that the river had never made. A hamlet of three cottages in an empty field. If the same lie turned up on somebody else's map, the case, as lawyers like to say, was closed.

It is said that one nineteenth-century publisher invented an entire lake, and that his rivals reproduced it so faithfully that a fishing club was formed to visit it. Amira had never managed to find out whether the story was true, which, she suspected, was rather the point.

For eleven years she had been the person Gridwell sent when its computers disagreed with each other. Gridwell made the maps on several million phones, and its motto, painted across the wall in reception, was a sentence about connecting people to places that nobody in the building could quote correctly. Amira's job was less poetic. When a road on the map ran through a lake on the photograph, she drove there. When a bridge appeared in one dataset and not in another, she walked across it, or failed to. She measured, photographed, asked questions and wrote reports, and her title was Senior Field Verification Specialist, a phrase so long that it was arguably a small journey in itself.

From Monday she would be Head of Verification Standards. She would sit at a desk and write the rules that other people took outside. It was a promotion. Everybody said so, in the tone people use when they recommend a medicine.

Her father would have understood. He had arrived in Canada from Lebanon with a surveyor's instrument in a wooden box, very little English and no intention of sitting down, and in thirty years he never really did. He had taught her to read contour lines at the kitchen table during long winters, tracing the brown curves with a pencil until a flat sheet of paper became a hill she could almost feel under her feet.

"A map," he used to say, "is a promise that somebody went and looked."

### [p03]
The file on Merrow Cross was thin, and most of it was apologetic.

The town had first appeared on a sheet published in 1931 by Hollins and Vane, a firm of mapmakers that had been respected, then famous, then bought, and then quietly forgotten. Their survey of the Corrin Hills had been so good that every later map of the region was based on it, which meant that every later map had inherited Merrow Cross, like a family nose. Who exactly had invented it was not recorded; the trap was generally assumed to have been the work of some anonymous draughtsman in the Hollins and Vane drawing office, long since retired or forgotten. Gridwell had bought a licence for the old paper data in 2009, and the town had travelled from paper to screen without anyone asking it for identification.

Then, two years ago, the satellite team had started comparing names with photographs, and Merrow Cross had failed the comparison. Since then the record had collected fourteen notes. Amira read them in order, enjoying the slow decline of their confidence.

"Feature not observed in imagery," said the first. "Cloud cover, probably," said the second. "Feature not observed in imagery (second attempt)," said the third. By the tenth, the analysts had stopped pretending. "It would appear that the feature is a deliberate fabrication of the 1931 survey, purportedly inserted to detect unlicensed reproduction. Recommendation: removal of the feature on the grounds of persistent non-observation."

Amira admired that last sentence the way one admires a well-made wall. Not a single person in it had done anything. Things had been observed, or not observed; removals had been recommended; grounds had been found. Nobody had driven to the Corrin Hills and looked.

It was the contour lines, in the end, that bothered her. She enlarged the 1931 sheet until the old brown curves filled her screen. Everywhere else on the map, the lines were careful but general, the work of surveyors in a hurry. Around Merrow Cross they suddenly became precise. They described a small hanging valley tucked behind a ridge, a steep shoulder of hill that would hide it from the road below, and a gentle slope down to the bend in the river. Never had she seen a fake drawn with such care.

Whoever had invented this town, Amira thought, had walked every metre of it first.

### [p04]
At half past five, when the fourth floor was emptying in the way offices empty on a Friday, as if somebody had pulled out a plug, Theo Marsh appeared at the edge of her desk holding a postcard.

Theo was twenty-four, worked in Data Quality, and believed that every problem could be solved with a spreadsheet and a packet of crisps. He had been the one who flagged Merrow Cross for deletion. He also, Amira had noticed, had been the one who kept postponing it.

"This came up from the post room," he said. "They didn't know who to give it to. It's addressed to the people who make the maps."

"That's us," said Amira. "Allegedly."

"Look at the postmark."

She took the card. On the front was a painting, not a photograph: a narrow green valley, a silver river, a spire of smoke, and a scatter of small stone houses whose roofs were the soft, deep green of old moss. It was pretty in the slightly old-fashioned way of postcards sold in shops that also sell fudge. Somebody had clearly painted it with affection, and with a certain artistic licence, Amira decided, since nobody's roofs were really that colour.

She turned it over. The postmark was a neat black circle with two words around its edge and a date in the middle. The words were Merrow Cross. The date was four weeks ago.

Underneath, in small upright handwriting, the kind of lettering they used to teach in drawing offices, someone had written three short sentences. "We have received your notice. Thank you. Please leave us where you found us."

There was no signature.

"What notice?" asked Amira.

Theo had the decency to look embarrassed. "When a place is under review and the system can't find any addresses, it sends one letter to the place itself. To the occupier, sort of thing. It's a template. They always come back marked unknown."

"And this one?"

"This one," said Theo, "didn't come back. It wrote back."

## [c2] The Road

### [p05]
Amira left on Monday morning in a small grey hire car that smelt of pine air freshener and other people's holidays.

She was supposed to be at her new desk. Instead, she had sent Gavin a message on Sunday night explaining, in her most formal register, that the receipt of correspondence from a purportedly non-existent settlement constituted a material change in the evidence, and that a final site inspection was therefore required before the deletion could responsibly proceed. She had read it through twice before sending it and had been rather pleased with "responsibly proceed". Gavin's reply had arrived at seven the next morning. "Fine. Three days. Confirm and delete. Don't go native."

She drove north for four hours, then west, and then along roads that seemed to grow narrower out of embarrassment. The motorways gave way to dual carriageways, the dual carriageways to lanes, and the lanes to something that was arguably a road only because nobody had told it otherwise. Stone walls appeared on either side, then sheep, then hills folded one behind another like a crumpled blanket.

Having spent eleven years in places that other people called the middle of nowhere, Amira had developed a theory about them. The middle of nowhere, she believed, was always somebody's somewhere. The trick was to find out whose. Merrow Cross, if it existed at all, was not so much off the beaten track as off the track altogether.

On the passenger seat lay the postcard, a printout of the 1931 sheet and a flask of coffee that was already lukewarm. She had considered several explanations for the card during the drive. A local joker with a sense of humour and access to an old rubber stamp. A walker who had found the stamp in an antique shop. A student doing a project on maps and absurdity. Theo had suggested, with real enthusiasm, a ghost.

Each theory had a hole in it, and the holes were all the same shape. Somebody had received Gridwell's letter. Somebody had replied.

The Corrin Hills rose ahead of her in the late afternoon, brown and green and blue in layers, their tops lost in a soft grey cloud that looked as if it had been there since 1931 and saw no reason to leave.

### [p06]
The last village before the hills was called Callowford. It had a church tower, a closed pub, a bus shelter and a petrol station with a single pump and a small café attached to it, as if the two businesses had been unable to afford a building each.

The woman behind the counter was doing a crossword with a pencil she had clearly sharpened with a knife. She was about sixty, wore a cardigan the colour of tomato soup, and had the air of someone who had been asked for directions by every lost person in the county and had enjoyed nearly all of it.

"Just the petrol?" she asked.

"And a question, if you don't mind," said Amira. "I was wondering if you could tell me how to get to Merrow Cross."

The pencil stopped. The woman looked up, and for a moment Amira could not read her face at all. Then it relaxed into something like amusement.

"Merrow Cross," she repeated. "Now there's a name. You're not from the council, are you?"

"I make maps."

"Oh, well, that's worse," said the woman cheerfully. "Bryony," she added, by way of introduction, and pointed out of the window with her pencil. "Back up the road, past the church, and keep going till you think you've gone too far. Then go a bit further. It's second left after the bridge that isn't there."

Amira waited for the rest of the sentence. There was no rest of the sentence.

"The bridge that isn't there," she said.

"That's right."

"And how will I know when I've passed it?"

"You'll know," said Bryony, and went back to her crossword. Then, without looking up, she added, "They buy a lot of green paint, that lot."

"Which lot?"

"Whoever they are," said Bryony, and wrote a word into the crossword with great satisfaction.

### [p07]
Amira bought a sandwich she did not want, so that she would have a reason to sit in the café, and spent half an hour collecting what her reports would later call local testimony.

The testimony was generous, confident and entirely contradictory.

An elderly farmer by the window told her that Merrow Cross had been drowned under a reservoir in the fifties, church and all, and that on still days you could hear the bell under the water. When Amira pointed out that there was no reservoir in the Corrin Hills, he said that this only proved how thoroughly it had been drowned. A young man fixing a bicycle by the door informed her that Merrow Cross was where the post went to die, and that his cousin had once sent a parcel there and received, a fortnight later, a thank-you letter and a loaf of bread. A woman with two dogs said that her aunt had married somebody from Merrow Cross, or possibly from near Merrow Cross, or possibly somebody who had once been to Merrow Cross and never stopped talking about it.

"It's a joke," said the farmer's wife, who had been listening. "The map people put it there to catch each other out. Everybody knows that."

"So do I," said Amira. "That's why I'm here."

The farmer's wife looked at her as if she had said something rather sad.

In her notebook, Amira wrote, "Local testimony: inconsistent. Reliability: low." It was a phrase she had written a hundred times. Even so, she found that she did not quite believe it this time. The stories did not agree on anything except one thing, and the one thing was large. Nobody in the café had said that the town was not there. They had said it was drowned, or dead, or famous for bread, or a joke. Not one of them had said it was empty.

You could take local legends with a pinch of salt, her father used to say, but you should never throw away the salt.

She finished the sandwich, filled the car, and asked Bryony whether there was mobile signal in the hills.

"Some," said Bryony. "On the tops. When it feels like it."

### [p08]
The road out of Callowford climbed steadily for twenty minutes and then lost interest in climbing and began to wander instead, following the side of a valley as if it were looking for something it had dropped.

Amira passed the church, passed a farm with a barking dog, passed a point where she was quite sure she had gone too far, and went a bit further. The tarmac grew thin and patched. Grass appeared down the middle of the road like a parting in someone's hair. On her phone, the bars of signal disappeared one by one, with the polite finality of guests leaving a party.

The first turning on the left was a farm track that ended at a gate and a very surprised cow. She reversed. The second came ten minutes later: a narrow lane between two stone walls, unmarked, half hidden by a hawthorn tree. There had been no bridge before it, or rather, Amira realised as she slowed down, there had been something that was not quite a bridge: two old stone pillars on either side of a dry stream, with nothing between them but air. A bridge that wasn't there. She almost laughed.

Second left.

The lane ran downhill through a wood of birch and fir, so steeply in places that she drove in first gear with her foot resting on the brake. The light turned green and underwater. Once, a deer stood in the road and looked at her with mild disapproval before stepping into the trees.

She was beginning to wonder whether Bryony had sent her on a wild goose chase, perhaps as a hobby, when the trees opened suddenly and the lane ran out onto a wide, flat bank of grey stones.

In front of her was a river, broad and brown and quick, talking to itself over the stones. On the far side, the hill rose steeply into a dark wall of fir. And the road, the road she had followed for an hour, ran straight down to the edge of the water and simply stopped, like a sentence somebody had forgotten to finish.

## [c3] Crossing

### [p09]
Amira sat in the car for a full minute with the engine off, listening to the river and to the small, accusing tick of the cooling metal.

Then she got out and walked down to the water.

It was not, she saw, quite as impossible as it had looked. Where the road ended, a line of flat pale stones ran out into the river and across it, slightly upstream, like a row of stepping stones that had been pushed under the surface. The water over them was shallow and clear, perhaps knee-deep at most, and it moved across them in a smooth, glassy sheet. On the far bank, beside a gap in the trees, stood a wooden post with a metal box fixed to it, the kind of box a farmer might use for milk. Its lid was painted green.

A ford. An old one, and a used one. The stones had been placed by people who knew what they were doing, and the path on the other side had been kept clear.

Nowhere in the file had anybody mentioned a ford. It was the kind of thing a photograph from orbit would never show, because it was, by its nature, underwater.

It was, of course, against Gridwell's field safety policy to cross a river on foot without a second person present. Amira knew this because she had helped to write the policy. Section four, paragraph two: "Failure to observe appropriate precautions in the vicinity of moving water may result in serious harm and the suspension of field privileges." She had always been fond of "in the vicinity of". It made a river sound like a rumour.

She checked the water carefully, the way her father had taught her. It was low after a dry week. The current was steady but not strong. The stones were wide and flat and she could see every one of them. She changed into her walking boots, rolled her trousers to the knee, took the extending pole from the boot of the car, and put the postcard and the printout into a plastic bag inside her rucksack.

Then, slowly, one stone at a time, with the pole planted firmly downstream of her, she crossed.

### [p10]
The water was so cold that for the first few steps she could think of nothing else. By the middle it had become merely painful, and by the far bank it was something she was able to describe to herself in complete sentences, most of which would not have been suitable for a report.

Having reached the other side, she sat on a rock, emptied her boots, wrung out her socks and put everything back on with the cheerful discomfort of a person who has done something slightly foolish and got away with it. Then she looked at the green box on its post. It was empty, but its hinges had been oiled.

The path climbed into the firs.

It was not a wild path. Not only had someone cut steps into the steepest parts, but they had edged the steps with flat stones and laid branches across the muddy patches so that walkers would not slip. In two places, where the slope fell away sharply, a rope had been fixed between posts as a handrail. The rope was new. Somebody, Amira thought, walked this path often, and carried heavy things along it.

The forest closed above her. It was quiet in the way that only very old woods are quiet, as if the trees had agreed among themselves to keep their voices down. Light fell in narrow lines between the trunks. Somewhere above, a bird kept repeating the same two notes, like someone practising a word in a foreign language.

She took out the printout and held it beside the slope. The 1931 contour lines, brown and fine, curled across the paper exactly as the ground curled under her feet. The path turned where the lines bent. The slope steepened where they crowded together. A small stream crossed the path precisely where a thin blue thread crossed the old map.

If her father hadn't spent those winters teaching her to read these lines, she would be lost now, she thought, and probably back in the car. Instead, she was walking up a hill that a liar had drawn ninety-five years ago, and the hill was agreeing with him in every detail.

### [p11]
The path climbed for forty minutes, then swung sharply to the right and came out onto a bare ridge where the trees thinned to heather and rock.

Amira stopped there to catch her breath and look around.

Below her, to the right, she could see the whole of the valley she had driven through: the river shining in its bed of stones, the white thread of the lane coming down through the wood, and, far away, the small grey dot of her hire car, parked at the edge of the water like a full stop. To the left, the ridge curved round in a long arm of hill and closed off whatever lay behind it. That was the hanging valley on the map, tucked away out of sight. From the road you would never guess it was there. You might well have driven past it every day for fifty years without once suspecting that the hill had a pocket.

She walked along the ridge until she could look down into the pocket.

Trees.

The hanging valley was perhaps a kilometre long and half as wide, a shallow green bowl with steep sides, and it was entirely full of trees. Fir and pine, mostly, and a few broad-leaved ones near the bottom where the river must run. From up here they made exactly the same smooth, combed carpet that she had seen on her screen in the office. There were no roofs. There was no road. There was no school, no bakery, no washing on a line, no car glinting in a yard.

Amira stood very still.

So the satellites were right, then. Fourteen notes, two years of analysis and every camera in orbit had all said the same thing, and they had all been correct. She had crossed a river in her socks to confirm what a computer had told her for free.

That said, there was the ford. There was the path with its steps and its new rope, the oiled hinges, the green box. There was a postcard in a plastic bag in her rucksack. Somebody walked here. Somebody carried things. Somebody wrote back.

Clouds were beginning to gather above the far end of the valley, grey and heavy, and the light was changing.

### [p12]
She sat down on a flat rock out of the wind and ate the second half of the sandwich she had not wanted, because it was there and because her father had taught her never to make decisions on an empty stomach.

The sensible decision was obvious. She had come, she had looked, she had found a valley full of trees. She could photograph it, walk back down, cross the ford before the light went, and be in a hotel by nine, writing a short and dignified report. Feature not present. Recommendation confirmed. Gavin would be pleased. The desk with the window would be waiting.

Hardly had she taken out her phone to photograph the valley when it buzzed in her hand. One bar of signal had arrived from somewhere, like a bird landing on a fence, and with it came a message.

"How's the trap town? Need a decision by Friday. Remember, it's not there. Gavin."

Amira looked at the message for a long time.

"It's not there," she said aloud, to nobody, to the heather.

Little did Gavin know how much she wanted to agree with him. It would have been so much simpler. She could have been in a dry car in half an hour. She put the phone away, stood up, brushed the crumbs from her jacket and lifted the camera to take one last, honest photograph of an empty valley.

Then she smelt it.

It came up to her on the wind, faint and unmistakable, the smell of every winter evening of her childhood: woodsmoke. Not the smell of a forest fire or of a walker's stove. The warm, slightly sweet smell of a stove inside a house, burning seasoned wood, the kind of fire people light for cooking and not for adventure.

She lowered the camera and looked again, more slowly this time, not at the valley as a whole but at its parts.

Only then did she see it. Down near the bottom of the bowl, where the trees were thickest, a thin grey line was rising straight into the still air between two firs, and then another beside it, and another, until she had counted seven.

Chimney smoke.

## [c4] Population: Unconfirmed

### [p13]
The path down into the hanging valley was steeper than the one up, and much better kept.

It zigzagged through the firs on wide, neat steps, and at each turn the smell of smoke grew stronger, and other smells joined it: cut grass, wet stone, and, unmistakably, bread. Halfway down, Amira heard a bell, a small, high, brisk bell, rung by someone who wanted children to come inside and did not expect them to hurry.

Then, without any warning at all, she was in a town.

So quietly did it appear that she could not afterwards say where the forest had ended. One moment she was walking between trees; the next, she realised that the dark shape to her left was not a trunk but the corner of a stone cottage, and that the green above it was not a branch but a roof. The houses stood among the trees rather than in a clearing, as if they had grown there. Tall firs had been left standing between them, and their branches met above the narrow lanes, so that the whole town lived under a kind of green ceiling.

The roofs were all the same colour. It was the deep, soft green of old moss, the green of the postcard. Here and there the green was flaking at the edges, and underneath it Amira could see grey slate.

She walked on slowly, the way you walk into a church or a library, and the town went on being a town around her. A woman was hanging washing between two trees and nodded at her as if strangers came down the hill every afternoon. Two old men sat on a bench beside a water pump, arguing about something with deep and peaceful satisfaction. There were window boxes full of herbs, a stack of firewood under every eave, a small stone bridge over the stream, and, at the centre of it all, a little green with a bakery on one side and a school on the other.

Amira stopped in the middle of the green and stood there with her mouth slightly open, a professional mapmaker in the middle of a professional fiction.

The town that wasn't there smelt wonderfully of bread.

### [p14]
The bakery was a low stone building with a bow window and a door that rang a bell when it opened. Inside, it was warm enough to make her wet boots steam. Loaves stood in rows on wooden shelves, and behind the counter a tall man in his forties, with flour on his forearms and a pencil behind his ear, was cutting curved lines into the top of a round, unbaked loaf.

He did not seem surprised to see her. He finished the last line, slid the loaf onto a tray and wiped his hands on his apron.

"You crossed the ford," he said. It was not a question. He was looking at her trousers, which were wet to the knee.

"I did."

"Most people wait for Tuesday. The post van leaves things in the box and I fetch them." He smiled, and his smile was friendly and slightly guarded, like a door left open on a chain. "Kit Rowley. Bread, mostly. Can I help you?"

"Amira Haddad. I was wondering if you could tell me the name of this town."

Kit considered this with great seriousness. "That depends," he said, "who's asking."

"Gridwell. The map company."

"Ah. In that case, it's nowhere at all. Very quiet. Hardly worth mentioning." He turned to the shelves. "Would you like some bread? The nowhere sourdough is particularly good today."

Behind him, on the wall, hung a blackboard with the day's bread written on it in chalk. Amira glanced at it, then glanced again. The letters were small, upright and beautifully even, the kind of lettering that seemed oddly familiar, though she could not have said why. She was tired, and wet, and her mind was on other things.

"The roofs," she said. "Why are they all green?"

"Moss," said Kit at once. "We have a great deal of moss. It's the damp."

"The moss has flaked off over the slate in places."

"Very hardy moss," said Kit gravely, and handed her a loaf wrapped in brown paper. "No charge. Visitors from nowhere get the first one free."

She took the bread. It was warm, and on top of it, cut into the crust, were curved, flowing lines, close together on one side and wide apart on the other, like the slopes of a small hill.

### [p15]
Within ten minutes of leaving the bakery, Amira had acquired a guide.

Her name was Nell, she was eleven, and she had been waiting outside the bakery door with the patient air of someone who had known that something interesting was about to come out of it. She wore wellington boots, a jumper with holes in both elbows, and the expression of a journalist.

"Are you from the map?" she asked.

"In a sense," said Amira.

"Are you going to take us off it?"

Amira hesitated, and Nell nodded, as if the hesitation had been an answer, and took her on a tour.

It was the most informative tour Amira had ever been given, and also the least. Nell showed her the school, which had one classroom, one teacher and eleven pupils, and which Nell described as "fine, mostly". She showed her the water pump, the bridge, the field where they played football and the shed where they kept the fire hose. She knew everybody, and everybody knew her, and everybody, without exception, refused to tell Amira what the town was called.

"This?" said the woman with the washing. "This is just here, love."

"We call it home," said one of the old men on the bench. "The post calls it something else. We try not to interfere."

"Which town?" said the other old man, and looked around in mild surprise, as if a town might have crept up on him.

It was not hostility. Nobody was rude, and several people offered her tea. It was more like a game in which everyone knew the rules except her, and in which the rules were being enjoyed enormously.

By the end of the afternoon, Amira had counted fifty-one houses, one bakery, one school, one shed, a small shop that was also the post office and was open, according to Nell, "when Mrs Gale's knees allow", and, on the teacher's careful estimate, about a hundred and forty people. She wrote it down in her notebook in her best report voice.

"Settlement observed. Population approximately one hundred and forty. Name: unconfirmed."

Nell read it over her shoulder. "You can't confirm a name," she said. "A name isn't a fact. It's just what people call you."

"I've been meaning to tell my company that for years," said Amira.

### [p16]
It was too late to cross the ford again by the time the light began to go, and Kit, appearing at her elbow with the quiet timing of a man who had been watching the sky, offered her the spare room above the bakery.

"It's small," he said. "It has a bed, a window and a strong smell of yeast. Two out of three is a good average in the hills."

She ate supper with him in the kitchen behind the shop: soup, cheese, and, inevitably, bread. The kitchen was crowded and comfortable, and on the shelf above the table, among the jars of flour and seeds, stood an old wooden box with brass corners, of the kind that drawing instruments once came in. Amira noticed it because her father had owned one exactly like it.

"My grandfather's," said Kit, seeing her look. "He drew lines for a living."

"What kind of lines?"

"The kind that tell you where things are." He refilled her glass of water. "Or where they aren't. He was never entirely sure there was a difference."

She waited for more. Kit, it appeared, had said all he intended to say, and changed the subject so smoothly that she only noticed afterwards. He asked about her work, and she found herself telling him: about the bridges that didn't exist, the lakes that turned out to be car parks, the villages that had been renamed by mistake and then kept the wrong name out of stubbornness. She told him about her father and his wooden box.

"When we first came to Canada," she said, "we lived for two months in a flat that the post office didn't recognise. The building was new and nobody had added it to the system. My grandmother wrote to us every week from Beirut, and every week the letters went back to her, stamped unknown. She thought we had disappeared. My father went to the post office every morning for eight weeks, until somebody finally typed in our street."

Kit was quiet for a moment.

"Then you know," he said, "what an address is worth."

"I know what it's worth to the people who live there," said Amira. "I'm paid to know what it's worth to everyone else."

Kit smiled, a little sadly, and did not argue. Neither did she.

### [p17]
In the morning, Nell was waiting outside the bakery again, this time with an announcement.

"Mrs Gale wants to see you," she said. "She's the oldest. She's ninety-four. She came here when it was just trees and mud. If anybody tells you anything, she will."

"Why would she tell me anything?"

Nell shrugged. "She says it's high time somebody from the maps came and looked."

Mrs Hester Gale lived in the cottage beside the shop that was also the post office, and she received Amira in a small sitting room so full of furniture that it seemed to have been furnished by several generations at once and never cleared. She was tiny and very upright, with white hair pinned on top of her head and eyes that missed nothing at all. A cup of tea was poured before Amira had finished sitting down.

"You'll be the young woman from Gridwell," said Mrs Gale. "You crossed the ford in your socks. Everybody's talking about it. We haven't had a stranger cross the ford in the wrong footwear since nineteen ninety-seven, and he was lost."

"I'd rather they didn't talk about my socks," said Amira.

"I'm sure you would. Drink your tea."

They drank tea. Mrs Gale asked a series of questions about Amira's family, her work, her opinion of Gavin Pell, whom she seemed to know about already, and her views on the correct way to make soup. The questions were polite and precise, and Amira had the strong impression that she was being surveyed, contour by contour.

At last, Mrs Gale put her cup down.

"Well," she said. "I suppose you'd better see it."

She nodded towards the wall behind Amira's chair. Amira turned.

Hanging there, in a plain wooden frame, its colours faded to brown and cream, was a map. She knew it instantly. She had spent a week staring at a scan of it. It was the 1931 Hollins and Vane sheet of the Corrin Hills: the same road, the same river, the same fine brown contour lines curling into the hanging valley, the same small cluster of black squares beside the bend.

It was not a copy. It was an original, first printing, and in its margin, beside the town, someone had written a short note in pencil, in small, upright, beautifully even letters.

## [c5] Invented

### [p18]
"What does it say?" asked Amira, although she was already leaning close enough to read it.

"It says, 'In case anybody needs it,'" said Mrs Gale. "He wrote that in nineteen thirty-one, on the day the sheet was printed. He was twenty-four. He thought he was being funny."

"Who did?"

Mrs Gale settled back in her chair with the air of a person who has told a story many times and intends to tell it properly once more.

"There was a young man," she said, "who worked in the drawing office at Hollins and Vane. Clever, quiet, and very bored. His job was to draw the Corrin Hills from the surveyors' notes, and the surveyors had walked round this valley rather than into it, because it was steep and wet and full of nothing. So there was a blank on his sheet. And the firm, like every firm in those days, wanted a little lie on every map, to catch the copiers."

"A trap," said Amira.

"A trap. Most of them invented a lane or a pond. He invented a town. He walked up here on his days off, all one summer, with a notebook and a pencil, and drew this valley more carefully than anybody had ever drawn anything in these hills. Then he put a handful of houses in it, by the river, where the ground was flattest. He said a town should be put where a town would want to be."

"And the name?"

"Merrow was a word his mother used for a mermaid. The cross was where two footpaths met, down by the bridge. He thought it sounded like somewhere you might have been born." Mrs Gale smiled. "Little did he know that somebody would be."

Amira looked at the map. The black squares, the careful curves, the bend of the river. Everything she had seen yesterday from the ridge was there: the shape of the bowl, the stream, the spot where the path came down.

"He was supposed to make something nobody would believe," she said slowly.

"He was supposed to make something nobody would check," said Mrs Gale. "That's not the same thing at all."

### [p19]
"In nineteen fifty-three," Mrs Gale went on, "they built a reservoir in the next valley but one. You'll have heard about it in Callowford, I expect, all wrong."

"I was told a town had been drowned. With the church bell still ringing."

"Nobody drowned and there was no bell. There were eleven farms in the bottom of that valley, and the water board bought them all, fairly enough, and then the water came up and covered them. My parents had one of those farms. I was twenty-one." She said it without sorrow, as one might mention the weather in a year long past. "Eleven families with money in their pockets and nowhere to put their feet. The land round here was cheap because it was useless. Steep, wet, all trees, and a river with no bridge. So we bought this valley between us, and we came over the ford with our furniture on our backs, and we built."

"From nothing."

"From stone. There's a great deal of stone." Mrs Gale's eyes glittered. "The trouble came afterwards. You can build a house in a summer. You can't build an address. The post office would not deliver to 'the valley behind the ridge, past the river, ask anybody'. The doctor in Callowford needed a name to write on his cards. The council needed a name for its forms. We had a valley and no name, and without a name we were, so to speak, not officially anywhere."

"And then you found the map," said Amira.

"My father found it. In the post office in Callowford, on the wall, a Hollins and Vane sheet, and right in the middle of our valley, a little town by the river with a name beside it. He came home and told us all in the evening, and we laughed until we cried. Then he wrote a letter to himself, with his own name on the envelope and, underneath it, the name of the town by the river. He posted it in Callowford on a Monday." She paused, enjoying herself. "It arrived on Thursday."

Amira found that she had put her hand over her mouth.

"Had the map been honest," said Mrs Gale, "we would never have had an address at all. Whatever it was meant to be, that lie was the kindest thing anybody ever did for us."

### [p20]
For a while, neither of them spoke. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere a child was singing a song with no clear ending.

"Who was he?" asked Amira at last. "The young man in the drawing office."

Mrs Gale looked at her for a long moment, and Amira had the curious feeling that a decision was being made, and that it was not going in her favour.

"He came to see us," she said. "In nineteen fifty-four. He'd heard, the way people hear things, that his town had people in it. He walked up the ford path with that same notebook, and he stood on the green, and he cried like a child. And then he stayed. It's said that he never drew another map in his life." She picked up her cup. "But that's his story, not mine, and I'd rather not tell it for him. More tea?"

Amira accepted more tea, because there seemed to be no alternative.

"Can I ask you something else?" she said. "Everybody here knows the name. You use it on your letters. You've had it for seventy years. So why does everyone pretend they've never heard of it?"

"We don't pretend we've never heard of it," said Mrs Gale. "We simply don't wear it in public. There's a difference." She set down her cup. "We are a small town. We like to be found by the people who need to find us, the postman, the doctor, the ambulance, and nobody else. When we needed an ambulance in nineteen eighty-seven, for my husband, it came to the ford, because Merrow Cross was on the map. The crew carried him over on a stretcher. He lived another twenty years." She said this quite simply. "That's what a name is for. Not for coaches. Not for people who want to photograph us and write about how charming we are. We are not charming. We are just here. Every few years somebody offers to put us on the map, as they say. We are already on the map. That's quite enough."

"Should my company ask," said Amira carefully, "how would you like me to describe you?"

Mrs Gale thought about it.

"Tell them we are a clerical error," she said, "that pays its taxes."

### [p21]
Nell showed her where the signal was, which turned out to be the top of a small rocky hill behind the school, beside a water tank. You had to stand on a particular stone and face south-east, Nell said, and not move your elbows.

Amira stood on the stone, faced south-east, kept her elbows still and called Theo.

"You're alive," said Theo. "Gavin said you'd probably gone native or fallen in a bog."

"There's a town, Theo."

There was a long silence, filled with the faint crunch of crisps.

"A real one?"

"Fifty-one houses, a bakery, a school, a post office and a hundred and forty people who refuse to say where they live. It's in the trees. You can't see it from the ridge, and you certainly can't see it from orbit. It's been here since nineteen fifty-three, and it's been using our name for it ever since."

"Our name?"

"The trap. They found it on a map and moved into it." She took a breath. "Theo, I need you to check something for me. Address Services. How many addresses do they hold under Merrow Cross?"

She heard him typing. The typing stopped.

"Sixty-one," he said in a small voice. "All linked to our place record. I never looked. It's not our database."

"And if our place record is deleted?"

"Then the addresses lose their town. They'll still exist, technically, but they won't point anywhere. Anything that uses our data to find an address won't find them. Delivery firms. Taxis." He hesitated. "The emergency dispatch system. They license our map for that."

The wind blew across the hill. Amira kept her elbows very still.

"When is the deletion scheduled?"

Theo's voice dropped further. "That's the other thing. Gavin didn't want to wait till Friday. He's put it in tomorrow night's batch. Automatic. If you haven't filed a report by then, Merrow Cross stops existing at midnight." He paused. "Again."

## [c6] Two Drafts

### [p22]
That evening, left alone in the small room above the bakery with a laptop, a candle-shaped lamp and a strong smell of yeast, Amira began to write.

She wrote the first draft quickly, because she had written it, in one form or another, a hundred times before. She wrote it the way Gridwell liked reports to be written, which was the way a glacier would write if it had been trained in administration.

"Report on the verification of the feature known as Merrow Cross, Corrin Hills. Following a site inspection conducted over two days, it would appear that a settlement of approximately one hundred and forty residents occupies the area purportedly represented by the feature. That said, the settlement is not observable in any available imagery, owing to dense tree cover and persistent cloud. Furthermore, the designation of the settlement as Merrow Cross is not confirmed by residents, who decline to adopt it in conversation. In view of the non-observation of the settlement in imagery, the absence of a confirmed designation, and the historical origin of the feature as a deliberate fabrication, it is recommended that the existing record be retained in a modified form, pending further review."

She read it back. It was a perfectly good report. It was also, she realised, a report written by somebody who had never had cold feet in a river or warm bread in her hands. In it, nobody lived anywhere. Settlements occupied areas. Designations were not confirmed. Residents declined to adopt. And the recommendation at the end, so carefully balanced, so beautifully hedged, would be read by Gavin in four seconds and turned, without any difficulty at all, into a deletion.

Pending further review meant nothing. Gavin did not do further review. Gavin did batches.

She opened a second document and wrote one line.

"There is a town. I have been in it. It smells of bread."

She looked at that for a long time too. It was true, every word of it. It was arguably the most accurate report she had ever written. It would also, she knew, get her laughed out of the building, and then Gavin would delete the town anyway, at midnight, with a click.

Between the two drafts lay the whole problem: one was correct and useless, the other was honest and useless, and somewhere in between there had to be a sentence that a computer would believe.

### [p23]
The difficulty, in the end, came down to one rule, and Amira had helped to write that too.

Section seven, paragraph one, of Gridwell's Verification Standards: "It is essential that each verified settlement be supported by current imagery in which its principal structures are clearly identifiable." Ground photographs were welcome as supporting evidence, the next paragraph added, but they could not, on their own, be used for verification. The reason had been sensible at the time. Anybody could take a photograph of a few cottages and claim they were somewhere else. The camera in orbit had no reason to lie.

Were it not for that sentence, she could have verified Merrow Cross tonight with forty photographs from her phone. Because of it, she needed one picture from the sky showing a town that had, for seventy years, refused to appear in one.

At about eleven o'clock, she went downstairs for a glass of water. The bakery was dark and silent and smelt of tomorrow's dough rising under cloths. The kitchen door was closed. The storeroom door beside it, however, stood half open, and the light from the street outside fell across the shelves inside.

The shelves were full of paint.

Not a few tins, of the kind you keep for small repairs, but dozens of them, large ones, stacked three deep and all exactly the same. On each lid someone had painted a broad stripe of colour to show what was inside, and every stripe was the same deep, soft green. In the corner stood a bundle of long-handled brushes, cleaned and wrapped in cloth, and two ladders long enough to reach a roof.

She remembered Bryony at the petrol station, writing her crossword word. They buy a lot of green paint, that lot.

The roofs, Amira thought sleepily, must need a lot of care. Moss, Kit had said. Very hardy moss. A town in a damp valley would need to paint its roofs, to protect the slate, and it would make sense to buy the paint all at once, in bulk, from somewhere cheap. Perfectly reasonable.

She drank her water, went back upstairs, and did not think about the paint again until it was almost too late.

### [p24]
In the morning, Kit asked her to help with the bread, and she found, to her surprise, that she said yes.

"You're being very quiet," he said, after twenty minutes of kneading in companionable silence. "For a woman whose job is to ask questions."

"I'm writing a report in my head."

"How's it going?"

"I have two drafts. One is accurate and the other is true."

Kit folded the dough over on itself, pushed it away with the heel of his hand, and drew it back. "Which one are you going to send?"

"The trouble is," said Amira, "neither of them will do any good. The first will be deleted by a man who reads only the last line. The second will be deleted by a man who stops reading after the first line." She punched her dough, perhaps harder than necessary. "If I had a satellite picture of this town, I could save it in a sentence. But there isn't one. There's never been one."

"No," said Kit. "I don't suppose there has."

Something in the way he said it made her look up, but he was shaping a loaf, and his face showed nothing except concentration.

Nell arrived at nine with a basket for the morning's bread and an air of enormous news.

"I'm going to be on the roof this summer," she announced. "Mum says I'm old enough now. Eleven is old enough, as long as you're tied on. I've been waiting for ages."

"On the roof?" said Amira. "To do what?"

Nell opened her mouth. Kit, without looking up from the dough, said, "To fix the slates, Nell. Weren't you going to take Mrs Gale her loaf?"

Nell closed her mouth, took the loaf and went.

Amira watched her go. Granted, roofs needed fixing. Granted, a town of a hundred and forty people would need every pair of hands it could find, even small ones. By the same token, it seemed a curious thing for a child to have been waiting for ages to do.

She opened her mouth to ask. Then her phone, which had been dead in her pocket for two days, gave a single, startled buzz from the windowsill where it had found, for one second, a thread of signal.

It was a message from Theo, and it consisted of one word, repeated three times, in capital letters. Call.

### [p25]
She stood on the stone by the water tank, faced south-east, and did not move her elbows.

"Good news," said Theo. "Sort of. Possibly. I've been looking at the forecast data for the Corrin Hills. Tomorrow morning there's a high-pressure system right over you, and a satellite pass at eleven fourteen. Clear sky over the whole valley for about forty minutes. The first completely cloud-free pass in six years."

Amira closed her eyes.

"If the town's there," Theo went on, "it'll be on the image. Roofs, gardens, the lot. You'll have your current imagery, and section seven can be satisfied. I've talked to Gavin. He's being difficult, obviously, but he's agreed to hold the deletion until he's seen the picture. If the town's on it, it stays. If it isn't..."

"I know," said Amira.

"Amira, it's going to be fine. A hundred and forty people can't hide from a satellite on a clear day. Nobody's that good."

She walked back down to the bakery with a strange lightness in her chest and a strange heaviness underneath it, as if the two drafts had moved from her head into her body. Kit was taking loaves out of the oven, his face red from the heat.

"There's a satellite tomorrow," she told him. "A clear one. Cloud-free, for the first time in six years. At eleven fourteen. If the town's on the picture, Gridwell can't delete it."

She had expected him to look relieved, or worried, or at least interested. He looked, instead, very calm.

"Clear skies," said Kit. "How lovely." And he turned back to the oven.

Amira stood in the warm shop with the bell still swaying above the door behind her, and for the first time since she had crossed the ford, she could not tell whether the town was on her side.

## [c7] Green Roofs

### [p26]
The morning of the satellite pass was so clear that the hills seemed to have been redrawn overnight with a sharper pencil.

Such was the stillness of the air that Amira could hear, from the green, a dog barking somewhere on the far side of the ridge. The sky above the trees was a pale, perfect blue, without a single cloud from one edge of the valley to the other. Theo's forecast, for once in the history of forecasts, had been right.

She had expected the town to be nervous. She had imagined, lying awake in the room above the bakery, all sorts of things: people hurrying with ladders, sheets pulled over gardens, a frantic hundred and forty trying to hide a town in a morning. She needn't have worried. Nothing of the kind happened. The baker baked. The teacher rang the little bell at nine and the children went in, not quickly. The two old men took their places on the bench by the water pump and resumed yesterday's argument, which appeared to concern the correct way to stack firewood. A woman hung out washing between two trees, white sheets and blue shirts and a red jumper, as if the sky were the most ordinary thing in the world.

At eleven o'clock, Amira went out onto the green and stood in the middle of it with her face turned up.

Kit came out of the bakery and stood beside her, wiping his hands on his apron. Nell appeared from nowhere, as Nell always did, and stood on her other side.

"Is it coming now?" asked Nell.

"In fourteen minutes," said Amira. "It moves very fast and very high. You won't be able to see it."

"Will it be able to see us?"

Amira did not answer. Kit said nothing at all.

At eleven fourteen, Nell waved at the sky with both arms. After a moment, feeling slightly ridiculous, Amira waved too. Kit kept his hands in his apron pockets and looked up at the green ceiling of branches above the lane, with an expression she could not read, something between patience and pride.

Then it was eleven fifteen, and the sky was just the sky, and the town went back to its morning.

### [p27]
The image arrived at four in the afternoon, while Amira was standing on her stone by the water tank with her elbows pinned to her sides. Theo had sent it without a message, which was so unlike Theo that she knew something was wrong before she opened it.

It was a beautiful image. It was sharp enough to count the sheep in the fields above Callowford and to see the hire car, still parked at the edge of the river like a patient grey dog. She moved her thumb across the screen into the hanging valley and zoomed in.

Trees.

A smooth, even, perfectly combed carpet of green, from one side of the bowl to the other. No roofs. No lanes. No green with a bakery on one side and a school on the other. No washing on a line. Nothing.

She lowered the phone and looked down.

There, below her, in the bright afternoon, was the town. She could see a roof from where she stood: the roof of the school, deep, soft, mossy green, under the spread branches of two tall firs. Beyond it, another roof, the same green. And another. Not one surface that a camera in orbit could see that was not, in one way or another, green.

She looked at the screen. She looked at the town. Then she put the two pictures side by side in her head, the way she had once laid tracing paper over a map, and everything she had been told for three days rearranged itself into a single, obvious shape.

Moss, Kit had said. Very hardy moss. The paint flaking at the edges of the roofs. The storeroom full of identical tins, all the same green. Bryony at the petrol station: they buy a lot of green paint, that lot. The trees left standing between every house. Nell, who had been waiting for ages to go on the roof. The postcard, painted with such affection, showing roofs that were exactly the colour they really were.

It had not been cloud for seventy years. It had not been bad luck, or dense tree cover, or a failure of imagery. Merrow Cross had not been missing from every satellite image ever taken.

It had been hiding.

### [p28]
She found Kit in the kitchen behind the shop, sitting at the table with a cup of tea and the wooden box with brass corners open in front of him. Inside it, on faded blue velvet, lay a set of old drawing pens, a pair of dividers and a small bottle of ink that had long ago turned to dust.

He looked up as she came in and saw the phone in her hand.

"Ah," he said. "Nothing, then."

"You paint the roofs," said Amira. "All of you. Green. On purpose."

"Every five summers," said Kit. "Everybody helps. It takes about a fortnight, and there's a party at the end. This year is Nell's first time on a ladder." He pushed a second cup towards her. "It started in the sixties. Somebody saw the first pictures from space in a newspaper and realised what they meant. The roofs were grey then. Grey slate shows up beautifully from above, apparently. Like a thumbprint."

"So you painted them out."

"We painted them in," said Kit. "Into the trees. We want the postman to find us. We want the ambulance to find us. We'd simply rather not be found by everybody else. A name on a map is an address. A picture on a screen is an invitation." He smiled slightly. "We've been told, more than once, how charming we'd look in a magazine. We'd rather not be charming. We'd rather be left where we were found."

Amira sat down. She looked at the box, at the pens, at the small, upright, beautifully even letters on the blackboard through the door. At the postcard, which she had taken out of her rucksack without noticing, and which lay now on the table between them.

"Your grandfather," she said. "The young man in the drawing office."

"Arthur Lisle," said Kit. "Twenty-four, very bored, and fond of his mother's word for mermaids. He came up here in nineteen fifty-four, saw what he'd started, and never went back. If he hadn't been so bored in nineteen thirty-one, none of us would be here now. He taught me to letter when I was smaller than Nell." He touched the edge of the postcard. "When your notice came, I wrote the reply. I'm afraid I couldn't resist the postmark. Mrs Gale keeps the stamp in a drawer."

"You realise," said Amira, "that you have kept a copyright trap alive for seventy years. A deliberate falsehood, carefully maintained."

"Granted," said Kit. "But it's a very good trap. And none of us, so far, has been copied."

### [p29]
She wrote the final report that night, in the small room above the bakery, and it took her four hours, most of which she spent deleting adjectives.

It was neither of her two drafts. In the box at the top marked Status, she typed two words, with a full stop after each. Exists. Verified. The rest was written in Gridwell's language, because Gridwell would not read anything else, and every sentence in it was true. "Verification of the feature known as Merrow Cross has been completed by site inspection. The feature exists. It is a settlement of one hundred and forty residents and sixty-one addresses, in continuous occupation since 1953. Its non-appearance in imagery is the result of a long-standing local practice of painting roofs and preserving tree cover, and should not be interpreted as an absence." Then, under a heading she invented for the purpose, she wrote the part that mattered. "Should the record be deleted, the settlement's addresses would lose their link to emergency and postal services. It is therefore essential that the record be retained, that the name be displayed at regional scale only, and that no close-scale rendering or imagery highlight be produced. Section seven does not apply. I have been in it."

Gavin rang the next morning, while she stood on the stone, and complained for nine minutes. Then, having spoken to the legal department about the phrase "emergency services", he approved it. Had he been given the first draft, Amira thought, he would have deleted the town by lunchtime. Had he been given the second, he would have deleted her.

She left Merrow Cross on Friday. Nell walked her to the ford and made her promise to come back in August, to help with the roofs, with a proper brush and the right boots. Mrs Gale sent a message by Nell, which was that she should drink more tea.

Amira crossed the river, slowly, with the pole planted downstream. On the far bank she sat on the same rock and emptied her boots, and then she looked back.

From the ford there was nothing to see. Only the dark wall of fir, the arm of the ridge, the trees, the combed and even green. If she had not walked up there, she would not believe it even now.

Then again, she thought, that was always the point of a map. It was a promise that somebody had gone and looked. And some fictions, it turned out, are simply waiting for their residents.
