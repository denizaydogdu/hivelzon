## [c1] First Night

### [p01]
Every night when the wind turned north, the hill began to sing.

Jonah Price did not know that yet when he arrived in the village on a hot afternoon at the end of June. He knew a lot about rocks, because he was studying geology at a university in Wales, and he knew a little about Turkey, because he had been reading about it on the plane for four hours. He did not know anything about singing hills.

The bus left him at the edge of the village, next to a stone fountain and a sleeping dog. Around him, the land looked as if a giant had been playing with soft sand. There were tall towers of rock with pointed tops, cliffs full of small dark windows, and valleys in every shade of pink, cream and orange. Some of those windows had been cut into the rock more than a thousand years ago.

"You must be Jonah," said a voice behind him.

A young woman with a dusty hat and a notebook under her arm was smiling at him. "I'm Defne. I'm on the dig team. Dr Aydın asked me to meet you, although she also said you would probably be late."

"The bus was late," said Jonah.

"The bus is always late," said Defne. "Welcome to summer."

### [p02]
The team was staying in an old stone house at the top of the village, which belonged to a man called Mr Kemal. He was over eighty and very thin, and he seemed to know everything that had ever happened in the valley. He gave Jonah a glass of tea and asked Defne something in Turkish.

"He asked whether you had brought warm clothes," she explained. "I told him it was thirty-five degrees. He said that didn't matter."

Jonah's small window looked towards the hill. Its sides were smooth and pale, and near the top there was a line of darker rock, like a belt. Inside the hill, an old underground city went down for at least eight floors. The upper floors had been opened to visitors years ago, Defne told him. The lower floors were closed.

Dr Aydın arrived just before dinner. She was a small woman in her fifties, with short grey hair, sharp eyes and a voice that never needed to be loud. She had been working in the region for twenty years, and everyone simply called her the Doctor.

"Mr Price," she said. "Our rock expert. Good. Tomorrow at six you will start measuring the walls of the lower valley. Not the hill. The valley. Is that clear?"

"Yes," said Jonah, who did not understand why she had mentioned the hill at all.

### [p03]
He woke up a little after two in the morning, and at first he did not know why.

The room was dark and the house was silent. Then he heard it again: a long, low sound, deeper than any voice he knew, coming from somewhere outside. It rose slowly, stayed in the air for a few seconds, and then fell away, like someone breathing out. A moment later it came back, a little higher.

Jonah sat up. The sound was not exactly music, but it was not just noise either. It had a shape. Sometimes two notes seemed to touch each other and then move apart again. If he had heard it in a city, he would have thought it was a ship far away, or a train. But there was no sea here, and the nearest railway was a long way off.

He went to the window. The night air had turned surprisingly cold, and a strong wind was blowing down from the north. In the moonlight the hill looked grey and flat, like a piece of paper. The sound was definitely coming from there.

He tried to record it on his phone, but the recording was mostly wind. At some point he must have fallen asleep in the chair, because when he opened his eyes again, the room was full of early light and there was a noise like a giant breathing right outside the house.

### [p04]
It was a balloon.

Jonah ran out onto the roof in his socks. The sky over the valley was full of them: thirty, forty, perhaps more, red and yellow and blue, rising slowly in the pink light. Every few seconds, one of them made the same breathing sound as a flame jumped up inside it.

Mr Kemal was already on the roof, drinking tea as if he had seen this every morning for eighty years, which he probably had.

"Mr Kemal," Jonah said, pointing at the hill. "Last night. A sound. Like this." He tried to copy it, a long low note, and felt very stupid.

But the old man nodded at once. He said something in Turkish, and then, slowly, in English: "Wind. North wind. Hill sings."

Defne had come up behind them, still half asleep. She asked Mr Kemal a question, and he answered for a long time.

"He says that when he was a boy, his grandmother told him to bring the animals inside whenever the hill sang," she translated. "And she said that in her grandmother's time, the hill used to sing with two voices, not one."

"Why the animals?"

Mr Kemal understood the question. He answered with one word.

"Snow," said Defne. "He says the hill sings before the snow."

"It's June," said Jonah.

"I told him that," said Defne. "He said that the hill doesn't sing much in summer, so you should listen carefully when it does."

### [p05]
The work in the valley was hot and slow. Jonah spent the day measuring rock walls and writing numbers in a notebook, while the sun moved across the sky like a heavy lamp. The soft rock here had been made by volcanoes millions of years ago. It was soft enough to cut with simple tools, which was why people had been able to dig houses, kitchens and whole cities into it.

In the evening, Jonah told Dr Aydın about the sound. She listened politely, but she did not seem very interested.

"The wind makes many noises here," she said. "There are thousands of holes in these rocks. It is like blowing across the top of a bottle."

"But it had notes," said Jonah. "They changed."

"The wind changes too," said Dr Aydın, and she went back to her maps.

That night the north wind returned, and so did the song. This time Jonah was ready. He sat on the roof in a thick jacket that he had borrowed from Mr Kemal, with a small instrument for measuring wind speed in one hand and a torch in the other. The song rose and fell, and the wind rose and fell with it.

At twenty past three, the wind suddenly stopped.

The air went completely still. And at exactly the same moment, as if someone had closed a door, the hill stopped singing.

## [c2] The Survey

### [p06]
"A bottle doesn't stop like that," Jonah said the next morning.

He was sitting with Defne under a tree at the edge of the site, showing her his notebook. She had been working since five, cleaning pieces of old pots with a brush.

"If you blow across a bottle, you get a sound," he explained. "But the sound doesn't end at once when you stop, because the air inside keeps moving for a moment. Last night the sound didn't fade. It stopped, completely. As if the wind was the only thing holding it up."

"So?"

"So I think the wind goes into something, a long way, and comes out somewhere else. Like a pipe."

Defne thought about this while she ate a tomato. "Like a flute," she said.

Jonah smiled. He reached into his bag and took out a small wooden whistle, which his grandmother had given him when he was eight. It was just a tube with six holes. He played a few notes, then covered different holes and played again.

"Exactly like a flute," he said. "Different holes, different notes. If the hill were just a rock with a few holes in it, it would make the same noise every time. But it makes different notes when the wind changes direction. Something inside must be shaping the air."

"Or somebody made it that way," said Defne, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

### [p07]
For the next five nights, Jonah measured the wind after work, when everyone else was asleep. He soon looked like someone who had not slept properly for a week, because he hadn't. Despite this, he was happier than he had been for months.

He drew graphs in his notebook, and a pattern appeared. The hill sang only when the wind came from the north and was stronger than about twenty kilometres an hour. Whereas a wind from the north-west produced nothing at all, a wind from exactly north produced the lowest, longest note. When the wind moved a little towards the east, the note climbed higher, and then, strangely, it broke and disappeared, as if the hill had tried to sing a note that it no longer had.

"I've been trying to understand this for a week," he told Defne, "and I've got more questions now than when I started."

Meanwhile, Defne had been doing some research of her own. The team kept a small library in a back room of the house: boxes of old reports, photographs and maps, most of which nobody had opened for years. She spent two evenings going through them.

On the sixth evening, she came onto the roof with a thin brown folder and a very serious face.

"I found something," she said. "It's the report from the dig in the lower city in 1964. It's in Turkish, but there's a drawing. Look."

### [p08]
The drawing showed the underground city from the side, like a slice through a cake. Floor after floor went down into the hill, connected by narrow passages and steep stairs. On the left, a long thin line went straight up from the lowest floor to the top of the hill.

Defne pointed to a note in small handwriting next to the line. "Hava bacası. Air chimney. These cities needed fresh air, so the people who built them cut long tubes from the deepest rooms up to the surface. But listen to this."

She translated slowly. "Air chimney on the seventh level. Passage closed in 1964 after a small collapse. Unusual holes in the north wall. Not studied. Recommended for future work."

"Unusual holes," Jonah repeated.

"And then nothing," said Defne. "Nobody ever came back to study them. The report was put in a box, the box was put on a shelf, and here we are, sixty years later."

"They closed the passage," Jonah said slowly, "but the air chimney itself might still be open. Look where it reaches the top." He touched the drawing. "That's the dark line of rock near the top of the hill. The belt. The wind could be going in there."

"So the song starts at the top," said Defne, "and goes all the way down to those holes."

"We need to see them," said Jonah.

### [p09]
The upper floors of the underground city were open to visitors, so on their next free afternoon, Jonah and Defne bought two tickets like everyone else and went in.

Narrow passages opened into rooms where families had once cooked, slept and stored food. Here and there a great round stone, as tall as a man, stood beside a doorway. In the past, Defne explained, these stones could be rolled across the passages from the inside, so that nobody could get in.

"People hid down here when there was danger," she said. "Thousands of them, sometimes for weeks. They had water, kitchens, even places for animals."

They went down, and down again. On the fourth level, the passage ended at a metal gate with a heavy lock. Behind it, stone stairs went down into complete darkness. A sign on the gate said in two languages that visitors were not allowed to go any further.

Jonah put his hand on the cold metal. From somewhere far below, so quietly that he was not sure at first, he could feel the low song of the hill through his fingers, like a heart beating.

A guard was watching them from the end of the passage. Defne smiled at him and pulled Jonah away.

"Not like this," she said.

### [p10]
They went to Dr Aydın the next morning with the old report and Jonah's notebook full of graphs. She was in her office, which was a small room with a view of the valley and a desk covered in papers.

She listened without saying a word. She turned the pages of the report. She looked at Jonah's graphs for a long time. Then she closed the folder and put her hand on it.

"No," she said.

"But, Doctor," Defne began.

"The lower floors are closed," said Dr Aydın. "They have been closed for sixty years, and they are closed for good reasons. Some of the stairs are broken. Some of the ceilings may not be safe. There was a collapse, as you have read. Nobody goes below the fourth level without an engineer's report and official permission, and at the moment I have neither."

"Could we get them?" asked Defne.

"Not easily. Not this summer." Dr Aydın stood up, which meant the conversation was over. At the door, however, she stopped and looked at them both.

"I know what you are thinking," she said quietly. "You are thinking that you could go down one night with a torch and a rope, and nobody would ever know. Do not do it." She held the door open for them. "Some doors are closed for a reason."

## [c3] Permission

### [p11]
Jonah was angry for the rest of the day. He measured the valley walls so hard that he broke two pencils.

"She's wrong," he said that evening on the roof. "She just doesn't want two students to find something that she missed."

"She isn't wrong," said Defne. "She's careful. My uncle has been working in mines for thirty years. If you had heard his stories, you wouldn't want to go into an old tunnel at night either."

Jonah said nothing.

"And there's something else," Defne went on. "If we went down there without permission and something happened, the whole team might lose its permit. Twenty people would have to go home. Years of work would be stopped because of one stupid night."

"So we just forget about it?"

"No," said Defne. "We do it properly."

She took his notebook, opened it at a clean page and wrote one word at the top in large letters: Proposal.

"If they won't open the door," she said, "we ask for the key. With evidence, a plan and a safety list. The Doctor reads proposals. She doesn't read angry faces."

Jonah looked at the page for a long time. Then he laughed for the first time that day.

"Where did you learn to be so annoying?" he asked.

"Archaeology," said Defne. "Now help me write."

### [p12]
It took them three evenings. Jonah wrote the science: the wind directions, the times, the notes, and the strange way the sound stopped at once when the wind died. Defne wrote the history: the 1964 report, the drawing, and the old village story about the hill singing before the snow. She also made Jonah talk to Mr Kemal again, so that his words could be written down exactly.

"Because a story is evidence too," she said, when Jonah asked why. "Not proof. But evidence. If people in this valley have told the same story for three hundred years, something must have happened to start it."

The safety section was the longest part. They wrote that nobody would go down without the Doctor, and that the ceilings and stairs would have to be checked by an engineer first. They listed helmets, lamps, radios and ropes, and they suggested a limit of two hours underground.

On the last page, Jonah added a short paragraph.

If the holes really were made by people, he wrote, they will tell us how the people of this valley lived with the wind. If they are natural, we will have learned something new about this rock. Either way, by the end of the summer, we will have answered a question that has been waiting since 1964.

Defne read it twice. "Good," she said. "That's the sentence she'll remember."

### [p13]
They left the proposal on Dr Aydın's desk on Thursday evening. On Friday she said nothing. On Saturday she said nothing. On Sunday morning, when Jonah came down for breakfast, she was sitting at the kitchen table with the proposal in front of her, and it was covered in small red notes.

"Sit down," she said to Jonah. Defne, who had heard her voice from the stairs, came and sat next to him.

"Your science section is interesting," she said to Jonah, "although you write too many long sentences. Your safety section is the best thing in it." She turned a page. "I have made some calls. I told them that we only wanted to complete the survey of 1964. They asked whether we had an engineer. I said we would have one by Wednesday."

Jonah opened his mouth, but nothing came out.

"On Wednesday, we will have the ceilings and stairs checked by Mr Hakan, who has worked in these cities for many years. If he says it is safe, we go down on Thursday. Three people, two hours. If he says it is not safe, we do not go, and I do not want to hear one more word about it. Agreed?"

"Agreed," they said together.

At the window she stopped and looked out at the hill.

"I have heard it too," she said, without turning round. "Many times. If only I had had the time to find out why."

### [p14]
Mr Hakan spent all of Wednesday underground. When he came up, covered in pale dust from his boots to his eyebrows, he said that the stairs were safe, that one ceiling on the sixth level needed wooden supports, and that nobody should touch the stones at the end of the lowest passage. By evening, two workers had put in the supports.

On Thursday morning, the three of them stood at the metal gate on the fourth level. They wore helmets with lamps, and each of them carried a small radio. One of the workers, a quiet young man called Murat, sat down on a chair by the gate with a watch, a rope and a bottle of tea.

"Two hours," said Dr Aydın. "If we are not back, Murat calls for help. If anyone feels unwell, we all turn back. Nobody goes anywhere alone."

Murat opened the lock, and the gate moved with a long, unhappy noise.

The stairs below were steep and narrow, and they had been worn smooth by centuries of use. Jonah went down slowly, one hand on the wall. The air grew cooler with every step. They walked under Mr Hakan's new wooden supports, which smelled fresh in the dusty air.

The song was getting louder. Although there was no wind down here, Jonah could feel the sound in his chest and in the stone under his hand.

### [p15]
The lowest level was different from the others. The passages were wider and higher, and the walls had been cut more carefully, with straight edges and smooth corners.

"Look at the tool marks," said Dr Aydın, shining her lamp on the wall. "These are older than the floors above. Much older, perhaps."

They followed the main passage towards the north side of the hill. The sound was all around them now, low and deep, coming out of the walls, the floor and the ceiling at the same time. It no longer sounded like the hill singing. It sounded like the hill breathing.

Then the passage ended.

In front of them was a pile of stones that reached almost to the ceiling. Some of the stones were large and pale, with sharp new edges. Others were smaller and dark, covered in a thick grey dust that looked much, much older.

Dr Aydın put her hand on the pile, very gently.

"The collapse of 1964," she said. "Mr Hakan was right. We do not touch this."

Jonah turned slowly in a circle, letting his lamp move over the walls. The song seemed to be coming from everywhere and nowhere. And then, to the right of the stones, his light found a low doorway that none of them had noticed.

## [c4] Holes in a Row

### [p16]
The doorway was so low that they had to go through it on their hands and knees. On the other side was a small room with a high, narrow ceiling that disappeared into darkness.

"There it is," said Defne quietly. "The air chimney."

But Jonah was not looking up. He was looking at the north wall.

It was covered in holes. Not natural holes, like the ones that the wind and rain had made on the outside of the hill, but holes in rows, one above the other, cut very carefully into the rock. There were seven rows. The holes at the bottom were as wide as a dinner plate. Each row above had smaller holes, and at the top they were no bigger than a coin.

And the sound was coming out of them.

Each row of holes had its own voice. The bottom row made the deep, long note that Jonah had heard on his first night. The rows above it added softer, higher sounds that moved in and out of each other, like water.

"These were not made by the wind," said Dr Aydın. She was touching the edge of one hole, where small regular marks could still be seen. "These were carved by hand. With great care, and probably over many years."

For a long moment, nobody said anything. The hill went on singing around them.

### [p17]
"It's a flute," Jonah said at last. "No. It's more like an organ. A huge one."

He took out his notebook and drew quickly as he spoke. "The wind goes into the air chimney at the top of the hill, through the dark belt of rock. It comes all the way down here and passes over these holes. Each hole is a different size and a different depth, so each one gives a different note. Then the sound goes back up the chimney, and the whole hill sings it to the valley."

"Like your whistle," said Defne.

"Like my whistle, but the wind is playing it. And it only works when the wind comes from the north, because that's the only direction that can get into the opening at the top. That's why the sound stops the moment the wind stops."

"But why would anyone build something like this?" asked Defne. "It must have taken years. You don't spend years cutting holes in a wall just for music."

Dr Aydın was quiet for a while. When she spoke, she spoke slowly, as if she was putting the pieces together while she talked.

"What does the north wind bring here, in autumn and winter?" she asked.

"Cold," said Defne. And then, more slowly: "Snow."

"Mr Kemal's grandmother," said Jonah. "When the hill sings, bring the animals inside."

### [p18]
They sat on the floor of the little room and talked in low voices, as if they were afraid of disturbing the wall.

"Think about it," said Dr Aydın. "A village in this valley, a thousand years ago or more. No weather reports. No phones. In winter, a storm from the north can bring snow in a few hours. If your sheep and goats are still out on the hills when it arrives, you can lose all of them in one night."

"So they built an alarm," said Defne.

"An alarm that nobody had to watch," said Jonah. "The cold north wind arrives, and the hill tells everyone, even people working far away in the fields. They must have studied the wind for years to get it right."

"And they can't have built this quickly," said Dr Aydın. "Look at the top rows. Those holes were cut later than the bottom ones. The marks are different. Perhaps each generation added a row."

Defne was writing as fast as she could. "If I had known about this last year," she said, "I would have chosen a completely different topic for my final project."

"You still can," said Dr Aydın, and for the first time since Jonah had met her, she smiled properly.

Jonah looked at his watch. They had been underground for an hour and twenty minutes, and the song was getting softer.

### [p19]
"The wind's dropping," said Jonah. One by one, the high voices disappeared, until only the deep note at the bottom was left.

Then that stopped too.

The silence was so complete that Jonah could hear his own heart. Dust floated slowly in the light of their lamps.

"We should go back," said Dr Aydın, getting to her knees. "We have forty minutes, and the stairs are slow."

But Jonah held up his hand.

Very far away, at the edge of hearing, a new sound was beginning. It was not coming from the holes in the north wall. It was coming from behind them, from the other side of the doorway, from the pile of stones in the main passage.

It was a note that none of them had ever heard. It was higher than the others and thinner, as if it was coming through a very small gap, and it shook a little, like a voice that had not sung for a long time.

"The wind has changed," Jonah whispered. "It's coming from the north-east now. Much colder."

"The hill doesn't sing in a north-east wind," said Defne. "You said so. You measured it for a week."

"I know," said Jonah. "Up on the roof, the note always broke and disappeared." He turned his lamp slowly towards the doorway and the pile of stones beyond it. "But down here, we can still hear it."

## [c5] The Second Song

### [p20]
It took eleven days to move the stones.

Nobody did anything quickly. Mr Hakan came back with two more engineers, and they studied the pile for a whole day before they allowed anyone to touch it. Then the stones were removed one by one, by hand, starting from the top. Each one was numbered and photographed. Dr Aydın had the whole process filmed, so that every step could be checked later. Jonah carried stones up the stairs until his arms hurt.

On the fourth day, Defne noticed something. The pale stones with sharp edges, which had fallen in 1964, were only the top layer. Under them, the dark stones were not lying in an untidy heap at all. They had been placed carefully, one on top of another, like a wall.

"Someone closed this on purpose," she said. "Centuries before 1964."

"But why would anyone block their own alarm?" asked Jonah.

Nobody could answer that. However, there were other clues. Among the dark stones, Defne found pieces of a broken pot and a small, worn iron tool. Dr Aydın held the tool up to the light for a long time.

"This might have been dropped by the person who built the wall," she said. "If we can find out its age, we will know when the second voice went silent."

### [p21]
On the last day, the last stone came out.

Behind the wall was a second room, exactly like the first, but turned towards the north-east. Its walls were also covered in rows of holes, smaller and closer together than the others. In the ceiling, a second air chimney went up into the dark. At the top, Jonah could see a round piece of sky, as small as a coin.

"There were two pipes all along," said Defne. "One for the north wind and one for the north-east."

Jonah was already doing the calculations. "The north-east wind is the colder one," he said. "On my graphs, the worst cold always comes from that direction. So one voice means a north wind, and two voices together mean that the really cold wind is arriving. Snow." He sat back on his heels. "It's not just an alarm. It's a weather forecast."

Dr Aydın looked up. "There was a very bad earthquake in this region about three hundred years ago," she said. "Many of these cities were damaged. Perhaps this room was damaged too, and the people closed it because they were afraid it would fall. They must have thought that one voice was better than none."

"And slowly everyone forgot that there had ever been two," said Defne.

"Not everyone," said Jonah. "Mr Kemal's grandmother's grandmother remembered."

### [p22]
The north-east wind came back on the last night of August.

Jonah was on the roof with Defne, Dr Aydın, Mr Kemal and half the team, all wrapped in blankets. Above them the stars were as thick as sand.

It began as usual, with the deep north note. Then the wind moved a little towards the east, and Jonah held his breath. This time, the note did not break. Instead, a second voice rose to join it, higher and clearer than anything he had heard before. The two voices moved around each other and filled the whole valley with a sound that was almost too beautiful to listen to.

Nobody on the roof spoke. In the houses below, lights came on, one after another. People came out into the streets and stood looking up at the hill.

Mr Kemal was crying, very quietly. He said something in Turkish, and Defne put her hand on his arm.

"He says his grandmother told him about this when he was six," she translated. "He wishes she could hear it. He thought it was just a story."

Then the old man wiped his eyes, stood up, and said something else, much louder, to everyone on the roof.

Defne laughed. "He says that two voices mean real snow. He says we should bring the animals in."

Three days later, the first cold rain fell on the valley, and the tops of the far mountains turned white.

### [p23]
Dr Aydın's report was sent to the ministry in October. It described the singing hill, the two air chimneys and the rows of holes in careful detail. At the top of the first page, under her own name, there were two more: Jonah Price and Defne Kaya.

Jonah read it in his room in Wales, on a grey afternoon, with rain against the window. By then he had been back at university for three weeks, and the valley already felt like a dream.

Defne had sent him a message with it. The iron tool has been tested, she wrote. It's about three hundred years old, just as the Doctor guessed. Mr Kemal says hello. He has had his roof repaired, and he wants to know when you are coming back. There is talk of opening the lowest level to visitors, with a guide. If that happens, I'll be working there next summer. Think about it.

Jonah thought about it all evening.

Then he took the small wooden whistle out of his desk, opened the window, and played two notes into the wet Welsh wind: one low and long, one higher and clear, moving around each other like old friends.

This time next year, he thought, I'll be standing on that roof again, listening.

Somewhere far away, he was sure, the wind had already turned north.
