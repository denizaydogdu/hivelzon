## [c1] The Diary

### [p01]
My great-aunt's diary described eleven bridges, but only ten of them were on any map.

I found it on a wet Sunday in June, in my mother's loft, inside a shoebox held together with brown tape. Under the diary lay a pair of cycling gloves gone stiff with age, a folded map of the River Ostrel so old that the creases had worn into holes, and a long envelope which I didn't open, because some things in other people's boxes are none of your business.

The diary was small and green, and the damp of several decades had swollen it to twice its natural thickness. On the first page, in a neat, upright hand, someone had written: Hedda Vogt, summer 1962, Brenning to the sea.

My great-aunt Hedda was eighty-nine. In three weeks she would be ninety, and the family had spent a month arguing about the cake.

I sat on a crate under the sloping roof and turned the pages. They were full of the ordinary business of a long journey: a puncture, a rude farmer, a plate of fried fish that had apparently changed her life. And every few pages there was a bridge.

They were drawn in soft pencil, with a care that made me sit up. A stone arch with every crumbling block shaded separately. A wooden footbridge with the grain of the planks showing. An iron span with each rivet a tiny dot. Rarely had I seen anyone draw a bridge so honestly, and I spend most of my working life looking at drawings of bridges.

In the corner of each drawing there was a small number inside a circle. One, two, three, and so on, up to eleven.

I counted them twice. Then I unfolded the old map and counted the bridges marked along the river between Brenning and the coast. There were ten.

### [p02]
I should explain that I am a civil engineer. I work for a firm that designs roads, drains and, when we are lucky, the occasional bridge. Most of what I do involves spreadsheets and arguing with people who want things to be cheaper than physics allows.

So it was with a professional eye, as well as a curious one, that I went through the diary again that evening on my kitchen table. Bridge one was the old packhorse bridge in Brenning itself, which I had crossed a thousand times as a child without ever looking at it properly. Bridges two to ten followed the river south, down to the wide, flat country where the Ostrel spreads itself out into the delta and finally gives up and becomes the sea.

Bridge eleven was different. It had no place name, no description and no story about lunch. There was only the drawing, a few small figures in the margin that I took for dates or distances, and a single line underneath, written more firmly than anything else in the book: Number eleven. Here, one day.

I looked at it for a long time. It was a long, low bridge with three spans, resting on two slender piers, and it was beautiful in the slightly unreal way that buildings in architects' brochures are beautiful. There was no river bank in the picture, no trees, no cows. The bridge simply stood on a ruled line, as if it were floating in the air.

A dream, I decided. A young woman on a long bicycle ride, imagining the perfect bridge at the end of it. I didn't give it much more thought than that.

That turned out to be a mistake, though not the last one I would make.

### [p03]
Hedda lived in a small ground-floor flat near the river in Brenning, with a view of the water, a cat called Mortimer who disliked everyone, and a bookcase that was mostly maps. I went round on Tuesday after work with the diary in a plastic bag, feeling oddly guilty, as if I had been reading her letters.

She was sitting by the window doing a crossword in pen. When I was nine she told me that pencils were for people who expected to be wrong.

"You found the diary," she said, before I had taken my coat off.

"How did you know?"

"You're holding that bag as if it contained a live animal. Sit down. Mortimer, move."

Mortimer didn't move, so I sat on the edge of the sofa. I told her about the shoebox, the gloves and the drawings. I told her that I thought they were wonderful, which was true, and then I told her my idea, which had arrived somewhere between the loft and the kettle.

"I want to ride it," I said. "The same route, the same eleven bridges. I'll find each one and draw it as it is now, next to your drawing as it was then. A book of both. For your birthday."

She looked at me over her glasses for a long moment. It is said that my great-aunt once silenced an entire planning committee simply by looking at it, and I could see how that might have happened.

"Three weeks," she said. "With a full-time job."

"I've got leave I was supposed to take last year. My manager keeps sending me emails about it."

"And a bicycle?"

"I'll borrow one."

She put down the crossword. Then she smiled, which she does not do often, and said something I didn't understand until much later.

"Some bridges you find," she said. "Some find you."

### [p04]
Before I left that evening, I asked her about the eleventh bridge.

"Where is it? It's the only one without a name."

Hedda took the diary from me and opened it at the right page without searching, which I noticed only afterwards. She looked at the drawing the way people look at old photographs of themselves, with affection and a certain amount of disbelief.

"It's at the end," she said. "You'll get there."

"It isn't on the map."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

And that was all I could get out of her. On my second attempt Mortimer bit me, which I chose to interpret as the end of the conversation.

On the way home I remembered something my mother had told me years ago, which I had filed away with all the other family facts I had never examined. Hedda had studied engineering at the technical college in the early sixties. She had been good at it, apparently very good. And then she had spent forty years working in the drawing office of a firm that made agricultural machinery, and as far as anyone knew she had never designed, let alone built, a single bridge.

I had always assumed that she had simply lost interest. People do. Having spent six years of my own life on an engineering degree, I could easily imagine wanting to do something else with the rest of it.

That night I spread the old map on my bed and traced the river south with my finger, bridge by bridge, from Brenning to the delta. One to ten fitted neatly. Eleven didn't fit anywhere at all.

What I needed, I decided, was not a better map. What I needed was to get on a bicycle and look.

I booked the leave the next morning. My manager replied within four minutes, with three exclamation marks, which I believe was the most emotional email he had ever sent.

## [c2] Stone, Wood, Iron

### [p05]
I set off from Brenning on a bright Saturday morning, eighteen days before Hedda's birthday, on a bicycle that belonged to my neighbour's son. He had told me by text that I could use it as long as I didn't "do anything weird to the gears". I had very little idea what the gears were for.

The plan was simple, at least on paper. The route Hedda had taken was a little under three hundred kilometres, following the river all the way. If I rode for nine or ten days and spent a few hours at each bridge, I would be back with nearly a week to spare. In that week I would turn the drawings into a book.

Bridge one took me twenty minutes, since it was three streets from my flat. I sat on the low wall beside it with the diary open on my knee and my sketchbook on the other. The packhorse bridge was exactly as Hedda had drawn it: two narrow arches of grey stone, so steep in the middle that the old carts must have needed a push. Beneath it the river ran fast and brown after a week of rain, gushing over the weir a little way downstream.

What surprised me was not the bridge but my drawing of it. Hedda's version breathed. Mine looked like something that had been through a planning department. Every line was correct and nothing was alive.

"That's very neat," said a small boy who had stopped to watch, with the honesty of the very young. "Is it supposed to be boring?"

"It's supposed to be accurate," I said.

"My gran draws ducks," he said, and walked off, as if that settled the matter.

I rubbed out half the arch and tried again. It wasn't much better, but it was a little less sure of itself, which I suspected was the point.

### [p06]
By midday I had left Brenning behind and was following the towpath south between fields of young wheat. The river widened and slowed. Herons stood in the shallows like retired officials, refusing to be impressed by anything.

Bridge two was at Odderby, a village made almost entirely of one long street and a shop that sold everything from stamps to fishing bait. The bridge was a wooden footbridge on stone feet, covered with a little pitched roof to keep the rain off the planks. Hedda had drawn it in the early evening, judging by the long shadows, and had written underneath: Roof leaks. Bread excellent.

The roof, I am happy to report, no longer leaked. The bread I didn't try, as the shop was closed for lunch.

An elderly man with a very fat spaniel was leaning on the rail. He watched me compare the diary with the bridge for some minutes before he spoke.

"Same bridge," he said. "Everyone asks. Same bridge."

"It looks the same," I said. "But surely the timber has been replaced."

"Every plank," he agreed cheerfully. "Some of them twice. Roof's new. Rails are new. Stone's the same, mind you." He patted the rail as if it were his dog. "My grandfather used to say it was like his old axe. New head, new handle, still his axe."

It was a better summary of bridge maintenance than anything I had read in a professional journal, and I wrote it in the margin of my sketchbook.

Having finished my drawing, I showed him Hedda's. He studied it for a long time, holding it at arm's length, and then he handed it back with a look of quiet respect.

"Whoever did that," he said, "knew how things stand up."

I thought about that sentence for the rest of the afternoon. It is a strange thing to be told about your own family by a stranger with a spaniel.

### [p07]
Bridge three was at Kerrow, where the river passes under an old iron railway bridge that no longer carries any trains. The line had closed decades ago, and the bridge now carried a cycle path, two benches and an impressive amount of moss. Hedda had drawn it with a train halfway across, a cloud of steam rising from the funnel, and the comment: Waved. Driver waved back. A good day.

When I arrived, the bridge was wrapped in scaffolding and plastic sheeting, and a woman in an orange jacket was standing on the bank with a clipboard, frowning at it. I recognised the frown immediately. It was my own.

I asked if it was an inspection.

"Corrosion survey," she said. "There's significant section loss on the lower chord at the third panel point, and the bearings at the south abutment haven't moved freely since about 1975."

"So it's a bit rusty underneath," I said, "and it's got stiff knees."

She looked at me properly for the first time and laughed. "You're an engineer."

"I'm afraid so."

Her name was Priya, and she worked for the county. We spent a happy half hour discussing paint systems. I showed her the diary. She turned the pages carefully, stopping at each drawing.

"These aren't sketches," she said finally. "Look at the iron one. She's counted the rivets. She's even drawn the expansion joint, and nobody draws the expansion joint unless they know what it's for."

"She trained as an engineer," I said.

"Did she? What did she build?"

"Nothing, as far as I know."

Priya handed the diary back and looked at the bridge in its plastic coat. "That seems a waste," she said simply.

I didn't answer. Not only had I never asked Hedda that question, but I had never even thought of it as a question.

### [p08]
I reached Tamsby on the second afternoon, sunburnt on one arm only, which is what happens when the river runs south and you don't think to turn round.

According to the diary, bridge four was an old stone bridge of five arches, with a little shelter halfway across where travellers could step aside to let carts pass. Hedda had drawn it from the bank on a misty morning, and it was one of the loveliest pictures in the book: the arches reflected in still water, so that each one became a perfect circle.

There was no stone bridge at Tamsby. In its place was a wide, flat concrete road bridge, built, according to a small metal plate on the parapet, in 1971. It was perfectly sensible and completely without charm, and I knew exactly how its designers had felt about it, because I have designed several just like it.

I stood on the pavement with the diary open and felt something close to grief, which was ridiculous. I had never seen the old bridge. Until a week ago I hadn't known it existed.

I asked in the café on the corner. A man at the next table thought the old bridge had been "knocked down for the lorries", which I could have guessed. Nobody knew where the stone had gone.

Engineers don't throw away good stone. In 1971 it would have been worth something, and somebody would have carted it off and used it. Bridges, like old axes, have a way of continuing in other forms.

I bought a cup of tea, sat by the window, and looked at Hedda's drawing of the five arches until the light outside began to fade.

Somewhere in Tamsby, I was sure of it, there was something left of the bridge she had drawn. All I had to do was find it.

## [c3] The Knee

### [p09]
In the end, it was a dog that found it.

I was walking back to my bed and breakfast the next morning, eating a pastry and feeling sorry for myself, when a large grey dog dragged its owner across the road to inspect my breakfast. While the owner apologised, I explained what I was looking for.

"Oh, the old bridge," she said, as if I had asked for the post office. "My grandfather bought the little shelter off the council. Stone by stone. He rebuilt it at the end of our lane as a bus stop."

It was exactly where she said it would be, at the edge of town by a field of cabbages. Four low walls of grey stone, a stone bench worn smooth by two centuries of waiting, and a tiled roof that was considerably newer than the rest. Two schoolgirls sat on the bench looking at their phones, entirely unaware that they were sitting in the middle of a river that was no longer there.

I compared it with the diary. In Hedda's drawing, the shelter sat halfway along the bridge, with the same rounded corners and the same stone bench. I could even see the shape of a crack she had drawn in one of the blocks, now sixty-four years older and a little wider.

I drew it standing up, while the schoolgirls watched me with polite suspicion. When the bus came, they got on it, and I like to think that somewhere in the back of their minds they felt a faint sensation of crossing water.

Bridge five, at Lindhaven, was a slender suspension footbridge that swung gently when I walked across it. It had barely changed. I drew it in an hour and began to think, with the dangerous confidence of someone halfway through a plan, that the whole thing was going rather well.

Then it started to rain.

### [p10]
It was not ordinary rain. It was the kind of rain that seems to have been waiting all spring for a personal opportunity. Within ten minutes the towpath had turned into a brown stream and my jacket had stopped pretending to be waterproof.

I should have stopped in Lindhaven. I knew I should, in the calm, professional part of my mind that writes risk assessments for other people. But Pellow's Ford was only fifteen kilometres away, and I had booked a room there, and I didn't want to lose a day.

I lost four.

The accident itself was so undramatic that I was almost embarrassed by it. Coming down a short slope into the village, I braked on a patch of wet cobbles. The back wheel slid sideways, slowly and with great dignity, and I went over with it. My left knee met the edge of a stone kerb with a sound I can still hear.

For a while I simply lay in the rain and considered my life choices. Then a car stopped, and a man got out and asked me, very carefully, whether I was all right.

"I'm fine," I said.

"You're bleeding through your trousers."

"Apart from that."

He drove me and the bicycle to the doctor's surgery. The doctor, a brisk woman who clearly regarded cyclists as a seasonal pest, examined my knee and told me that nothing was broken but that a ligament had been badly strained.

"Rest it," she said. "A week, ideally."

"I've got sixteen days."

"Then rest it for four," she said, "and don't do anything stupid for the other twelve."

If I had stopped in Lindhaven, I would not now be sitting in a strange village with my leg on a cushion.

### [p11]
Bridge six no longer existed. According to the diary it had been a long timber bridge on wooden legs, which Hedda had drawn in great detail and labelled: Shaky. Charming. Would not trust it in a flood. She was right not to. It is believed to have been swept away in the great flood of 1968, along with a barn, several sheep and, according to local legend, a piano.

In its place there was a ferry. Not a large one, but a flat, square steel platform that pulled itself across the river on a chain, carrying cars, cyclists and the occasional cow. The ferryman was called Ilan. He was somewhere in his seventies, with a white beard and the forearms of a man who had pulled a great deal of chain.

For four days I limped down to the landing every morning and sat on an upturned boat while he worked. On the second day he started bringing me coffee. On the third day he started talking.

Ilan knew everything about the river. He knew the stories his father had told him, and the stories his father's father had told, and he told them all with the same slow pleasure, as if they had happened that morning.

"My father ran this ferry before me," he said, when I showed him the diary. "Nineteen sixty-two, you say?" He looked at the drawing of the old timber bridge. "Then she'd have crossed this bridge, not the ferry. But I'll tell you something. My father used to talk about a girl on a bicycle that summer. Stopped for three days. Measured the river with a length of string and a stone."

"Measured it?"

"How deep, how fast, how wide. He thought she was mad. Polite, mind you. But mad." He smiled. "He liked her."

I wrote it down. At the time I thought it was just a nice story.

### [p12]
On the fourth evening, when my knee was finally willing to bend without complaining, Ilan told me about the end of the river.

"You're going all the way down to the mouth?" he asked. "Wrenmouth?"

I said I was. The diary ended in the delta, and the last named bridge, number ten, was at a place called Saltings, a few kilometres short of the coast.

"Pretty in summer," Ilan said. "But the river closes in winter, down there. The water comes up with the tides and the storms, and the ferry stops from November to March. The people on the far bank have to go the long way round, nearly two hours, for the doctor or the market or the school." He shook his head. "My father used to say someone ought to build them a bridge. Everybody's said it. Nobody's done it."

"Why not?"

"Too wide, they say. Too soft underneath. Too expensive." He shrugged. "There's always a reason not to."

The next morning I set off again, slowly, with my knee strapped up and a bag of Ilan's apples. "You won't fall off again?" he asked.

"I hope not," I said.

He waved me off from the middle of the river, as if I were a ship.

Bridge seven was at Harl, half a day's ride on, a long brick viaduct of fourteen arches crossing the whole valley. Opposite it stood an old inn with a garden, and on the wall of the bar there was a faded photograph of two young cyclists standing in front of the viaduct.

One of them was a tall young man with a lot of dark hair. The other was a young woman in a checked shirt, laughing, with a long cardboard tube under her arm.

I knew that face. I had grown up with it.

It was Hedda, at twenty-six. And I had no idea who the young man was.

## [c4] The Festival

### [p13]
I arrived in Ambrey on midsummer's eve to find the whole village hanging lanterns on a bridge.

Bridge eight was a wooden footbridge, painted white, linking the village green to a meadow on the far bank. Strings of paper lanterns had been looped along both rails, and a brass band was practising something that might well have been a waltz, though it was difficult to be certain.

I pushed my bicycle onto the green and asked a woman carrying a tray of sausage rolls what was going on.

"The Bridge Fair," she said, as if that explained everything. "Every year since 1963. Have a sausage roll."

I had a sausage roll. Then I showed her the diary, because by now I showed it to everybody, and the effect was extraordinary. She looked at the drawing of the white footbridge, then at the name on the first page, and put down the tray.

"Vogt," she said. "You're not related to the engineer girl?"

Within twenty minutes I was sitting in a deckchair in the middle of the green with a cup of tea, a slice of lemon cake and a small crowd around me, and an elderly lady called Margit was telling the story in a voice that suggested she had told it many times before.

In the summer of 1962, a young woman on a bicycle had stopped in Ambrey for the night. In the morning, she had walked across the footbridge, stopped halfway, jumped up and down twice, and then gone straight to the village hall to tell the committee that the main beam was rotten and that the bridge would fall into the river within a year.

"Nobody believed her, of course," said Margit. "A girl, telling the committee about beams. But my father went down with a knife and pushed it into the wood. It went in like butter."

### [p14]
They had replaced the beam that autumn. And the following summer, so that nobody would forget how close they had come to falling in, they had held a small party on the new bridge. It had been held every summer since.

"We call it the Bridge Fair," said Margit, "but between ourselves, the old people still call it Hedda's Day."

I didn't trust myself to speak, so I ate the lemon cake instead.

It was Margit who mentioned Tomasz. He had come to Ambrey with Hedda that summer, she said, and had come back some years later to marry a local girl, and had never left. He was ninety now and lived in the house with the blue door at the end of the green, and he would not forgive me if I went away without visiting him.

I found him in his garden, sitting among his beehives in a wide straw hat. When I said my name, he took off the hat, looked at me very carefully, and said, "You have her chin. I'm sorry. It's not a compliment."

It was the tall young man from the photograph in Harl, of course, sixty-four years later, a little bent but with the same amount of hair, now entirely white. Over tea in a kitchen full of honey jars, he told me how he had met my great-aunt.

"We were at the technical college together," he said. "Civil engineering. Forty of us in the class. Thirty-nine young men and Hedda."

"Was that difficult for her?"

He thought about it. "Some of the lecturers behaved as if she had come into the wrong room by mistake," he said. "And some of the students. Not all. She was the best in the year. That did not make it easier."

"And you two were friends?"

"We were the two who liked drawing more than talking," he said. "So we drew. We did our projects side by side, every one of them, until the last."

### [p15]
I asked him what had happened with the last one.

Tomasz looked out of the window at his bees for a while, as if consulting them.

"That is Hedda's story," he said at last. "I was there for some of it. But I would rather you heard it from her. She tells it better, and she will be very cross with me if I tell it first."

"She hasn't told me anything. She just smiles and says things like 'Some bridges you find.'"

He laughed so suddenly that he spilt his tea. "She has been saying that for sixty years," he said. "It was not always a joke."

I would rather he had told me. I said so. He nodded sympathetically and offered me honey, and I realised that he had no intention of saying any more about it, and that if I pushed, he would simply become very old and very deaf.

So instead I showed him the diary, page by page, and he turned them with the delicate fingers of a man who has spent his life with bees. When he reached the eleventh drawing, he stopped and looked at it for a long time without speaking.

"Do you know where this is?"

"Do you?"

"No. There's no place name. Only those little figures in the corner. I thought they were dates."

He tapped the page. "Hedda never wrote a date in her life without the year," he said. "Look again. What else could they be?"

I looked. Two groups of figures, three and three, written very small. I had been looking at them for two weeks without seeing them.

"A grid reference," I said slowly.

Tomasz smiled and said nothing. Then he put a jar of honey into my bag and told me to go and enjoy the fair before the band got any worse.

### [p16]
The band did get worse, as it happened, but by then nobody minded. There was dancing on the green, and at dusk someone lit the lanterns along the bridge, and their reflections lay in the dark water like a second row of lanterns under the first. I sat on the bank with my knee stretched out in front of me and drew bridge eight by lantern light. It was the first drawing of the whole journey that I was pleased with.

The next morning I rode on to bridge nine, a small iron bridge over a lock at a place called Mardle, where a lock-keeper with a remarkable moustache let me sit in his garden to draw it. Hardly had I finished the drawing when he brought out a pot of tea and a map, and asked me, in the tone of a man who has very little to do, where I was heading next.

"Saltings," I said. "Bridge ten. And then I'm looking for this."

I read him the grid reference. He unfolded the map on the garden table, ran one large finger along the edges and down, and then stopped. He frowned. He moved his finger slightly, checked again, and frowned harder.

"You've got that wrong," he said.

"I've checked it four times."

"Then she got it wrong." He turned the map round so that I could see. "That's not a bridge. That's not a road, or a village, or anything at all. That's the middle of the river, just below Wrenmouth, where it's nearly half a kilometre wide." He sat back. "There's nothing there but water."

I looked at the spot under his finger. A broad grey ribbon on the map, with no lines crossing it.

Then I looked at the drawing in the diary: a long, low bridge, three spans on two slender piers, standing exactly where nothing had ever stood.

## [c5] The Delta

### [p17]
Below Mardle the country flattens out as if someone had ironed it. The trees give up first, then the hedges, and finally even the hills on the horizon, until there is nothing but reeds, sky and long straight dykes with the river sliding between them, wide and brown and in no hurry at all.

It is said that you can see the weather coming in the delta a full day before it arrives. What nobody tells you is that you can also feel the wind coming, all of it, directly into your face, for the entire day. By the afternoon my knee had begun to ache in a dull, steady way that suggested it was keeping a list of complaints for later. I had been riding for nine days, and for the first time I began to wonder whether the whole idea had been a mistake.

Left alone with the wind and the reeds, I found myself having long, rather unfair conversations with my great-aunt in my head. Why hadn't she told me? Why the smiling and the riddles? If she had simply explained the eleventh bridge in her flat that evening, I would now be at home with a cup of tea and two working knees.

The honest answer, I suspected, was that I hadn't asked the right question. But that is not the kind of answer you want on a bicycle, in a headwind, with forty kilometres still to go.

I reached Saltings at six in the evening. Bridge ten was a small iron swing bridge over a tidal creek, painted green and operated by hand. An old man in a cap was turning the handle to let a sailing boat through, and the whole bridge pivoted slowly on its centre, as graceful as a door in a dream.

### [p18]
I drew it the next morning, with my back against a bollard and the tide running out under the bridge. It was the tenth drawing, and when I laid it next to Hedda's, I found that I no longer minded that mine were worse. They were mine. That seemed, after nine days, to be the point.

In the afternoon I phoned her. I had been sending her photographs every evening, along with short, cheerful messages that left out the knee, the rain and the night I had spent in a bed and breakfast that smelt of boiled cabbage.

"You've hurt yourself," she said, before I had finished saying hello.

"I haven't."

"You're breathing like a person who has hurt herself. Is it the knee?"

There seemed no point in lying to a woman who could diagnose injuries over the phone. "A bit," I admitted. "It's fine now. Mostly."

"You needn't have come all this way for me," she said. "You do know that?"

"I wanted to. I met Tomasz."

There was a pause on the line, long enough for me to wonder whether the signal had gone.

"How is he?" she said eventually.

"Old. Keeps bees. Said I have your chin and apologised for it."

She laughed, a short surprised laugh. "So did I, once," she said. "Apologise for it, I mean. Did he tell you anything?"

"He said it was your story."

"Good," said Hedda, and I could hear that she was smiling. "He always was the sensible one."

"I'm going to Wrenmouth tomorrow," I said. "To the grid reference."

Another pause. Then, very lightly: "Take a good look," she said, and rang off.

### [p19]
Wrenmouth is the last town on the river before the sea, and it has the slightly surprised air of a place that knows it is the end of the road. A row of tall, narrow houses painted in faded colours faces the water. There is a harbour full of small fishing boats, a café, a shop, a primary school and a great many gulls, who regard the whole town as their property and the residents as staff.

The river here is enormous. At high tide it is nearly half a kilometre from bank to bank, a great sheet of moving grey water. On the far side, very small, you can see a scatter of houses, a water tower and a line of trees. That, I learnt, was Holm, which is technically part of Wrenmouth but might as well be on another planet for five months of the year.

I followed the grid reference along the quay, checking the map on my phone every few steps, until the little blue dot and the coordinates met. I was standing at the top of a concrete slipway that ran down into the water, beside a small wooden hut and a noticeboard.

It was a ferry landing. Out in the river, a small yellow ferry was making its way back from Holm with three cars and a tractor on board.

That was it. That was all there was. Not a stone, not a pier, not a single post sticking out of the mud to suggest that anything had ever been built here or ever would be. I had cycled nearly three hundred kilometres, with one knee, to find a ferry.

### [p20]
I went into the hut, because there was nowhere else to go. Inside, a young man in a fleece was selling tickets and eating crisps with equal concentration.

"Return to Holm?" he asked.

"No. Thank you. I just wondered," I said, and then didn't know how to finish the sentence. "Has there ever been a bridge here?"

He looked at me as if I had asked whether there had ever been a palace. "A bridge? Here?" He waved a crisp at the window, at the half kilometre of grey water outside. "No. There's the ferry."

"And it runs all year?"

"April to October," he said. "Stops in November. Weather's too rough, and the tides. Starts again in spring."

"And in winter? How do people get across?"

"They don't, much." He shrugged. "They drive round by the road bridge upriver. It's about an hour and three quarters. The kids from Holm get a bus to school here at half six in the morning. My cousin lives over there. She says winter's like living on an island where somebody's taken away the boat."

I thanked him and went back out to the slipway. The ferry had docked, and the three cars and the tractor were rolling off, one by one, up the concrete past me.

I sat down on the low wall beside the hut and took out the diary. I opened it at the eleventh drawing: the long, low bridge, three spans on two slender piers, floating on its single ruled line.

Here, one day, Hedda had written.

I looked from the page to the river, and from the river back to the page, and for the first time on the whole journey I had absolutely no idea what to do next.

## [c6] Nothing There

### [p21]
I spent the next two days looking for a bridge that did not exist, which is harder than it sounds, because you never know when to stop.

I walked the quay from one end to the other at high tide and again at low tide. At low tide the river shrank back into a narrower channel, leaving wide shining banks of mud on either side, and in the middle, roughly opposite the slipway, a long hump of gravel rose out of the water like the back of a sleeping animal. The gulls stood on it in rows, facing the wind.

"The Bank," said the woman who ran the café, when I asked. "It's always there. Moves about a bit, but it never goes. The fishermen hate it."

I asked her about bridges. She had never heard of one. Neither had the harbour master, the man in the shop, or two fishermen mending a net on the quay, who discussed the question at length and concluded that a bridge would be "a nice idea" and "never happen", in that order.

I went to the little library above the post office and spent an afternoon with the local history shelf. There were books about the great flood of 1968, the old fishing fleet and a famous storm in which a whale had been washed onto the quay. There was not a single mention of a bridge.

By the evening of the second day I had walked so far on my bad knee that it had begun to make small clicking noises, as if it were trying to send me a message in code. I sat on the wall by the ferry hut and watched the last crossing of the day come back from Holm.

### [p22]
A woman got off the ferry with a bicycle and a large bag of exercise books, and sat down on the other end of the wall to put on her cycle clips. She looked at my bicycle, then at my strapped knee, and then at the diary on my lap.

"You're the bridge woman," she said. "The café told me."

Her name was Ms Dunmore. She was the head teacher of the primary school, and nearly half her pupils lived in Holm.

"In summer they come across on the ferry, ten minutes," she said. "In winter they come by bus. Half past six in the morning, an hour and three quarters each way, in the dark." She shook her head. "The little ones fall asleep on it. Some of them have never seen their own village in daylight on a school day between November and March."

I showed her the drawing. She looked at it for a while.

"People have been talking about a bridge here for as long as I can remember," she said. "Every few years the council looks at it. And every few years they say the same thing. The riverbed is too soft, all mud, except for the Bank in the middle. A bridge would need to stand on the Bank, and nobody has ever come up with a design that could." She handed the diary back. "So we have a ferry that stops in winter, and a lot of tired children."

"I'm sorry," I said, though I wasn't sure what for.

"Don't be." She stood up and tucked the exercise books under her arm. "It's a lovely drawing. Whoever made it must have wanted it very much."

She cycled off along the quay. I looked at the drawing again and felt nothing but tired.

### [p23]
That night, in my room above the café, I made a list of what I had achieved. It was a short list.

I had cycled most of the way down the River Ostrel. I had drawn ten bridges, or nine and a bus shelter, with varying degrees of success. I had met a man with a spaniel, a ferryman, a village full of lanterns and a beekeeper who had known my great-aunt at twenty-six. I had injured one knee and burnt one arm. And I had come to the end of a diary in search of a bridge that, as far as I could tell, had never been anything but a pencil drawing of a wish.

Never had I felt quite so foolish about a project. And I have, in my time, worked on a footbridge that was built two metres too short.

The worst of it was the birthday. There were six days left. I had imagined a book of then and now, eleven pairs of drawings, with the eleventh as a triumphant ending. Now the ending was a photograph of a ferry hut and a man eating crisps.

If I had asked Hedda straight out, back in her flat, what the eleventh bridge really was, I would not now be sitting in a strange town feeling like an idiot. I knew that perfectly well. But I had wanted to find it myself, as if it were a test, and it seemed I had failed it.

It was high time I went home, I told myself. I could take the train back from Wrenmouth in the morning, finish the ten drawings properly, and give her those. Ten good bridges. It was a perfectly respectable present.

I would rather have found a ruin, though. A heap of old stones, a rotten post in the mud, anything at all. Anything but water.

### [p24]
I phoned her at nine, which is late for Hedda. She answered on the first ring, as if she had been sitting beside the phone, which, knowing her, she probably had.

"Well?" she said.

"There's nothing there," I said. "I'm sorry. I've looked everywhere. I've walked up and down that river until my knee is making noises. There's a ferry, and a gravel bank, and a lot of very tired schoolchildren, and that's all. I must have misunderstood the diary. Or the grid reference is wrong."

"The grid reference is not wrong," said Hedda, with some dignity.

"Then there's nothing to find. I'm coming home tomorrow. I'll finish the other ten for your birthday. They're good, some of them. You'll like the bus shelter."

There was a long silence. I could hear the clock in her flat ticking, and Mortimer complaining somewhere in the background.

"Lena," she said. "Have you looked at the drawing?"

"I've looked at it every day for nearly three weeks."

"No," said Hedda. "You've looked at it as my great-niece. You've looked at it as a nice old picture in a nice old book, by a girl on a bicycle, a long time ago." Her voice was quite gentle, but there was something underneath it that I had never heard before, something that sounded very much like sixty-four years of patience coming to an end.

"Look at the drawing again," she said. "As an engineer."

And she put the phone down.

## [c7] The Eleventh Bridge

### [p25]
I didn't sleep. I sat on the bed with the diary under the lamp, a ruler borrowed from the café and my laptop, and I looked at the drawing as if somebody were paying me to.

The first thing I noticed was the line. I had always thought of it as the ground, a single ruled line for the bridge to stand on. But there was a second line under it, so faint that I had taken it for shading, curving down in the middle and rising again at each side. Not the ground. The water level, and beneath it, the riverbed.

Then the tick marks. Along the ruled line, every few millimetres, there was a tiny upright stroke. I measured them. They were exactly evenly spaced. It was a scale.

Then the figures in the margin, the ones I had dismissed as dates. Beneath the grid reference there were three more numbers, written very small. I checked them against the scale. They were the lengths of the three spans, in feet. The two piers stood precisely where the gravel Bank rose out of the river, with their foundations drawn going down through the mud into the harder ground beneath. Under the deck, a row of tiny arrows that I had taken for decoration marked the loads.

Only then did I understand what I had been carrying in my bag for two weeks. It was not a sketch. It was not a wish. It was a general arrangement drawing, the kind I produce at work every month, folded down to the size of a diary page by someone who had known exactly what she was doing.

Not only had Hedda drawn a bridge across the river at Wrenmouth; she had designed one. In 1962, at the age of twenty-six, she had worked out how to stand a bridge on the one piece of firm ground that everyone said was useless.

And nobody had built it.

### [p26]
I phoned her at seven in the morning. She answered on the first ring again.

"You designed it," I said.

"Good morning to you too," said Hedda. "Yes."

She told me the rest quite simply, as if it were someone else's story. In her final year at the technical college, every student had to design a real structure for a real place. Tomasz chose a multi-storey car park. Most of the class chose roads. Hedda chose the crossing at Wrenmouth, because she had heard about the children on the winter bus from a girl in her lodgings who had grown up in Holm.

That summer she cycled the whole river, studying every bridge on the way, stone, wood and iron, to learn how each of them stood up and how each of them failed. At Pellow's Ford she practised measuring the current with a string and a stone. At Wrenmouth she hired a rowing boat for a week and measured everything else.

"It was the best work I ever did," she said. "I knew it was."

The committee had disagreed. The letter, which had been waiting in the shoebox under the gloves all this time, described her design as unnecessarily ambitious and the crossing itself as unlikely ever to be required. It ended by suggesting that Miss Vogt might find herself better suited to work in a drawing office.

"So I went to a drawing office," said Hedda. "For forty years. I was very good at it." A pause. "Tomasz got a distinction for his car park. He has been apologising for it ever since."

"You should have told me."

"I might well have," she said. "But I wanted you to see it yourself. I wanted somebody, just once, to look at it and see a bridge." She cleared her throat. "And I would rather you didn't feel sorry for me. It's very bad for my blood pressure. You're not going to cry, are you?"

"I'd rather not," I said, not altogether truthfully.

### [p27]
I had five days. I did not take the train home.

Instead, I set up in the corner of the café with my laptop, the diary and an endless supply of tea, and I went through Hedda's calculations line by line, the way I would check the work of a junior engineer. It was a strange experience, a little like being marked by your own grandmother. Her arithmetic was faultless. Her assumptions were sensible for 1962. What had changed was the world around them.

Lorries were heavier now. The flood of 1968 had raised every design level on the river. The Bank had moved about thirty metres downstream in sixty-four years, which ought to have been a disaster, except that Hedda had made her piers wide enough to allow for exactly that kind of movement. Hardly anyone would have thought of it in 1962.

I updated the loads, replaced her riveted steel with modern welded sections, and redrew the whole thing properly. It took three days and most of three nights. By the end of it, I was sure of one thing: the bridge would stand.

It was Ms Dunmore who got me into the town council. She knew the clerk, the clerk knew the mayor, and on the Thursday evening I stood in a small hall above the harbour office and explained to nine councillors, two reporters and a dog how to cross half a kilometre of soft river.

"In short," I said, having spent twenty minutes on bearing pressures, "the piers sit on the Bank. And the Bank isn't going anywhere."

"So it's possible," said one councillor, slowly, "after all."

"It was always possible," I said. "Since 1962."

The council voted that night to examine the design formally and commission a full study. The clerk wrote the letter the next morning, signed it, and put it into my hands.

### [p28]
Hedda's ninetieth birthday was held in her flat, since she refused to go anywhere else, with the window open onto the river and Mortimer hiding under the bed in protest. The cake, after a month of family argument, was lemon. Tomasz came down from Ambrey with three jars of honey and a tie he had last worn in 1971.

I gave her the book after the cake. I had had it printed in Wrenmouth, on good paper: eleven pairs of drawings, hers on the left, mine on the right. She turned the pages slowly. She laughed at the bus shelter. At the white footbridge in Ambrey she stopped, and Tomasz put his hand on her shoulder and said nothing at all.

The last pair was the eleventh bridge. On the left, her pencil drawing from 1962. On the right, mine, the same bridge, the same three spans, standing on the same Bank, but drawn as it would be built now.

Folded inside the back cover were two letters. One was old and yellow, in a long envelope she recognised at once. The other was new.

She read the new one twice. Then she took off her glasses and looked out of the window at the river for a long time, the way she had once looked at a planning committee, and nobody in the room said a word.

"They're going to look at it," she said at last.

"They're going to look at it properly," I said. "It might take years. It might never happen."

"I know," said Hedda. "I'm an engineer."

She closed the book and held it on her lap with both hands, as if it were heavier than it looked.

"Some bridges you find," she said. "Some find you." She smiled at me. "This one took sixty-four years, and it had to send a girl on a bicycle. Twice."
