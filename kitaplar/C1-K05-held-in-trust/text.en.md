> Anlatıcı Nur Demir (41), birinci tekil şahıs, geçmiş zaman; olgun, sıcak, hafif ironik iç ses. Türkçe sözcükler (emanet, Hanım, Amca, anne) metinde bilinçli olarak korunur.

## [c1] The Job

### [p01]
Margaret had kept a small blue box for fifty-eight years, and she had come back to return it.

I didn't know that when I took the job. What I knew was what the agency told me on a wet Wednesday in late September: an elderly English client, a week on the Turkish coast, consecutive interpreting, nothing technical, flights and hotel paid. Priya, who runs the agency and has never once described a job accurately, called it "practically a holiday".

"She's eighty-one," Priya said. "She taught English in a village down there in the sixties. I imagine she wants to see it again before, well. You know."

"Before what?"

"Before it's too late, Nur. Don't make me say it."

I said I would think about it, which in my family means yes. I had been interpreting for nineteen years: courtrooms, hospitals, trade fairs, and one unforgettable week with a delegation of dentists who disagreed about everything except lunch. I am good at my job precisely because I disappear in it. An interpreter, my first teacher used to say, is a pane of glass. If people notice the glass, it is dirty.

There was one problem. The job ran from Monday to Friday, and on Friday evening at half past six my daughter Mina, who is nine, was supposed to appear in her school's autumn show as the Moon. She had one line. She had been practising it for three weeks, in the bath, on the stairs, and once, unforgivably, at four in the morning.

"You'll be back in plenty of time," said Ollie, my husband, who believes that most problems can be solved with a spreadsheet. "Your flight lands at two. The show starts at half six. Even you can't miss that."

"Thank you for your confidence."

"You know what I mean."

I did. I had missed things before. Not many, but enough for Mina to have started asking "Will you actually be there?" with a small, careful weight on the word actually.

### [p02]
I met Margaret Ellis at the airport on Monday morning at six. She was easy to find. She was the only person in the departures hall who appeared to have dressed for a meeting with her bank manager: a grey wool coat, a silk scarf, and a handbag held in front of her like a shield. She was small and very upright, with short white hair and eyes of the pale, unforgiving blue you sometimes see in a winter sky.

"Mrs Demir," she said, before I could introduce myself. "You are four minutes early. I approve."

"Please call me Nur."

"I shall try." She studied me for a moment longer than was comfortable. "Your agency tells me you are the best Turkish interpreter in London."

"My agency tells everyone that."

"Then I hope, for both our sakes, that on this occasion it happens to be true."

It was, I would later learn, the closest Margaret came to a joke before ten in the morning.

On the plane she refused the window seat, refused the tea, and refused, politely and completely, to tell me anything about the purpose of our trip. She kept a small leather bag on the floor between her feet, and every so often her hand went down to it, as if to check that it was still there.

"Priya said you taught in the village," I tried. "In the sixties?"

"From September 1967 until October 1968. Thirteen months."

"You must have wonderful memories."

"I have memories," said Margaret. "Whether they are wonderful is not for you to decide." She smoothed her scarf. "I do not require a guide, Mrs Demir. Nor do I require a companion. I require an interpreter. I trust you understand the difference."

I understood perfectly. Never had a client drawn the line so clearly, or so early. I found, somewhat to my surprise, that I rather liked her for it.

### [p03]
Somewhere over the Alps, the cabin grew cold, and Margaret asked the steward for a blanket in a tone that suggested he had personally arranged the weather. She wrapped it round her shoulders and looked, for the first time, like what she was: an old woman a long way from home.

"That is a good colour on you," she said suddenly, nodding at my jumper, which was green.

"Thank you. I nearly didn't bring it."

"I used to wear green myself."

"Why did you stop?"

She was quiet for so long that I thought she hadn't heard. Then she said, "I lost the habit," and turned towards the window she had refused to sit beside.

Having worked with clients for nineteen years, I know when a door has been closed, and I know better than to knock on it. I took out my phone instead. There was a message from my mother. It was in Turkish, as all her messages are, and it said, in its entirety: Mina tells me you are going to Turkey. You did not tell me.

I read it twice. My mother has a gift for writing sentences that are perfectly polite and land like a slap. I typed three replies and deleted all of them. In the end I sent nothing, which was, I suppose, a reply of its own.

My mother came to London in 1983 with two suitcases and almost no English, and from the age of seven I translated for her: at the doctor's, at the bank, at parents' evenings where I was, rather awkwardly, the subject under discussion. I was a very good little interpreter. I learned to soften what the doctor said and sharpen what my mother said, and somewhere along the way, I suspect, I stopped listening to her altogether. You can translate a person for thirty years without ever once hearing what she means.

I put the phone away. Margaret's hand went down to the leather bag again.

### [p04]
We landed in the early afternoon into a heat that felt like a mistake. It was October, and the air smelled of pine and hot tarmac and, faintly, of the sea. Margaret took off her coat with visible reluctance, as if conceding an argument.

A driver from the hotel was waiting for us, a cheerful young man who talked about football for the first forty minutes. I translated none of it. Margaret didn't ask me to. She sat in the back with the leather bag on her knees and watched the coast unfold: the steep hills dark with pine, the sudden blue of the bays, the white towns stacked above the water like sugar cubes.

"It was not like this," she said once, quietly, to nobody.

"What wasn't?"

"The road. There was no road. Not a proper one. One came by boat, or one walked, or one waited for Mr Kaya's tractor and hoped for the best."

It was the first time she had used a name, and I noticed it the way you notice a coin on the pavement. I didn't pick it up. Not yet.

The village, when we reached it, was not a village any more. It is said that Sarnıçlı was once so poor that the children shared shoes. If so, nobody had told the shops. There were boutique hotels with names in English, a café selling oat-milk coffee at London prices, a yoga studio in what had obviously once been a barn, and a great many blue wooden gates, all newly painted, all very charming, all slightly too charming.

Margaret looked at it all without expression. Only when we stopped outside the hotel did she speak.

"That," she said, "was my school."

### [p05]
She was right. The hotel had been built inside the old village school, a long stone building with tall windows and a bell that no longer rang. The owners had kept the bell, the stone and a few old wooden desks, which now stood in the hallway holding glossy brochures about authentic village life. The brochure in my room, I later discovered, described the building as "lovingly restored". It did not mention the teacher.

Margaret walked through the reception area very slowly. She stopped in the doorway of what had been her classroom. It was now the breakfast room. A young couple were eating figs at the table where, I imagine, a blackboard had once stood.

"There were thirty-one of them," she said. "Thirty-one children and one book. I wrote the lessons on the wall in chalk, and every evening the caretaker's wife washed them off, and every morning I wrote them again. I thought it a terrible waste at the time." She paused. "I might well have been wrong about that. I was wrong about a great many things."

That evening we sat on the terrace with a view that would have made Priya weep with professional pride. The sun went down behind the island across the bay, turning the sea from blue to silver to copper, and the first lights came on in the hills.

"It's a beautiful view," I said, because someone had to say something, and it was, after all, my job to fill silences with words.

Margaret didn't look at it. She had the leather bag on her lap, and both her hands were resting on it, the way you might rest your hands on a sleeping cat.

"I didn't come for the view," she said. "I came to return something."

## [c2] The Village

### [p06]
I didn't ask what. I would like to say this was professional discipline, but it was mostly cowardice. There is a particular tone of voice which tells you that the next question will open a door you cannot close again, and Margaret had used it.

In the morning she came down to breakfast at exactly eight, dressed as if for a wedding, sat at the table where the blackboard had stood, and ate one boiled egg with the concentration of a surgeon.

"Today," she announced, "we shall look for a person."

"Who?"

"A woman named Hatice Kaya. She was nineteen in 1968. She would now be seventy-seven, if she is alive. I have no address. I have a name, a village and a great deal of time that I have already wasted." She folded her napkin into a perfect square. "I should be grateful if you could ask the appropriate people."

"Who are the appropriate people?"

"That, Mrs Demir, is what I am paying you to find out."

We started with the hotel. The young woman at reception had been born in Istanbul and had moved to the coast for, as she put it, "the quality of life". She had never heard of the Kaya family. Neither had the man who ran the café, nor the woman in the soap shop, nor the yoga teacher, who was from Manchester.

The soap shop turned out to be the house where Margaret had lodged. She stood outside it for a long time while tourists stepped politely round her. The wooden balcony was gone. The fig tree in the yard was gone. A sign by the door, in three languages, promised soap made by hand using traditional methods. The soap, I noticed, came in boxes printed with the address of a factory near the airport.

"Not only have they taken the balcony," said Margaret, "but they have also taken the goats."

"Were there goats?"

"There were always goats," she said, with deep feeling.

### [p07]
By eleven it was hot, and Margaret was tired, though she would rather have walked into the sea than admit it. I suggested a coffee. She accepted, on the condition that it was not oat-milk coffee.

Just off the main square, behind a row of new shops, we found something the brochures had overlooked: a small, shabby coffee house with plastic chairs, a television showing a cooking programme with the sound off, and five old men playing backgammon with the seriousness of generals. It was the first place in Sarnıçlı that Margaret looked at as if she recognised it.

The men stopped playing when we came in. Five pairs of eyes travelled from my face to Margaret's hat and back again.

"Kindly inform these gentlemen," Margaret said, in the voice of someone opening a public library, "that I am seeking a Miss Hatice Kaya, who resided in this village in 1967 and 1968, and that I should be most grateful for any information they are able to provide."

I translated this as: "Good morning. We are looking for Hatice Kaya. She lived here a long time ago."

A great deal is lost in translation, but sometimes that is the point. If I had translated Margaret word for word, we would still be standing in that doorway now while five men tried to work out whether she was from the government.

There followed what every interpreter in Turkey knows as the questioning. Who was I? Where was I from? Where were my parents from? Was I married? Did I have children? Only one? Why only one? Who was the English lady? Was she married? Why not? Had she eaten?

"What are they saying?" Margaret asked.

"They are asking about your health," I said, which was, if you read between the lines, roughly true.

### [p08]
It was the fifth man who had not asked anything. He was older than the others, or perhaps only thinner, with a white moustache and a flat cap, and he had been looking at Margaret since the moment she sat down. Now he stood up. He took off his cap. And then, in careful, old-fashioned English, as if lifting each word down from a high shelf, he said:

"Good morning, Miss Ellis. The cat is on the table."

Margaret went completely still.

The other men looked at each other.

"Osman," said Margaret at last. It was not a question. "Osman, the coffee-house boy. Second row, by the window. You could never sit still."

He laughed, and nodded, and laughed again, and said something to the others in Turkish so fast that even I lost half of it. The half I caught was enough. This English lady, he was telling them, had been his teacher. She had come in 1967 with one suitcase and one book, and she had made thirty-one village children say "The cat is on the table" until the whole village said it, including the cats.

"He says you were very strict," I told her.

"I was."

"He says you were the only teacher who ever told him he was clever."

Margaret looked down at her hands. "Well," she said, rather briskly. "He was."

Osman made us sit. He brought tea himself, though he must have been seventy, and he refused to let us pay for it with an indignation that I didn't need to translate. He had owned this coffee house for forty years, he said, after his father. He had stayed when everyone else sold their land to the hotels. "Somebody had to," he said. "Where else would old men go to argue?"

It was only when the second glass of tea had been poured that Margaret said the name again.

### [p09]
"Ask him," she said to me, "whether he remembers Hatice Kaya."

I asked him. Osman's face changed. It did not darken, exactly. It became careful, the way a face does when it is deciding how much of a story belongs to it.

"Of course I remember Hatice," he said in Turkish. "Everyone remembers Hatice. She was the one who learned English faster than all of us. She used to go to the teacher's room in the evenings with her schoolbooks, and they would sit on the balcony and teach each other. Hatice taught her Turkish. The teacher taught Hatice English. My mother said it was improper. My father said it was the only sensible thing anyone in this village had ever done."

I translated. Margaret's mouth tightened, though whether at the memory or at Osman's mother, I couldn't tell.

"Is she alive?" Margaret asked. "Does she still live here?"

Osman took a long time to answer. He turned his tea glass round on its little saucer, once, twice.

"She lives up in the old village," he said finally. "Above the cistern, where the hotels haven't reached yet. She married a man from Kaş, but he died, a long time ago now. Her son lives in town. Her grandson works down at the beach café." He paused. "She doesn't come down much. She doesn't see people."

"Doesn't see people?" Margaret repeated, when I had translated. "Is she ill?"

"No," said Osman. "Not ill."

"Then why?"

Osman looked at Margaret for a long moment. Then he looked at me, and I had the strangest feeling that he was speaking to me rather than to her, that he wanted to be sure that this, at least, would be translated exactly.

"Hatice?" he said. "She has been waiting for a long time."

## [c3] 1968

### [p10]
Margaret said nothing on the walk back to the hotel. She said nothing at lunch, which she did not eat, and nothing all afternoon, which she spent in her room with the door shut. I spent it on the terrace, watching a cat sleep on one of the old school desks with the total commitment that only cats can manage.

At seven she came out. She had changed into a dark blue dress and put on a little lipstick, and she was carrying the leather bag. She sat down opposite me, placed the bag on the table between us, and looked at it as if it might say something first.

"I am going to tell you a story, Mrs Demir," she said. "It is not part of your job, and you needn't listen if you would rather not."

"I'd like to listen."

"I thought you might." She opened the bag. "I have told it to nobody. Not to my husband, while he was alive, and not to my son, who would only try to fix it."

She took out two things and set them on the table.

The first was a small wooden box, about the size of a paperback book but deeper, painted the clear, bright blue of a summer sea. Tiny white flowers had been painted round the edges by someone with a steady hand and a great deal of patience. The paint had worn thin at the corners, where fingers had held it, I thought, many thousands of times.

The second was a little red dictionary, its cover cracked, its pages the colour of weak tea. English to Turkish, Turkish to English. On the inside of the cover, in faded blue ink, someone had written: M. Ellis, 1967.

"Hatice," said Margaret, and touched the box with one finger. "And me," she added, and touched the dictionary.

### [p11]
"I came here in 1967 because I was twenty-two and bored," she began. "That is the honest answer. The official answer was that I wished to serve the cause of education. I had a degree in English, a mother who wanted me to marry a solicitor, and a strong suspicion that if I stayed in our town another year I would turn into her. An advertisement in a newspaper asked for teachers willing to work abroad in difficult conditions. I applied that afternoon. It was generally believed in the office, I learned later, that they had taken me because nobody else applied."

The conditions, it turned out, had been as difficult as promised. No electricity in the evenings. Water from the old stone cistern, carried up the hill in buckets. A classroom that was freezing in January and an oven in July.

"I lodged with the Kaya family," she said. "Mr Kaya, Mrs Kaya, two small boys, a great many goats and Hatice. Hatice was eighteen when I arrived. She had left school at twelve, as girls did then, but she was the cleverest person in the house, and possibly in the village. She wanted to learn everything. English, mostly. She would sit on the balcony every evening while I marked the children's work, and she would point at things and wait for me to name them. Lamp. Star. Goat. Impossible."

"Impossible?"

"That was the word I used most. She learned it in the first week." Margaret almost smiled. "In return, she taught me Turkish, which I spoke very badly, and she laughed at me, which she did very well. By the spring we had invented a language of our own, half English, half Turkish and half hands. I am aware that is three halves. It was that kind of language."

She stopped. The waiter came out and lit a candle on our table without being asked.

"I have never," said Margaret, "had a friend like her. Before or since."

### [p12]
"What happened?" I asked.

"A telegram happened." She said it the way some people say a storm. "It arrived on a Thursday in October, in the evening. We were sitting at the kitchen table, Hatice and I, with the lamp lit. I remember it was cold, the first cold night of the autumn, and I had taken my cardigan off because the stove was hot. Mr Kaya brought the telegram up from the coffee house, where the boy had left it. It said that my father was seriously ill and that I should come home at once."

She turned the blue box very slightly on the table, so that it was square with the edge.

"There was a bus from Kaş at six the next morning. The only way to reach it in time was to leave that night. Mr Kaya said he would take me on the tractor. Hatice packed my things while I sat there like a fool, holding the telegram. Hardly had I put on my coat when the tractor was at the gate. I left so quickly that I left half my belongings behind. My cardigan, for one. My mother had knitted it for me before I came. Green, with one brass button at the top that never matched the others, because she had run out of the proper ones. I was cold all the way to Istanbul."

So that, I thought, was what had happened to the green.

"Hatice came running down to the gate in the dark. She had this box in her hands. She had been given it by her grandmother, and inside it were her grandmother's silver earrings, the only valuable thing she owned. She pressed it into my hands and closed my fingers round it, and she said something to me in Turkish. Four words. I have never forgotten them."

Margaret looked up at me. In the candlelight, her blue eyes were very bright.

"She said: Bu sana emanet."

### [p13]
I didn't say anything. I didn't need to. Margaret had already opened the red dictionary at a page marked with a faded ribbon, and she turned it round so that I could read it.

The entry was short. Emanet: a deposit; a thing lent; a trust. Someone, many years ago, had underlined the words a thing lent in pencil, twice.

"I looked it up on the bus," Margaret said. "Somewhere near dawn. I can tell you exactly where I was when I read it, because it was the moment I understood what I had done." She put one finger on the underlined words. "It was a loan. She had lent me the most precious thing she owned, to be given back. And I took it to England, and I never gave it back."

"You must have meant to."

"Of course I meant to. I meant to every day for a year. My father was ill for many weeks, and then he died, and then there was a job, and then there was Peter, and then there was a son. I wrote to Hatice twice. Neither letter was answered. I told myself she was angry, and that she was right to be. I told myself that I would go back next year, when there was money, when there was time. It is a remarkable thing, Mrs Demir, how many years can pass while one is waiting for next year."

She closed the dictionary.

"So that is the story. A girl trusted me with her grandmother's earrings, and I ran away in the night and kept them for fifty-eight years. I have been, for most of my life, a kind of thief. It is high time I stopped being one. If I had been braver at twenty-three, I would be a very different old woman today. I should like to give them back to her, and apologise, and then I should like to go home."

### [p14]
I am a professional. I said the professional things. I said that it must have been very difficult. I said that I would help in any way I could. I said that it was getting cold. Margaret agreed, and took the box and the dictionary upstairs.

Left alone on the terrace, I sat there for a long time after the candle had gone out.

Because I knew what emanet meant.

Not what the dictionary said. What it meant. It is arguably one of those Turkish words that a dictionary cannot hold, the way a jug cannot hold the sea. Yes, it can mean a deposit, a thing left in your care. But when one person puts something precious into another person's hands at the moment of parting, and closes their fingers round it, and says bu sana emanet, she is not making a loan. She is saying: keep this safe for me. Keep it until we meet again. As long as you have it, a part of me is with you, and you will have to come back.

It is not a debt. It is a promise.

My grandmother said it to me once, at the airport, when I was eleven, handing me a silver bracelet I still have. My mother said it to the neighbours every summer, giving them her house keys and her plants. I had heard it all my life. I had simply never had to translate it.

Should I tell her? Every rule I had ever been taught said no. The interpreter does not interpret the client's life. The interpreter does not add, explain, comfort or correct. The interpreter is a pane of glass. If I told Margaret that she had spent fifty-eight years misreading a single word, I would no longer be glass. I would be something else entirely, and I wasn't sure what.

So I went to bed. I knew exactly what Hatice had meant. And I said nothing.

## [c4] Mothers

### [p15]
My mother rang at half past seven the next morning, which was half past five in London. My mother does not believe in time zones. She regards them, I think, as an English invention designed to keep her from her family.

"Nur? Are you awake?"

"I am now, Anne."

"Good. You didn't answer my message."

There it was. Not hello, not how are you. The case for the prosecution, opened before the defence had found its slippers. I sat up in bed and looked at the ceiling of my hotel room, which had once been a classroom ceiling, and wondered how many children had stared at it in exactly the same mood.

"I've been working," I said. "It's a job. A client."

"In Turkey."

"Yes."

"Where in Turkey?"

I told her. There was a short silence on the line.

"I went there once," my mother said. "In 1979. Before you were born. With your father, before we were married. There were no hotels then. We slept in a house with goats under the floor."

"You never told me that."

"You never asked." A pause. "Is it still beautiful?"

"It's full of yoga studios."

"Then it is still beautiful underneath." Another pause. "Are you eating properly? Oralar akşam serin olur. Üşütme."

The evenings are cool there. Don't catch a cold. She has said it to me, in one form or another, every time I have left a room since 1985. In August. In Spain. Once, unforgettably, when I was going into labour. For thirty years I have heard it as a small complaint about my competence: you are not able to look after yourself, and so I must still do it for you.

"I'm forty-one, Anne. I'm not going to catch a cold."

"Of course not," said my mother, and the line went very quiet.

### [p16]
After that the conversation went where our conversations usually go, which is nowhere, slowly. She asked whether I would be back for Mina's show. I said yes. She said she would like to come to the show, if there was room. I said the tickets had sold out, which was true, and which I had not tried very hard to change. She said, "I see." She has a way of saying I see that could stop traffic.

We said goodbye. Neither of us said anything else.

When I came down to breakfast, Margaret was already at her table, working through her egg. She looked up as I sat down.

"Your mother," she said. It was not a question.

"Is it that obvious?"

"The walls of this building are made of stone, Mrs Demir, but the windows are not. I was on my balcony." She took a sip of tea. "You speak to her as if she were a client."

I opened my mouth to object and found I had nothing to object with.

"My mother never once told me that she loved me," said Margaret. "Not when I left, not when she was dying. It was not something people of her kind said. She knitted instead. A cardigan every winter, whether I needed one or not. I used to find it suffocating. It was only after she died that I realised every one of them had been a sentence." She put down her cup. "I needn't have waited so long to understand that. But there we are. Most of us learn to read our mothers rather late, and some of us never learn at all."

I didn't answer. I was thinking about thirty years of don't catch a cold, and wondering, for the first time, whether I had been translating it wrong.

"Now," said Margaret, folding her napkin. "The old village. Above the cistern. I believe we have a gate to find."

### [p17]
Osman had said that the hotels had not yet reached the old village. This was true in the way that it is true that the tide has not yet reached your towel. They were close. On the road up the hill we passed two building sites and a sign showing a drawing of a very large swimming pool. But above the cistern, a round stone tank as old as anything in the valley, the new paint ran out, and the village became itself again: narrow lanes, low stone walls, tomatoes drying on flat roofs, and an elderly dog who escorted us up the lane with great dignity.

The problem was the gates. Osman had said Hatice lived behind a blue gate. Every gate in the old village was blue. It was, I learned later, a local custom. The hotels had copied it, which was why every gate in the new village was blue as well, though in a slightly more expensive shade.

"How very helpful," said Margaret, leaning on her umbrella, which she used as a walking stick and, I suspected, as a weapon.

We knocked on nine blue gates. Behind them we found a woman making bread, who gave us some; two brothers mending a fishing net, who argued about which of them should answer the door; and a girl of about six, who asked me whether the English lady was a hundred. Nobody knew Hatice, or everybody did and nobody would say. In a village, it is often hard to tell the difference.

At the tenth gate an old woman in a headscarf was sweeping her step. She looked at Margaret, and then she looked at me, and then she pointed with her broom further up the lane, to a house standing slightly apart from the others, with a fig tree leaning over its wall.

"Hatice," was all she said.

### [p18]
It was a small house, whitewashed, with a wooden balcony on the upper floor and a vine growing over the door. The gate was blue, of course, but an older blue than the others, faded almost to grey, the paint peeling in long curls. The fig tree was enormous. Its fruit lay squashed on the path, and the air was thick with the sweet, heavy smell of it.

Margaret stopped at the gate. For a moment she didn't move at all. Then she took her hand off her umbrella and put it on the leather bag, which she had insisted on carrying up the hill herself.

"There was a fig tree in the Kayas' yard," she said quietly. "Exactly like this one. It is quite possible that someone took a cutting." She swallowed. "Or that I am an old woman seeing what she wishes to see."

"Shall I knock?"

"That is what I am paying you for."

I knocked. Nothing happened. I knocked again, harder, and somewhere inside the house a door closed, and footsteps came down a stone staircase, quick and light. The gate opened.

It was not an old woman. It was a young man of about twenty-five, in a white shirt from the beach café, with dark curly hair and a kind, slightly anxious face. He looked from me to Margaret and back again with an expression I could not quite read.

I explained, in Turkish, who we were. I said that the English lady had known Hatice Kaya a long time ago, in 1968, and that she had come a very long way to see her.

The young man listened to all of it without interrupting. When I had finished, he looked at Margaret again, for a long moment. Then he said, gently, as if apologising for the weather:

"Babaannem kimseyi görmek istemiyor."

My grandmother doesn't want to see anyone.

## [c5] Beyond the Brief

### [p19]
His name was Emre, and he was, he told me, very sorry. His grandmother was not ill. She had heard the day before, from Osman, that the English teacher had come back, and she had gone upstairs and closed her door, and this morning she had told him to say, if anyone came, that she did not want to see anyone.

"Anyone?" I asked. "Or her?"

Emre looked unhappy. "She didn't say."

I translated. Margaret received it the way she received most things, standing very straight, with no expression at all. Only her hand on the leather bag gave her away. The knuckles had gone white.

"Please tell your grandmother," she said, "that I understand, and that I quite deserve it."

I translated that too, word for word, because those were the rules. Emre listened, and then he did something unexpected. He turned to Margaret and spoke to her directly, in the slow, cheerful English of someone who has learned it from tourists.

"I am agree, it is very strange," he said. "My grandmother is not angry person. Never. She is quiet person. Maybe she is shy."

"Shy," Margaret repeated, as if it were a word from a language she had never studied. "Thank you, young man. You are very kind. You needn't have come down."

On the way back down the hill she walked faster than I had seen her walk all week. Only when we reached the cistern did she stop to rest. She stood looking down into the dark water for a while.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I shall come back once more. I shall leave the box with the young man, with a note, and that will be the end of it. You will write the note for me, in Turkish. I will tell you what to say."

"Of course."

"Good." She turned away from the water. "It would seem that I have come a very long way to deliver a parcel."

### [p20]
She dictated the note after dinner, on the terrace, in a voice so steady that it must have cost her a great deal.

"Dear Hatice. I am returning what you lent me. I am sorry that I kept it for so long. I hope that one day you will be able to forgive me. Margaret."

Having dictated it, she went to bed, and left me with a pen, a sheet of hotel notepaper and three sentences that I could translate perfectly in about a minute.

I didn't. I sat there for an hour.

The problem was the word lent. Ödünç. It is a perfectly good Turkish word, and it means exactly what Margaret thought it meant. The translation would be accurate, and nobody could ever say that I had failed to do my job. And Hatice would open her door, or not, and read that her oldest friend had spent fifty-eight years thinking of her gift as a debt. That the promise had been received as an invoice. That the girl at the gate, closing Margaret's fingers round the box, had been understood, all this time, as a lender wanting her property back.

What I needed was not a better word. What I needed was permission to stop being glass.

At nine I rang home. Ollie answered, and then Mina took the phone, and told me without any introduction that she had been practising her line and wanted me to hear it, and would I please be quiet and listen properly.

I was quiet. I listened properly.

"I am the Moon," said my daughter, from nearly two thousand miles away, in her most serious voice. "I am still here, even when you cannot see me."

"That's perfect," I said. My throat was doing something inconvenient. "That's absolutely perfect."

"Granny says it's a good line too. Granny says you'll get cold in Turkey because you never take a coat." A pause. "Will you actually be there, Mum?"

"Yes," I said. "I'll actually be there."

### [p21]
I found Margaret at breakfast the next morning, at her table, working on her egg. I sat down opposite her without being invited, which I had never done before, and she raised one eyebrow by perhaps a millimetre.

"Mrs Demir."

"Mrs Ellis. I need to step outside my role for a few minutes. If you would rather I didn't, say so now, and I'll write your note exactly as you dictated it."

She put down her spoon. "Go on."

So I told her. I told her what emanet meant, not in the dictionary but in a kitchen, at a gate, at an airport. I told her about my grandmother and the silver bracelet, and my mother and the neighbours' keys. I told her that when a person puts something into your hands at the moment of parting and says bu sana emanet, she is not asking you to give it back. She is asking you to keep it safe. She is asking you to come back.

"It isn't a loan," I said. "It's something held in trust. It's a promise that you'll meet again. That's what she gave you, Mrs Ellis. Not her earrings. A reason to come back."

Margaret didn't move for a long time. A waiter came towards us, saw her face and went away again.

"Fifty-eight years," she said at last. "Because of a pocket dictionary."

"Because of a pocket dictionary."

"If I had asked anyone, anyone at all, I would not be sitting here now as a foolish old woman with a guilty conscience and a box I had every right to keep." She gave a short, dry laugh with no pleasure in it. "And you knew. You knew on Tuesday night."

"Yes."

"And you said nothing."

"I was supposed to say nothing. That's my job."

"I see." She looked at me, and something in her face had changed, though I couldn't have said exactly what. "Then I am very glad, Mrs Demir, that you are so bad at it."

### [p22]
We went back up the hill at ten. The dog met us again at the cistern and escorted us all the way, as if he had been expecting us. Emre opened the gate before we had knocked. He had not gone to work, he said. He had a feeling.

The yard was small and shady, with pots of basil along the wall and the great fig tree spreading over everything. The door of the house was closed. Above it, the shutters on the upstairs window were closed too, but not quite. One of them stood open by perhaps the width of a hand.

Margaret stood in the middle of the yard, under the fig tree, holding the blue box in both hands. She turned to me.

"I am going to speak to her," she said. "I would like you to translate. Not what I say, Mrs Demir. What I mean. You appear to be rather good at telling the difference."

Then she lifted her face to the window and began.

She did not make a speech. She talked, haltingly at first, about the balcony and the lamp, and the word impossible, and the goats that ate her lesson notes. She said that she had kept the box in the drawer beside her bed for fifty-eight years, and that she had never once worn the earrings, because they were not hers to wear. She said she had been afraid, for most of her life, that Hatice thought she was a thief.

And I translated. Not word for word. When Margaret said "I have brought back what you lent me," I said, in Turkish, "I have kept your emanet safe all these years, and I have brought it back so that you can see I kept it." When Margaret said "I am sorry," I said "I am sorry it took me so long to come back." It was the most unprofessional thing I have ever done. It was also, I think, the most accurate.

### [p23]
When Margaret had finished, she said one more thing, not to me but to the window, in a Turkish so old and so badly pronounced that it took me a moment to recognise it.

"Hatice. Ben geldim."

I have come. It is what you call out when you come home and open the door. It is not a sentence anyone ever teaches you in a classroom. She must have heard it on the balcony, every evening, fifty-eight years ago, when Mr Kaya came up the steps from the fields.

Nothing happened. The shutter didn't move. Somewhere down the lane a cockerel began to complain about something, and the dog lay down in the dust with a sigh. Emre looked at his shoes.

"Will she come down?" he whispered.

"I hope so," I whispered back.

I looked at the closed door and thought, with a sinking heart, that I had broken every rule of my profession for nothing, and that I would have to explain this to Priya.

Margaret didn't move. She stood under the fig tree, holding the blue box against her chest, as straight as she had stood in the departures hall on Monday, and she waited. She had, after all, a great deal of practice.

I don't know how long it was. A minute. Perhaps two. Then, from inside the house, came the sound of a key turning, slowly, as if the lock had not been used for a long time.

The door opened.

The woman in the doorway was small and very thin, with white hair pulled back under a cotton scarf, and a face so full of lines that it looked like a map of somewhere one would like to visit. She was holding on to the door frame with one hand. Her eyes went straight to Margaret and stayed there.

She was wearing an old green cardigan, much mended at the elbows, buttoned up to the throat. The top button was brass.

## [c6] Emanet

### [p24]
I have interpreted at weddings and in courtrooms, and in hospital corridors at three in the morning. I have never been less needed in my life than I was in that yard.

Margaret made a small sound, not a word. She was looking at the cardigan. I saw her see it: the colour, the shape, the mended elbows, the brass button that had never matched the others because her mother had run out of the proper ones. I saw her begin to understand.

"Impossible," she said.

Hatice let go of the door frame. She came down the two steps into the yard, carefully, and she stopped in front of Margaret, and for a long moment the two women simply looked at each other: the English girl who had run for the bus in the dark, and the village girl who had chased her to the gate, fifty-eight years and a whole life later.

Then Hatice touched the button at her throat and said, in English, in a voice that shook only slightly:

"Impossible."

It was the first English word she had ever learned. Margaret laughed, or perhaps it was the other thing, and put her hand over her mouth.

"Senin emanetin," said Hatice, in Turkish now, touching the cardigan again. And then, because she could see that Margaret did not understand, she looked at me for the first time. "Söyle ona," she said. Tell her.

So I told her. "She says, this is your emanet. You left it on the chair by the stove, on the night of the telegram. She has been keeping it for you. You were supposed to come back for it."

Only then did Margaret understand it all. Not only had she been keeping Hatice's promise for fifty-eight years, but Hatice had been keeping hers.

### [p25]
We went inside. It was Emre who made the tea, and I who carried the glasses, and the two old women who sat side by side on a low sofa under the window, with the blue box on the table in front of them and their hands, I noticed, almost touching.

My job, for the next hour, was the strangest of my career. I translated, but less and less. They didn't need me for the important parts, only for dates and names and the longer explanations, which came out of Hatice slowly, in a soft Turkish full of pauses.

The letters had come, she said. Both of them. But the family had moved up to the old village that winter, and the letters had sat in a drawer at the coffee house for weeks before anyone thought to bring them up the hill. By the time she held them, she was ashamed of how much English she had forgotten already. She had tried to write back. She had written Dear Margaret at the top of a page eleven times, and then she had not known what came next, and she had put the pages in the pocket of the cardigan, where they still were.

She showed us. Eleven pieces of yellowed paper, each with the same two words in a careful, schoolgirl hand, and nothing else.

"In the village it is believed that I am proud," Hatice said. "That I think I am too good for visitors. I am not proud. I have never been proud. I am shy. When Osman told me the teacher had come, I thought: she will see an old woman who has forgotten everything she taught her, and she will be sorry she came. I would rather she remembered the girl on the balcony."

I translated. Margaret took Hatice's hand, the one with the eleven letters in it.

"Tell her," she said, "that so would I. And that I have forgotten nothing."

### [p26]
Towards noon, Margaret did what she had come to do. She picked up the blue box and held it out to Hatice with both hands.

Hatice looked at it for a long time. Then she put her own hands round Margaret's, and gently, firmly, closed Margaret's fingers over the box, exactly as she had done at the gate in 1968. She said something in Turkish, and smiled, and I didn't need to be asked to translate it.

"She says it's still an emanet. You haven't finished keeping it."

"Then she must keep the cardigan," said Margaret immediately.

"She says she was going to give it back."

"Tell her I would rather she didn't. Tell her it suits her far better than it ever suited me. And tell her that if she keeps mine, and I keep hers, then neither of us has finished, and I shall have to come back." Margaret paused. "When is the almond blossom? Is it still in March?"

Hatice laughed when I asked her, a real laugh, surprisingly young. "February," she said. "Sometimes March. It depends on the winter."

"Then I shall come in February," said Margaret. "Which means I shall have to live until February. How extremely inconvenient." She turned to me. "Mrs Demir, are you available in February?"

"I'll check my diary," I said, which in my family also means yes.

After that I went outside and sat under the fig tree, because there was nothing left for me to do indoors. They were talking in their old language, half English, half Turkish and half hands, and it was clear that it still worked. Emre came out and sat beside me with two glasses of tea.

"I think they don't need us," he said, in English.

"I think not."

He thought about it. "It is good job, yours," he said. "Translating."

"Today it is," I said.

### [p27]
Our flight left on Friday morning. Margaret slept for most of it, upright, with her hands resting on the leather bag and the blue box inside it. She had the look of someone who has put down a heavy suitcase after carrying it a very long way, and has just remembered that it was never hers to carry.

At the airport in London, while we waited for her son to collect her, I rang my mother.

"Nur? Is something wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong, Anne. I'm back. I wanted to tell you that Ollie has found a spare ticket for Mina's show tonight. One of the other parents can't come. It's yours, if you'd like it."

There was a silence on the line. I waited. I had, after all, recently been taught how.

"I would like it," said my mother. Then, after a moment: "It's cold in London. Take a coat. Üşütme."

And for the first time in thirty years, I heard what she was saying. Not you can't look after yourself. Not you never listen. Something much simpler, which she had been saying all my life in the only words she trusted to carry it.

"I won't, Anne," I said. "Sen de üşütme."

At half past six that evening, in a school hall that smelled of floor polish and orange squash, my daughter walked onto the stage in a silver cardboard costume, looked out into the dark, found us, and said her line to the two of us, her mother and her grandmother, sitting side by side.

"I am the Moon. I am still here, even when you cannot see me."

Margaret Ellis had kept a small blue box for fifty-eight years, and she had come back to return it. In the end, she took it home again. Some things are not lent. They are held in trust, by both sides, for as long as it takes.
