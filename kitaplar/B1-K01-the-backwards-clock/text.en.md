## [c1] The Apprentice

### [p01]
Every Saturday at midnight, the old clock in the square started to run backwards.

Nobody in Haldenberg knew why. The clock had worked perfectly for more than a hundred years. Now, once a week, its long black hands turned the wrong way, slowly, for almost an hour. Then they stopped.

Aziz saw it for the first time from the small window of his room above the clock shop. He was seventeen, and he had arrived in the mountains only two weeks before. He had come to spend the summer with Mrs Ilse Brandt, who was seventy-one and the only clockmaker in the town.

Haldenberg was a quiet place. There were dark green forests on every side, a cold river, a bakery, a café and a square with a tall stone tower.

"Who would do something like that?" Aziz whispered. "And why?"

The clock did not answer. Its hands were still moving the wrong way.

### [p02]
Aziz had always loved machines. At home in the city, he used to open old radios and watches just to see how they worked. Most of them never worked again. His science teacher had noticed this, and she had written a letter to Mrs Brandt.

"I have a student who can't stop asking how things work," she had written. "Can you teach him?"

Mrs Brandt was small and thin, with short white hair and very small feet in green shoes. She moved quickly around the shop, and she hummed old songs while she worked. The shop was full of clocks: clocks on the walls, clocks on the tables, clocks inside boxes. All of them were ticking, and all of them showed exactly the same time.

At the back of the shop there was a storeroom with a small, low door that was always locked.

"Only old boxes in there," Mrs Brandt had said.

"A clock that is wrong is worse than no clock," she told Aziz on his first day. "Remember that."

### [p03]
On Sunday morning, Aziz went to the café in the square for breakfast. Marta, the owner, put a cup of hot chocolate in front of him.

"Did you see it last night?" she asked.

"I saw it," said Aziz. "It ran backwards and stopped at a quarter past nine."

At the next table, Mr Hofer, the baker, put down his newspaper. "It's a ghost," he said loudly. "My grandmother always said there was a ghost in that tower."

"Or it's a person," said a woman near the window. She was looking at Aziz. "Only two people have a key to the tower. Mrs Brandt, who is seventy-one. And her new apprentice."

The café went very quiet. Aziz felt his face turn hot.

"It started on my first weekend here," he said. "I know. But it isn't me."

In the corner, five old people were sitting together at a round table. They didn't say anything. One of them, a tall man with a grey beard, was smiling into his coffee.

### [p04]
That afternoon, Aziz asked Mrs Brandt if he could go up the tower.

"Why?" she asked, without looking up from a tiny watch.

"Because people think I'm the ghost," he said. "If I find out what's happening, they will believe me."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she gave him the big iron key. "My knees are too old for those stairs," she said. "I haven't been up there for years. Be careful."

The tower was dark and cold inside. Aziz climbed ninety-nine stone steps until he reached the room behind the face of the clock. It was full of metal wheels of every size. Some were as big as a table, and some were as small as a coin. Light came through the white glass of the clock face, and the whole room seemed to shine.

He put his hand on the biggest wheel. Then he looked at his fingers. They were shiny, and they smelled of fresh oil.

Somebody had been here, and not very long ago.

## [c2] Footprints

### [p05]
Aziz didn't tell anyone about the oil. He wasn't sure who he could trust.

On Monday, he went up the tower again, this time with a torch. The stairs were covered in grey dust, because nobody ever cleaned them. In the light of the torch, he saw footprints. Most of them were small, much smaller than his own. Others were bigger, but they were mixed together and hard to see.

He bent down and looked closely. The small prints had a pattern of tiny stars on them.

"It might be a child," he thought. "But why would a child climb a clock tower at midnight?"

He took a photo of the prints with his phone and went back down.

In the shop, Mrs Brandt was waiting for him with two cups of tea. "Well?" she asked. "Have you caught your ghost yet?"

"Not yet," said Aziz. "But I've found its feet."

She laughed so suddenly that some of her tea went on the table.

### [p06]
On Wednesday, the café was almost empty, and Marta had time to talk. Aziz was eating apple cake while she was cleaning the coffee machine.

"This square used to be the happiest place in the mountains," she said. "Every Saturday evening there was music here. People came from all the villages in the valley. They used to dance until midnight."

"What happened?" asked Aziz.

"The last bus happened," said Marta. "The bus that came up from the valley used to arrive at a quarter past seven every Saturday, full of young people. The music always started when it arrived. Then, thirty years ago, they stopped the bus. The young people stopped coming, and the band stopped playing. That was the end of it."

She looked out at the empty square for a long time.

"Have you ever danced in a square under the stars?" she asked.

"Never," said Aziz.

"Then you've missed something wonderful," she said.

### [p07]
By the next Saturday, the story of the backwards clock had travelled all the way down the valley. At half past eleven, Aziz was surprised to see the square full of people. Families had brought chairs. Children were running around in their coats. Mr Hofer was selling warm bread from a table and telling everyone about his grandmother's ghost.

Aziz stood near the tower door with his phone ready. He watched the door the whole time. Nobody went in, and nobody came out.

At exactly midnight, the big hands shook. Then they began to move backwards. The crowd made a long, low sound, like one person.

The hands went past eleven, past ten, past nine. Finally, they stopped.

A quarter past eight.

Everyone clapped, as if it was a show. Aziz didn't clap. He was staring at the tower door, which was still locked.

"How did they get in?" he whispered. "I was watching the door!"

### [p08]
Back in his room, Aziz couldn't sleep. He took out a notebook and wrote down everything that he knew.

The first Saturday, a quarter past eleven. The second Saturday, a quarter past ten. The third Saturday, a quarter past nine. Tonight, a quarter past eight.

Every week, exactly one hour earlier.

"It isn't a ghost," he said to himself. "Ghosts don't count."

He thought about the footprints, the fresh oil and the locked door. He thought about the five old people in the café, who never seemed worried about the ghost. They always seemed amused.

Then he looked at his notebook again, and his heart started to beat faster.

If the pattern continued, the clock would stop at a quarter past seven next Saturday. A quarter past seven was the exact time when the last bus used to arrive. It was the time when the music used to start.

Somebody was counting down. But to what?

## [c3] Old Photographs

### [p09]
Haldenberg had a small library above the post office. It was open only two mornings a week, and it was run by a young man called Pavel, who was very bored and very happy to see a visitor.

"Old photos of the square?" said Pavel. "We have boxes of them. Nobody has asked for them since I started this job."

He brought down three dusty boxes. Aziz looked through them for two hours. There were photos of markets, of snow, of the river when it was frozen. And then there were the dances.

Hundreds of people were dancing in the square. Women were spinning in long skirts. Young men were laughing. A band was playing on the steps of the tower: an accordion, two violins and a drum.

In every photo, behind the dancers, Aziz could see the clock.

In every photo, it showed exactly the same time. A quarter past seven.

### [p10]
"That's strange," said Pavel, when Aziz showed him. "Maybe the photographer always came at the same time."

"Maybe," said Aziz. But he didn't think so.

At the bottom of the last box, there was one more photo. It was bigger than the others, and someone had written on the back in blue ink: The last dance. The last Saturday of August. Thirty years ago this summer.

Aziz looked at the calendar on Pavel's desk. Next Saturday was the last Saturday of August.

He looked at the photo again more closely. In the middle of the dancers, there was a small young woman with short hair. She was laughing, and her feet were not touching the ground. Next to the band, a tall young man was playing the accordion.

His face looked strangely familiar.

"Can I borrow this one?" asked Aziz.

"Of course," said Pavel. "You're the first person who has wanted it."

### [p11]
That evening, Aziz put the photo on Mrs Brandt's work table.

"I found this in the library," he said. "Do you remember the dances?"

Mrs Brandt looked at the photo for a long moment. Her hands stopped moving.

"Everyone remembers the dances," she said quietly. Then she picked up a small screwdriver. "Did you finish cleaning the kitchen clock? The one that is always two minutes slow?"

"Yes. But Mrs Brandt, look at the time on the tower. It's a quarter past seven in every photo."

"Clocks stop," she said. "That's why people need clockmakers." And she started to talk about the kitchen clock again.

Aziz was confused. Mrs Brandt usually loved questions. Tonight, she didn't want to answer a single one.

Later, as he was going upstairs, he noticed her green shoes by the back door. They were covered in fine grey dust.

### [p12]
Aziz lay awake and thought.

Mrs Brandt had said that her knees were too old for the tower stairs. But last week he had seen her climb the tall ladder in the shop to reach the top shelf, quickly and easily, like a cat.

She had a key to the tower. She knew more about clocks than anyone in the mountains. And she hadn't wanted to talk about the dances.

"No," he said to the dark room. "It can't be her. Why would a seventy-one-year-old woman climb a tower at midnight?"

But there was only one way to find out.

If he hid in the tower before midnight, he would see the ghost with his own eyes. If the ghost was a person, he would finally know who it was. And if it was really a ghost, well, he would probably run very fast.

He decided. Next Saturday, the last Saturday of August, he would be waiting in the clock room.

## [c4] Midnight

### [p13]
On Saturday evening, Aziz told Mrs Brandt that he was going to watch the clock from the café with Marta.

"Good," she said. She was looking out of the window at the square. "Wear a warm coat. It might be a long night."

Aziz thought that was a strange thing to say, but he didn't ask.

At ten o'clock, he went into the tower with his own key. He locked the door behind him and climbed the ninety-nine steps in the dark. In the clock room, he found a place behind the biggest wheel, where nobody could see him. Then he switched off his torch and waited.

Outside, the square was filling up. He could hear voices, music from someone's phone and children shouting. More people had come than ever before. Some had driven up from towns he had never heard of. Everyone wanted to see the ghost on the last Saturday of the summer.

### [p14]
The clock room was cold, and the waiting was boring. The big wheels turned slowly above him, tick, tick, tick. Aziz counted the ticks. He counted the stars through a small window. He tried not to fall asleep.

At a quarter to twelve, he heard something.

It didn't come from the tower door. It came from below, from somewhere deep inside the stone, as if a heavy old door was opening under the stairs.

Then there were footsteps. Not one person. Several people. They were climbing slowly, and somebody was breathing hard.

"Shh!" said a voice.

"I am being quiet," said another voice. "It's my knees that are making the noise."

Somebody laughed very softly.

Aziz held his breath. His heart was beating so loudly that he was sure they could hear it. The footsteps came closer and closer. A soft yellow light moved on the wall.

### [p15]
The ghosts came into the clock room.

From behind the wheel, Aziz could only see their shadows on the wall. They were moving carefully, as if they knew every corner of the room. One shadow was carrying a lamp. One was carrying something big and square on its back. One small shadow went straight to the centre of the machine.

He heard the sound of metal. A small door opened somewhere in the clock. Then there was a long, careful click.

The ticking stopped.

For a second, the room was completely silent. Down in the square, the crowd went silent too.

Then the great wheels began to turn the other way.

"Perfect," said the small shadow. "Exactly on time."

Aziz knew that voice. He had heard it every day for a month. He had heard it humming old songs over tiny watches.

He stood up and switched on his torch.

### [p16]
Six faces turned towards the light.

There was Mrs Brandt, with a small can of oil in her hand and her green shoes on her feet. Behind her stood the five old people from the round table in the café. The tall man with the grey beard had an accordion case on his back. A woman in a red scarf was holding the lamp. A short man was carrying a small drum, and two others had violin cases under their arms.

Nobody screamed. Nobody ran.

Mrs Brandt looked at Aziz, and then she looked at her watch.

"Good evening, Aziz," she said quietly. "You're late. We started without you."

## [c5] Seven Fifteen

### [p17]
"You?" said Aziz. "All of you?"

"All of us," said Mrs Brandt. "This is Otto, Rosa, Hanne, Bernard and Klaus. Thirty years ago, we were the band and the best dancers in the valley."

Otto, the man with the grey beard, bowed. "I'm the accordion," he said.

Mrs Brandt explained while the hands moved backwards. Her father had looked after this clock before her, and there was an old passage from her storeroom to the tower. She knew how to make the clock run the other way.

"When the bus stopped, the dances stopped," she said. "We have asked the town for years to bring them back. Every time, they said, 'Who will come?' So we decided to show them."

"A ghost brings a crowd," said Rosa, smiling. "A dance doesn't, until it starts."

Aziz pointed at the footprints with the stars. "Those are yours."

"Dance shoes," said Mrs Brandt. "I've had them for fifty years."

### [p18]
"But everyone thinks the ghost is me," said Aziz.

Mrs Brandt's face changed. "I know. That wasn't part of our plan, and I'm sorry. I didn't think they would blame you. I was going to tell everyone tonight."

"Help us finish," said Otto, "and nobody will ever blame you again."

They all looked at the clock. The hands were moving past nine, past eight. Below, the square was so full that people were standing on the café chairs.

"Every week we turned it back one more hour," said Hanne. "Like a countdown. Tonight is thirty years since the last dance. Tonight it stops at a quarter past seven, when the music used to start."

Aziz looked at the great wheels. He had studied this machine all summer. He knew which wheel stopped the hands.

"Then let me do the last part," he said.

He put his hand on the lever. When the long hand reached the three, he pulled it slowly. The clock stopped at exactly a quarter past seven.

### [p19]
They came down through the passage, out through the shop and into the square.

The crowd turned. Hundreds of faces looked at the six old people and the young apprentice. Mr Hofer's mouth fell open.

Mrs Brandt stepped forward. She wasn't loud, but in the silence everyone could hear her.

"The ghost of Haldenberg has a message," she said. "The clock says a quarter past seven, and the dance starts now."

Otto opened his accordion and began to play. Klaus hit the drum. Rosa and Bernard took out their violins. The music was old and fast and happy, and for a moment nobody moved.

Then Marta laughed, took Mr Hofer by the hand and pulled him into the middle of the square. Children followed. Parents followed the children. Soon the whole square was turning and spinning under the stars.

Mrs Brandt held out her hand to Aziz. Her feet in their star shoes hardly touched the ground.

"Now you've danced under the stars," she said.

### [p20]
On Monday morning, Mrs Brandt gave Aziz the big iron key.

"A clock that is wrong is worse than no clock," she said. "Go and make it right."

So Aziz climbed the ninety-nine steps once more. He oiled the wheels, checked every part and set the hands to the correct time. When he came down, the clock in the square was running forwards again, strong and steady.

Nobody called him a ghost again. The town council met that week and made a decision. The dance would return every Saturday evening, all year, and a bus would come up from the valley again.

On his last evening in Haldenberg, Aziz stood by the tower and watched the bus arrive. Young people got off, laughing. The band was already waiting on the steps.

He looked up at the clock. It said a quarter past seven, and this time it was exactly right.

He had come to the mountains to learn how clocks worked. Now he was going home, and he had learned something better: what they were for.
