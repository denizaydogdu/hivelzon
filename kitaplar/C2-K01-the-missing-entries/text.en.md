## [c1] An Absence

### [p01]
Somebody was stealing words from the village dictionary, one entry a night, and Edmund Pryce took it personally.

He discovered the first absence on a Tuesday in October, at four minutes past nine. He knew the exact time because he wrote it down at once, in the small black notebook he kept in his inside pocket. A discovery without a time, in Edmund's view, was not a discovery at all. It was gossip.

Quillham Library occupied the old schoolhouse at the bottom of Mill Lane, between the bakery and a bus shelter at which no bus had been seen to stop since the spring. It had one paid librarian, four volunteers, two radiators and a smell of damp coats that no amount of fresh air could defeat. Edmund had volunteered there every morning from Tuesday to Saturday for three years, ever since he had retired from a long and, he felt, insufficiently celebrated career in lexicography. He shelved returns. He mended torn spines. He corrected the spelling on the noticeboard, occasionally before the ink had dried.

And every morning, before anything else, he turned the pages of the Thornwick.

The Thornwick Dictionary of the English Language, second edition, 1989, lived on a wooden stand in the reference corner. It weighed as much as a small dog and was considerably better behaved. Nobody consulted it any more; people had phones for that sort of thing. Even so, Edmund turned a few of its pages each morning, because a dictionary left open at the same place for too long develops a crease, and a crease, as he liked to tell anyone who had not managed to leave the room, is how decline begins.

That Tuesday he turned to the L section and found a window.

### [p02]
It was a neat rectangle, no bigger than a playing card, cut cleanly through the page where the word lonely ought to have been. Through it he could see part of the following page, on which, by some unkind accident of the alphabet, the only complete word was long.

Edmund stood there for roughly as long as the word suggested.

Then he bent closer. Not only had the entry been removed; it had been removed with care. The cut followed the edges of the column exactly. Nothing above it or below it had been touched. Whoever had done this had known precisely where the entry began and where it ended, which was more than could be said for most of the people who used the word.

"Janet," he said, without raising his voice, because one did not raise one's voice in a library, not even in an emergency. "Janet, would you come here, please?"

Janet Moss, the librarian, was on her knees in the children's corner, rescuing a picture book from under a radiator. She was a cheerful woman of fifty with reading glasses on a chain and the permanently interrupted look of someone who did the work of three people.

"Is it the heating again?"

"It is a crime."

"So it's the heating."

He showed her the window. She looked through it, as people always do look through holes, and then she looked at him.

"Oh dear," she said. "Kids, probably."

"Kids," Edmund repeated, in the tone of a man holding up a dead mouse by its tail.

Behind them, in the children's corner, a small girl was reading aloud to herself from a picture book, slowly and with great seriousness. "On Wed-nes-day," she announced, "the cat went to the sea."

Edmund, who could no more ignore a mispronunciation than a dripping tap, turned round. "Wensday," he said, not unkindly. "The middle d is silent. I can only apologise on behalf of the language."

The girl looked up at him with enormous attention, as if he had handed her something valuable. "Wensday," she said. "Thank you."

"That's Mr Pryce," Janet told her. "He helped to write the big dictionary over there. He knows every word in it."

"Most of them," said Edmund, and returned to the matter in hand.

### [p03]
Janet's view, which she expressed while making him a cup of tea he had not asked for, was that it was a shame but hardly a catastrophe. Nobody had opened the Thornwick in years. The library, she said, had bigger problems, and she lowered her voice to tell him what they were.

"The council's doing a review. Of all the village libraries. A man's coming on Thursday to look at us."

"To look at us for what?"

"For value, Edmund. Footfall. Usage. If our numbers aren't good enough..." She made a small, final gesture, like someone closing a book.

Edmund said that a library was not a shop and could not be measured in feet. "Granted, the building is cold," he said. "Granted, the computers are older than some of the volunteers. Even so, one does not close a library because it is quiet. Quiet is rather the point."

"Tell that to Mr Dunmore."

"I fully intend to."

But the review was a matter for Thursday, and the absence was a matter for now. He went back to the reference corner, took out his notebook and wrote, in the small, upright hand that had once been the despair of three typists: Tuesday, four minutes past nine. Entry removed: lonely, adjective. Instrument: scissors. Motive: unknown.

He underlined the last word twice. It was the only one he did not like.

Edmund, it should be said, had joined the staff of the Thornwick in 1987, at the age of twenty-eight, full of ambition and long words. He had hoped to be given the magnificent end of the language: words like crepuscular, perspicacious and defenestration, words that wore evening dress. Instead, the chief editor, Dr Marjorie Fenn, had given him the small ones. And, go, thing, too, will. Two thousand of the commonest words in English, and every one of them, he had complained at the time, as exciting as a bus timetable.

"Write them for a ten-year-old in a hurry," Dr Fenn had told him. "Anyone can define crepuscular, Edmund. It takes real talent to define the."

He had suspected flattery. Thirty-nine years later, he still did.

### [p04]
At one o'clock, his shift over, he put on his coat and stepped out into Mill Lane. A child's bicycle with a bright yellow basket was leaning against the railings, the only cheerful object in the street. He walked past it without a glance, composing in his head the letter he would write to the council about the vandalism of reference works, and the decline of civilisation more generally.

Lark Cottage stood at the top of the village, where the lane gave up being a lane and became a field. Edmund had lived there alone for eleven years. He would have described his evenings, had anyone asked, as orderly. He listened to the news at six. He ate at seven, at the kitchen table, with the radio for company and a crossword folded beside his plate. He did the washing-up at once, because a plate left until morning was a moral failure. At ten he wound the clock, said "Well, then" to nobody in particular, and went to bed.

It was not loneliness. It was efficiency.

That night, however, he found it hard to sleep. He kept seeing the neat window in the page, and the word beyond it. It was the neatness that troubled him. Vandals were careless; vandals tore. This had been done slowly, by somebody who cared about the edges. One might almost have called it respectful, had it not been so obviously a crime.

Should it happen again, he decided, he would treat it as a case.

On Wednesday morning he arrived at the library eleven minutes early, which was unheard of, and went straight to the reference corner without taking off his scarf. He turned to the L section. The window was still there, which was a relief of a strange kind; at least the world was consistent. Then, for no reason he could have explained, he turned on through the alphabet, page by page, all the way to T.

At three minutes past nine he found that too had gone too.

## [c2] Suspects

### [p05]
By Thursday morning there were three windows in the Thornwick, and Edmund had a list.

He had drawn it up the previous evening at the kitchen table, in the manner of a man preparing a dictionary entry: first the headword, then the evidence, then the definition. The entries were removed, Janet had confirmed, in the afternoons, after his shift and before closing at six. Three people, by her account, made regular use of the reference corner in those hours. Children came in too, of course, after half past three, but Edmund had crossed them out at once. Children did not steal words. They had no use for them.

Suspect the first was Alf Tidy, seventy-four, retired postman, who sat in the reference corner most days with a newspaper crossword and a pencil sharpened to a point of almost threatening precision. Alf had delivered the post in Quillham for forty-one years. He was believed to know the contents of every letter box in the village, though he had never, so far as anyone knew, opened one.

Edmund approached him with the formality the occasion required.

"Mr Tidy. I was wondering if I might ask you one or two questions."

Alf looked up over his glasses. "You're being very polite, Edmund. What have I done?"

"Nothing, I hope."

"So do I," said Alf. He put down his pencil. "Go on, then."

Edmund explained about the windows. He watched Alf's face carefully for signs of guilt, but found only interest, and something that looked unpleasantly like amusement.

"Somebody's nicking words," said Alf, "and you thought, who needs words? Old Alf with his crossword."

"The thought had arisen."

"Edmund, if I wanted a word, I'd ask you. You'd give me twelve, and a talk about where they came from." Alf picked up his pencil again. "Besides, I've never cut anything out of a book in my life. My mother would have had something to say about that, and she'd have gone on saying it for a week."

Granted, it was not an alibi. It was, however, entirely convincing, which Edmund found irritating. He drew a careful line through the first name on his list.

### [p06]
Suspect the second was Gordon Speake.

Gordon ran the general knowledge quiz in the village hall on the last Friday of every month. It had not always been Gordon who ran it. For eleven years it had been Edmund, until what the village still referred to as the octopus incident. A team called The Quizzly Bears had written octopi on their answer sheet; Edmund had marked it wrong and explained, at some length and with reference to ancient Greek, why the plural was octopuses; the explanation had lasted eleven minutes; and the following month the hall committee had decided, as they put it, to go in a different direction. The direction was Gordon.

Edmund found him later that morning in the library's back room, writing questions for the next quiz on a pad of yellow paper and looking up the answers on his phone.

"Edmund!" Gordon was a large, friendly man in a jumper the colour of tomato soup. "Come to check my spelling?"

"I have come," said Edmund, "on a more serious matter."

He explained. Gordon listened with an expression of growing delight.

"And you think I did it? To get back at you?"

"I think nothing. I am merely establishing facts."

"Edmund, look at me." Gordon held up his phone. "Who on earth uses dictionaries any more? It's high time you got yourself a phone that does more than ring." He grinned. "Mind you, if I had wanted to annoy you, I'd have taken octopus."

It was, Edmund was forced to admit, a fair point. What a man of Gordon's temperament would have taken was something with a story attached to it, something he could boast about at the quiz. Nobody could boast about too.

He crossed out the second name, rather less neatly than the first.

That left one. Edmund had saved him until last, in the way that one saves the most unpleasant item on the plate, in the hope that it might somehow disappear.

### [p07]
Before he could deal with suspect the third, however, Mr Dunmore arrived.

Keith Dunmore was a thin, pale man in a grey suit, with a laptop under one arm and the air of someone who had never in his life been surprised by anything, and did not intend to start now. His title, according to the card he gave Janet, was Head of Community Asset Optimisation. Edmund read it twice and decided that it meant nothing at all.

"The purpose of today's visit," Mr Dunmore announced to the small group gathered at the front desk, "is the assessment of current utilisation levels, with a view to the identification of opportunities for the rationalisation of provision."

There was a short silence.

"He means they might shut it," said a voice from behind the returns trolley.

The voice belonged to Callum Reid, sixteen, who was doing two afternoons a week of work experience at the library and making, as far as Edmund could tell, a career of it. He wore a black hood indoors and had a thin white wire running from one ear to somewhere inside his clothing.

"Thank you, Callum," said Janet weakly.

"We'll commence with the footfall data," Mr Dunmore went on.

"Start," Callum translated, to nobody in particular.

Mr Dunmore spent an hour counting chairs, computers and people, and typed every number into his laptop with the expression of a man adding up a bill. In the reference corner, unfortunately, he found the Thornwick, open at the W section, with its fresh window.

"It would appear," he said, "that there has been an incidence of damage to stock."

"Kids," said Janet quickly. "Probably."

Mr Dunmore typed something. "Should any further incidents occur, it is essential that they be reported to my office without delay. Failure to secure assets will, I'm afraid, be reflected in the outcome of the review."

When he had gone, Janet sat down heavily behind the desk.

"Wonderful. Now we're a crime scene with no footfall." She rubbed her eyes. "And he wants usage up! Half the people who want to join can't. Under-sixteens need a parent to come in and sign, with proof of address, between ten and five. We had a little girl in a few weeks ago, ever so keen, asking how to get a card, and her mum works nights. What am I supposed to do, bend the rules?"

But Edmund was not listening. He was writing a name in his notebook: Callum Reid.

### [p08]
He conducted the interview by the returns trolley, since Callum showed no sign of moving away from it.

"Where were you," Edmund asked, "on Tuesday and Wednesday afternoons, between half past three and six?"

"Here." Callum took out one earphone, which Edmund chose to regard as a concession. "Literally here. Laminating."

"Laminating what?"

"Signs. Janet wants signs. Please be quiet. Please don't eat. Please return your books." He shrugged. "Nobody reads them. But they're shiny."

"You will forgive me for observing," said Edmund, "that a young man who spends two afternoons a week cutting out signs is a young man in regular possession of scissors."

Callum looked at him for a long moment. "Why would I nick a word, Mr Dictionary? I've got loads."

"Mr Pryce."

"Whatever."

But Callum, it turned out, had also brought his scissors. He pulled them out of the trolley to make his point: a large pair of office scissors with long, sharp blades, the kind that cut laminate like paper. Edmund took a magnifying glass from his pocket, a habit he had kept since 1987, and went to look at the Thornwick again.

He studied the edges of the three windows for a long time. They were straight, but at each of the four corners the cut was not quite square. It was slightly rounded and a little chewed, as though the blades had not been able to reach properly into the angle.

"Round-ended scissors," he said at last. "The kind that are given to people who cannot be trusted with sharp points."

"Like little kids," said Callum.

"Or the elderly," said Edmund, and thought darkly of Alf.

It might well have been anybody. That was the trouble with evidence: it rarely pointed where one wanted it to.

On Saturday morning the dictionary had five windows. Edmund copied the missing words into his notebook in the order in which they had vanished: lonely, too, will, me, are. Then he looked at the list for a long time, waiting for it to make sense.

He had assumed, as anyone would, that the thief was working through the alphabet. A methodical criminal, at least, would have been a criminal one could respect. But the words were not in alphabetical order. They were not in any order at all, and Edmund found this, obscurely, the most offensive thing of all.

## [c3] A Pattern

### [p09]
Every contributor to the Thornwick had been given a copy of the finished dictionary in 1989, bound in dark green leather, with a letter of thanks from the publishers that Edmund had thought, at the time, a little short. His copy stood on the bottom shelf of the sitting room at Lark Cottage, where the floor was strongest. On Saturday afternoon he lifted it onto the kitchen table, cleared away the remains of lunch, and looked up the five missing entries one by one.

They were, he noted with professional approval, very good definitions. They were short, plain and exact. None of them used a word harder than the word being defined, which was a rule that most dictionaries broke on every page. The entry for me, for example, did not begin with the pronoun, the objective case, or any of the other phrases with which lexicographers like to frighten the public. It said simply: the word you use for yourself when you are the person something is done to or given to. Whoever had written that had known exactly what he was doing.

Little did Edmund suspect, as he copied it into his notebook with a small nod of respect, whose work he was admiring.

He spent the rest of the afternoon looking for a code. He tried the first letters: L, T, W, M, A. He tried anagrams. He arranged the five words in a circle on the table and walked round it, as if they might be more willing to talk from a different angle.

They were not.

Sunday was worse. The library was closed on Sundays and Mondays, and Edmund, who had never before minded either day, found that he did not know what to do with them. He polished his shoes, which did not need polishing. He wrote two letters to the council and tore up both. Such was the silence of Lark Cottage on a Sunday that he could hear the kitchen clock preparing, with a small mechanical sigh, to strike the hour.

He told himself it was the case that was keeping him restless. It was not an unreasonable thing to tell himself, and he believed it nearly all the way.

### [p10]
On Tuesday morning there was a sixth window, and the missing word was you.

Edmund looked at it for some time. Then he took out his notebook and wrote the list again, in a column this time, as if a different shape might change its meaning. Lonely. Too. Will. Me. Are. You.

Six words, and every one of them short. They were the kind of words that nobody ever looked up, because nobody needed to; the kind of words that held a sentence together without ever being noticed, like the nails in a floor.

"Small words," he said aloud.

"Sorry?" said Janet from the desk.

"Nothing."

But it was not nothing. Something was moving at the back of his mind, slowly, like a fish under ice. He thought of Dr Fenn in her office in 1987, handing him the first box of empty slips. He thought of the long list pinned to the wall above his desk, two thousand words in alphabetical order, from a to zero. He thought of himself at twenty-eight, sulking. You'll be given the small words, Edmund. Somebody has to do them properly.

He went to the front of the Thornwick, where the preface was, and turned the thin pages until he found it.

He had read the preface once, in 1989, and had found it, on the whole, insufficiently generous. It was eleven pages long and was mostly about the history of the project, the difficulties of the age, and the brilliance of the senior editors. But on the ninth page, near the bottom, there was a single paragraph he had not forgotten, although he had pretended to.

The two thousand commonest words of the language, the paragraph said, were defined by a single hand, that of Edmund Pryce. No job in the building was harder, and nobody did a job better. A dictionary, wrote Dr Fenn, is only as good as its smallest words.

Edmund read the paragraph twice, standing up. He was aware of a sudden heat in his face, which he put down to the radiator.

### [p11]
It was, of course, only a theory, and Edmund did not trust theories until they had been checked against the evidence. Fortunately, he had kept the evidence. He had kept everything.

At one o'clock he walked home faster than a man of sixty-seven was supposed to walk, and climbed the narrow stairs to the attic. Under the roof, between a broken lamp and a suitcase he had last used in 1996, were nine shoeboxes, tied with string. They contained the slips: the small white cards on which, in 1987 and 1988, every definition in the Thornwick had first been written by hand, before it was typed, checked, argued about and finally printed. Most of his colleagues had thrown theirs away when the work was finished. Edmund had not been able to. Throwing them away would have felt like throwing away the year.

He untied the first box. The slips smelt of dust and of the old office, the coffee and the floor polish and the heat of the photocopier. Each one was in his own handwriting, which had been neater then, and each had a small tick in red ink in the corner: Dr Fenn's tick, which meant approved, and which he had once wanted more than almost anything.

It took him an hour to find all six.

Lonely was in the fourth box, between lodger and long. Me was in the fifth, with a correction in red that he had argued against and lost. Are was in the first box. Too was in the eighth, near the back. Will and you lay side by side in the ninth. He laid them out on the floor of the attic in a row, his own young handwriting looking up at him, and knelt there in the dust with his knees complaining.

Every one of them was his. Not one belonged to anyone else.

Hardly had he set down the sixth slip when he heard himself say, quite clearly, "Oh."

It would appear, he thought, sitting back on his heels, that this was not vandalism at all. Vandals were not selective. Vandals did not read prefaces. Somebody had gone through the Thornwick, a book of two thousand pages, and had taken from it only the work of one man.

That man, for want of a better word, was him.

### [p12]
He spent the evening drawing up a new list. It was a much shorter one, and much more uncomfortable to write, since it consisted of people who might wish him harm.

He was not, he knew, an easy man. He had corrected the spelling of the hall committee's minutes during the meeting at which they were being read. He had once returned a birthday card to his own nephew with the apostrophes put right. He had ended the octopus incident by saying, in front of forty people, that he would rather lose his job than lose an argument about a plural, and had then, in effect, done both. If he had been a little less certain of himself all his life, he might well have more friends now. He knew this the way one knows the weather in a country one has never visited: as information, not experience.

The trouble was that none of the names on the list seemed likely. Gordon would never have bothered. Alf had laughed at the idea. There had been a colleague in 1987, Nigel Brimble, who had been given the letter Q and had never forgiven anybody, but Nigel was believed to have moved to the coast and taken up sailing many years ago, and it was hard to imagine him sailing back to Quillham to cut up a library book.

And the boy. The boy with the hood and the scissors, who called him Mr Dictionary. But what could a sixteen-year-old possibly know about a preface printed before he was born?

By ten o'clock the list had three names on it, all crossed out, and a question mark. Edmund wound the clock. He said "Well, then" to the kitchen. Then he stood for a moment at the window, looking out at the dark lane, and thought about the person who knew his name and had read his words and, for reasons he could not begin to imagine, had decided to take them away.

Only then did it occur to him that the thief might not have finished. There were still two thousand small words in the Thornwick, give or take six, and every one of them was his.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would be waiting.

## [c4] The Stakeout

### [p13]
On Wednesday morning there was no new window in the Thornwick. Edmund checked every page from a to zero, twice, and found nothing missing except what had been missing already. A reasonable man would have taken this as good news. Edmund took it as a sign that the enemy was planning something.

He went home at one o'clock, as usual, and came back at a quarter to three, which was not usual at all. He was carrying a canvas bag that contained a flask of tea, two cheese sandwiches wrapped in greaseproof paper, an apple, his notebook, two pencils, the magnifying glass and a cushion.

Janet watched him unpack it on the desk.

"Edmund, we close at six. You needn't have brought sandwiches."

"One never knows," said Edmund, "how long a stakeout will last."

"It'll last until six. Because we close at six."

From behind the returns trolley came a soft, delighted noise. Callum had taken out both earphones, which Edmund had never seen him do before, and was looking at the cushion with the expression of someone who had been given an unexpected present.

"Are you, like, undercover?"

"I am being discreet," said Edmund. "And I would rather you didn't mention it to anyone."

"Who would I tell? Literally nobody talks to me."

That, Edmund thought, was probably true, and for a moment he felt something that was almost sympathy, before he remembered the hood.

He had chosen his position the night before. In the reference corner, behind a tall bookcase of large-print novels, there stood an old armchair with a high back and a broken spring. From it, by leaning slightly to the left, one could see the Thornwick on its stand through a gap between two shelves, while remaining, or so Edmund believed, entirely invisible. He arranged the cushion over the broken spring, poured a cup of tea from the flask, opened his notebook to a clean page and wrote the time at the top: Wednesday, two minutes to three.

Then he leaned slightly to the left, and waited.

### [p14]
For the first half-hour, the library behaved exactly as it always did, which was to say that very little happened, very slowly.

Alf Tidy finished his crossword at ten past three, put his pencil behind his ear and left, nodding at the large-print bookcase as he passed as though he knew perfectly well who was behind it. Gordon put his head round the door to ask Janet whether the capital of somewhere ended in a or in e, and went away happy. Callum laminated a sign that said Please Be Quiet, and then, as if to test it, dropped the laminator.

At half past three the primary school let out, and the library changed its nature entirely.

They came in groups, in coats that were too big for them, with bags that were too heavy, talking at a volume that would have shocked Mr Dunmore into a fresh paragraph of nominalisations. Some sat at the computers. Some lay on the carpet in the children's corner and read with their feet in the air. Two boys had a whispered argument about a dinosaur that went on for a quarter of an hour and was never, so far as Edmund could tell, resolved. None of them came anywhere near the Thornwick. Children did not, after all, steal words.

Edmund watched the dictionary through his gap. The dictionary, open at the T section, watched him back.

The radiator beside the armchair was the only one in the building that worked properly, and it was working now with all its heart. The tea was warm. The armchair, once the broken spring had been defeated, was surprisingly kind. To keep his mind alert, he took a small students' dictionary from the shelf beside him and began to compare its entries with his own, which he knew by heart, one after another, as other men might read old letters. And. Go. Thing. Help. Teach. The words were small and the definitions were plain, and he had written them a very long time ago, and somewhere around the word pocket, though he would later deny it in the strongest terms, his eyes began to close.

He would rest them, he thought, for a moment. Purely to rest them.

### [p15]
"Edmund."

He opened his eyes. The library was quiet and dim. The children had gone. Somebody had switched off the big lights, and outside the windows the sky was the colour of ink. Janet was standing over him with her coat on and her keys in her hand.

"We're closing," she said gently. "It's six."

Edmund sat up with as much dignity as the broken spring allowed. Two facts presented themselves to him at once. The first was that he had slept for over two hours in the middle of a stakeout, which was the kind of thing that happened to other, lesser detectives. The second was stranger. His scarf, which he remembered quite clearly having taken off and dropped beside the chair because of the radiator, was lying across his knees, folded neatly into four.

"Did you do that?" he asked Janet.

"Do what?"

"Never mind."

He went at once to the Thornwick. He turned every page, from a to zero, while Janet waited at the door, jingling her keys like a hint. There were six windows, and only six. Nothing new had been taken.

"Good stakeout?" asked Callum, who had appeared from nowhere in his hood with a grin that seemed to go all the way round his head.

Edmund did not reply. There are questions to which silence is the only dignified answer, and this, he felt, was one of them.

He walked home through the dark. A frost was coming down; he could feel it in the air, sharp and clean, and the pavement was already beginning to shine under the few streetlights of Mill Lane. If he had stayed awake, he told himself, he would not now be walking home feeling like a fool. Then again, nobody had come. Had he stayed awake, he would simply have watched nobody for three hours instead of sleeping through them, and that did not seem a great deal more distinguished.

At home he ate his supper with the radio on. He washed the plate at once. At ten he wound the clock and said "Well, then", and went upstairs, and lay awake for a long time thinking about the folded scarf.

### [p16]
The card was waiting for him on the doorstep in the morning.

He very nearly stepped on it. He had opened the front door at half past seven to look at the frost, as he did every morning in winter, purely in order to have an opinion about it, and there it was on the mat outside, white against the dark wood: a small rectangular card, like a postcard but thicker, lying face up.

He picked it up. It was an old library catalogue card, the kind on which every book in every library had once been recorded by hand, before computers made the job unnecessary and the cards themselves a kind of fossil. Quillham Library had thrown its catalogue away years ago. Edmund had argued against it, and lost. Janet kept the leftover blank cards in a box by the door, under a sign that said Free, Please Take, and nobody ever did.

Somebody had, now.

In the centre of the card, glued down with great care so that not a single corner lifted, was a strip of thin, slightly yellow dictionary paper. It was exactly the size of the first window. Edmund did not need his magnifying glass to recognise the corners, slightly rounded and a little chewed. He did not need it to read the entry either. He knew the entry. He had written it.

Lonely, adjective. Unhappy because you are alone, or because you feel that nobody is thinking about you.

He looked up. The lane was empty. The frost lay over everything, smooth and unbroken, except in one place. Across his path, from the gate to the step and back again, ran a single thin line, as narrow as a pencil mark, curving slightly, the kind of line that a small bicycle wheel leaves behind it.

Edmund read the card twice. Then, for reasons he could not have defined, and he could define almost anything, he sat down on the cold step in his dressing gown and stayed there for some time.

## [c5] Correspondence

### [p17]
"Someone," said Janet, holding the card up to the light, "is sending you love letters."

"It is nothing of the kind."

"It's a card on your doorstep with the word lonely on it, Edmund. In my day that was a love letter."

"In your day," said Edmund, "people could spell." He took the card back from her and put it carefully into the envelope he had brought for the purpose. "It is evidence. It is, if anything, a ransom note."

"What's the ransom?"

"That," Edmund admitted, "has not yet been made clear."

He had examined the card at length over breakfast, which he had eaten standing up, a thing he had not done since 1974. The strip of dictionary paper had been glued down with a children's glue stick; he could see the faint, shiny streaks at the edges, and when he held the card close to his nose, it smelt, very slightly but unmistakably, of strawberries. He had written this down. He had not written down the fact that he had sat on the step for twenty minutes in his dressing gown, because it was not relevant to the investigation.

On Friday morning there was a second card on the mat. It held the entry for are. On Saturday there was a third: too. On Sunday, when the library was closed and the village was so quiet that he could hear a dog barking three fields away, there was a fourth: will. Each was glued with the same care onto the same kind of old catalogue card, and each smelt faintly of strawberries.

Granted, Edmund could have waited up for the messenger. He could have sat at the front window in the dark with his flask, as he had sat behind the large-print novels, and caught whoever it was in the act. He thought about doing so on Saturday night, and found, somewhat to his surprise, that he would rather not.

A correspondent who wished to be seen, he reasoned, would have knocked.

### [p18]
He laid the cards out on the kitchen table every evening after supper, and moved them about.

It was the kind of work he had once done for a living. In 1987 the Thornwick had employed a whole room of people whose job it was to arrange slips of paper into an order that made sense, and Edmund had been, by general agreement, the best of them. He could look at forty citations for the word run and see at a glance that they divided into seventeen meanings, two of them wrong. He did not see why four cards should defeat him.

They defeated him.

Will too are lonely. Lonely are too, will. Too lonely, will are. He tried them in every order there was, and then, with the methodical despair of a man who has been given a lock without a key, in several orders there wasn't. On Monday morning the fifth card arrived, me, and he added it to the others. Will me too. Are me lonely. Lonely me will too are.

None of it was English. It was, he thought crossly, the sort of thing one found on the back of a cheap shampoo bottle.

But the word in the middle of the table, the first one, the one he kept coming back to, was lonely. He found he did not like it being there. It looked up at him from the table and said, quite reasonably, unhappy because you are alone. He would have liked to argue with it. Unfortunately, he had defined it himself, and he had defined it well.

He was not lonely. He was alone, which was a different word entirely, and any competent dictionary would have told you so. He had checked. He had checked in the green Thornwick on Sunday night, and then, because the green Thornwick was a book he had helped to write and might therefore be accused of bias, in two other dictionaries as well. Alone: without other people. Lonely: unhappy about it. The distinction was perfectly clear.

It was simply that, sitting at the kitchen table with five small cards and the radio, he was no longer entirely sure which side of it he was on.

### [p19]
It is said that people who live alone for long enough begin to talk to their furniture. Edmund had always considered this an exaggeration. He talked to the kettle, certainly, and occasionally to the clock, but that was conversation, not madness, and the kettle had never once answered back.

That week, however, he found himself thinking about the old office in a way he had not done for years.

He remembered the noise of it, mostly. Thirty people at thirty desks, and every one of them arguing. They had argued about commas and about meanings, about whether a tomato was a fruit in a dictionary as well as in a garden, about whose turn it was to buy biscuits. Somebody was always reading something out loud. Somebody was always saying, "Listen to this." He had complained about the noise constantly; he had asked twice to be moved to a quieter room. Now he would have given a good deal to hear it again for an afternoon.

When the Thornwick had been finished, the team had gone their separate ways, as teams do. There had been a party, with speeches and sandwiches, and Dr Fenn had shaken his hand and said, "The small words, Edmund. Never let anyone tell you they don't matter." Then there had been other jobs, other dictionaries, a long and respectable career, and at the end of it this cottage and this kitchen and this clock.

He had been meaning to write to Dr Fenn for years. The last he had heard, she was ninety-one and living by the sea, where she was said to correct the menus at her home with a red pen. He had been meaning to write to Nigel Brimble too, to apologise for something he had said about the letter Q in 1988. He had been meaning to do a great many things. The trouble with meaning to was that it felt so very like doing.

On Monday night, for the first time in eleven years, he took out a sheet of writing paper and wrote to Dr Fenn. It was a short letter. It said that he hoped she was well, that he was volunteering in a library, and that something odd was happening to the small words. He did not say what. He found that he did not quite know.

### [p20]
On Tuesday morning the sixth card was on the mat.

He knew what it would be before he turned it over. There was only one word left in the notebook; there was only one window in the Thornwick whose contents had not yet come home. Even so, he turned it over slowly, the way one opens a letter from the bank.

You, pronoun. The person or people that you are speaking or writing to.

He took it into the kitchen and sat down. The other five cards were still on the table where he had left them the night before, in a row that meant nothing: lonely, will, too, me, are. He looked at them for a while. Then, without thinking very hard about it, which was perhaps the secret, he picked up the new card and the card that said are, and put them down in front of all the others.

Are you.

He moved lonely to join them.

Are you lonely.

And then, because it was sitting there, the way small words do sit, waiting quietly to be useful, he added too.

Are you lonely too.

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, a bird was saying something repetitive in a hedge. The clock ticked. Edmund sat at the table in his dressing gown and looked at the four cards for a long time, and it seemed to him, though it was of course impossible, that they were looking back.

It was a question. It was not a ransom note, or a code, or an insult. It was a question, put to him with great care by somebody who had gone to a good deal of trouble to find the right words, and had found them, of all places, in him.

For the first time in a very long while, somebody had asked Edmund Pryce something that he could not look up.

## [c6] Callum

### [p21]
Edmund arrived at the library that afternoon to find that a trial was already in progress.

Mr Dunmore had returned for what he called a follow-up engagement. He was standing in the reference corner beside the Thornwick, with his laptop open on the stand of a neighbouring atlas, and in front of him, with his hood down for once and his face very red, stood Callum. Janet was hovering between them, wringing a duster.

"Six separate incidents of deliberate damage to stock," Mr Dunmore was saying, "reported over a period of eight days. It would appear that on each of the relevant afternoons, the young man was present in the building. It would further appear that he was in regular possession of a cutting instrument." He nodded towards the desk, on which lay Callum's long office scissors and a plastic bag full of narrow strips of shiny paper. "And of a quantity of paper offcuts."

"They're from the signs," said Callum. "I told you. It's laminate. Literally, look at it."

"In the circumstances," Mr Dunmore went on, as if no one had spoken, "the continuation of the placement would be inappropriate. I shall be informing the school."

Callum looked at the floor. Janet looked at the ceiling. And Edmund, who had disliked the boy steadily for six weeks, and had written his name in a notebook under the heading Suspect the Third, found that he had put down his canvas bag and was walking across the room.

"Mr Dunmore," he said. "I wonder if I might be permitted a word."

Mr Dunmore looked at him with mild surprise, the way one looks at a chair that has started to speak. "Mr Pryce. Of course."

"I believe," said Edmund, "that you may be about to make a mistake. I say this with the greatest respect, and as one who would very much like the culprit caught. The boy did not do it."

"And on what basis do you make that assessment?"

Edmund took out his magnifying glass.

### [p22]
He did it properly, as he did everything. He showed Mr Dunmore the corners of the windows, slightly rounded and a little chewed. He showed him Callum's scissors, long and pointed and perfectly capable of cutting a clean right angle in anything from dictionary paper to a laminated notice. He produced from his inside pocket the envelope containing the six cards, and laid them on the stand, and explained, as briefly as he could, which was not very briefly, that every one of the stolen entries had since been returned to his own doorstep, one a day, before eight in the morning, by somebody on a small bicycle.

"Arguably," he concluded, "the young man is rude. One might suggest that he is idle. He wears his hood indoors, he says literally when he means nothing of the kind, and he has called me Mr Dictionary on no fewer than four occasions. But these windows were cut slowly and with enormous care, by someone with patience. If you had spent a single afternoon with this boy, Mr Dunmore, you would know that patience is not among his faults."

There was a pause.

"Also," said Edmund, "he would have had to get up before eight. I take that to be beyond him."

Mr Dunmore looked at the cards for some time. Then he typed something, and closed his laptop.

"Were it not for Mr Pryce's evidence," he said, "I would have had no alternative but to proceed. In the absence of conclusive identification, however, no further action will be taken at this stage." He picked up his laptop. "The outstanding security issue will nonetheless be reflected in my report."

"What does that mean?" asked Callum, when he had gone.

"It means," said Janet, sitting down, "that you're not in trouble, and we still are."

They went outside, Edmund and the boy, without quite deciding to, and sat on the bench in the bus shelter where no bus came. It was cold. Callum pulled his hood up again, and then, after a moment, pushed it back down.

"Did you think I did it?" he said. "Before?"

"I did."

"Thought so."

"I was wrong," said Edmund. "I apologise."

### [p23]
Callum seemed to find this harder to deal with than the accusation. He looked at the road for a while.

"Why'd you stick up for me? You don't even like me."

"I don't," said Edmund. "That is not the same thing as believing you guilty. A dictionary does not leave out a word because it finds it disagreeable."

Callum thought about this. "That's literally the weirdest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I doubt it."

"No, it is." But he was almost smiling. "Do you actually like it here? The library?"

"I suppose so," said Edmund, and then, because the afternoon seemed to call for honesty, "Rather more than I let anyone see."

"So do I," said Callum. He said it quickly, as though he would rather nobody had heard. "It's quiet. Nobody wants anything. At home it's all, what are you going to do with your life, Callum. Here it's just, can you laminate this." He paused. "I read, sometimes. In the reference corner, when Janet doesn't need me. Not your dictionary. Other stuff."

Edmund, who had spent six weeks assuming that the boy did nothing in the reference corner except breathe loudly, found that he had nothing to say. It was not a sensation he was used to.

"Listen," Callum said. He was looking at his trainers now. "I've been meaning to tell you something. Only I didn't think it mattered, and then you were being all detective about Alf, and I thought, he won't listen to me anyway."

"I am listening now."

"I do a paper round. Six till half seven, every day. Your lane's on it." He frowned, remembering. "Last Thursday, really early, it was still dark, there was frost on everything. I was coming down from the top of the village with my bag, and I saw someone coming out of your gate."

Edmund sat very still. "Who?"

"I don't know who. I couldn't see properly." Callum shrugged. "Just that they were really little. Like, not a grown-up. I saw a small person with a bicycle basket."

## [c7] The Basket

### [p24]
A small person with a bicycle basket.

Edmund said the phrase to himself all the way home, and again over supper, and again at ten o'clock while he wound the clock, until it had worn smooth in his mind like a pebble. And somewhere underneath it, as he lay awake that night, a memory rose slowly to the surface, faint and yellow: a child's bicycle leaning against the railings in Mill Lane on the morning of the first window. The only cheerful object in the street. He had walked straight past it.

In the morning he went to see Alf.

There was nobody in Quillham who knew more about who lived where. Alf had delivered to every door in the village for forty-one years, and although he had retired, his knowledge had not. He was in the reference corner, as usual, with his crossword.

"A yellow basket?" he said. "On a little bike? That'll be number four, Mill Row."

"You're quite sure?"

"Edmund, I know every bicycle in this village. I used to have to get round them." Alf put down his pencil. "New family. Came in August: mother, daughter and a grandmother who sits on the step and knits. The mum works nights at the care home over in Marsh End, so she sleeps half the day, poor woman. The little girl's called Zofia. Ten, or thereabouts. Says good morning to me every single day, very carefully, like she's practising it." He smiled. "Asked me once where they came from, and she told me, with great precision, a big city, with trams."

"Zofia," Edmund repeated.

"Polite as anything. Reads walking along, which I don't approve of. Nearly went into the bus shelter last week with her nose in a notebook." Alf looked at him over his glasses. "Why? What's she done?"

Edmund found that he did not know how to answer. A fortnight ago, he would have said that she had committed a crime against a reference work. Now the phrase seemed to belong to somebody else, somebody rather pompous, whom he did not entirely like.

"I'm not sure," he said at last. "I believe she may have written to me."

### [p25]
Janet knew the name at once.

"Zofia! That's her, the keen one I told you about. She came in three times asking how to get a card. I had to keep saying her mum needed to come in and sign, and her mum's asleep all day and at work all night, so." Janet spread her hands. "She still comes in most afternoons, mind. Sits in the children's corner and reads whatever's there. She's got a notebook she writes everything in. Why?"

Edmund told her. When he had finished, Janet sat down on the edge of the desk and said nothing for a long time, which was itself unusual.

"Oh, Edmund," she said finally.

Only then did he allow himself to put the whole thing together, piece by piece, the way he had once put together forty citations for the word run.

A girl of ten, newly arrived, learning English from whatever she could reach. Not allowed to take a single book home, because of a form she could not get signed. The girl in the children's corner, with her picture book and her careful Wednesday. That's Mr Pryce, Janet had said. He helped to write the big dictionary. Round-ended scissors, the kind they give you at school. A glue stick that smelt of strawberries. A scarf, folded neatly into four.

Children did not steal words, he had written in his notebook. They had no use for them.

He thought he had never written a sentence that was more completely wrong, and he had once, in 1991, defined a hedgehog as a kind of small pig.

There was still a great deal he did not understand. Why his entries, out of all the thousands in the book? Why these six? And why, having taken them, give them back? But he understood enough to know what he must not do, which was to march up to a stranger's door and demand explanations from a child. He had marched up to enough people for one autumn.

"We'll do it properly," said Janet, as if she had read his mind. "I'll write to her mum from the library, today, and put it through the door. A proper letter. And you..." She looked at him. "You write to Zofia."

"How?"

"Edmund. You're the word man. You'll think of something."

### [p26]
It took him the whole of the afternoon to write the note, and most of that was spent crossing things out.

His first attempt began: Should the sender of the enclosed correspondence wish to commence a dialogue with the undersigned regarding the return of library property, it is requested that... He read it back, and heard, quite distinctly, Callum's voice saying start. He crossed it out.

His second attempt was shorter, but it contained the word hitherto, and he crossed that out too.

His third attempt he wrote very slowly, choosing every word as if it cost money, and when he had finished, he found that almost every word in it was small. He read it through four times. Then he copied it out neatly onto one of the leftover catalogue cards from the box marked Free, Please Take.

To the person with the yellow basket. Thank you for the cards. I have read them all, many times. I think I know what some of them mean, but not all. I have put them here so you can see them. Please come and talk to me on Saturday at ten, here in the library, with Janet. Please bring your mum. Edmund Pryce.

Then, with Janet watching from the desk, he took the six cards out of their envelope and pinned them to the noticeboard beside the Thornwick, in a row, in the order that had come to him on Tuesday morning at the kitchen table. Are you lonely too. And then, a little apart, because he still did not know what to do with them, will and me. He pinned his note underneath.

"Will she see it?" he asked.

"She's in most days," said Janet. "She'll see it."

He went home at six. He did not sleep well. He lay listening to the wind, which had moved round to the north, and wondered, as he had not wondered about anything for years, what somebody else was thinking.

### [p27]
On Friday morning he did not stop to look at the frost. He was at the library door at a quarter to nine, a full quarter of an hour before Janet, and he stood on the step with his hands in his pockets in a state that he would have described, had anyone asked, as mild interest.

When she let him in, he went straight to the noticeboard.

The cards had been moved.

His note was still there, pinned at the bottom, exactly where he had left it. But above it the six cards had been taken down and pinned up again, carefully, in a new order, with the small round holes of the old pins still showing beside the new ones. Somebody had stood here, perhaps only yesterday afternoon, with a box of drawing pins and a great deal of concentration, and arranged them into two lines.

The first line said: Are you lonely too.

And somebody had drawn after it, in pencil, a small, careful question mark.

The second line began with will. After it came a seventh card, one he had never seen before: another old catalogue card, on which, since the sender had evidently run out of yous, the word you had been written by hand, in pencil, in the round, painstaking letters of someone who has only lately learned them. After that, there was an eighth card, perfectly blank. Then me. Then another question mark.

Will you, blank, me.

Edmund stood in front of the noticeboard with his coat still buttoned. He read the two lines over and over, and each time he came to the blank card, his mind stopped there, like a foot on a missing stair.

Behind him, Janet had come to look as well. "What goes in the gap?" she said softly.

He did not answer. He was thinking of all the small words he knew, two thousand of them, every one defined by his own hand, and wondering which of them a child would choose, and why she had left it out.

There was one word missing from the message. Edmund Pryce, who knew every word, did not know which.

## [c8] Definitions

### [p28]
At five to ten on Saturday the door opened, and a small girl in a red coat came in, pushing a bicycle with a yellow basket, which Janet, against every rule in the building, allowed her to bring inside. Behind her came a tired young woman in the blue tunic of a care worker, straight from her night shift, holding Janet's letter in her hand as though it might be a summons.

Edmund, who had been standing by the noticeboard since half past nine, recognised the girl at once. She was the one from the children's corner. She looked at him with the same enormous attention as before, and then, very formally, she held out her hand.

"Good morning, Mr Pryce. I am Zofia. I am sorry for the dictionary."

"Good morning, Zofia," said Edmund, and shook it. "I am sorry for the dictionary too. But I suspect we are sorry for different reasons."

It came out slowly, over hot chocolate in the children's corner, with Janet translating the hard parts into simpler English and Mrs Lenart, Zofia's mother, translating the simpler English into something else and nodding with growing horror.

She had wanted to learn the words. That was all. She could not take books home, because of the form, and in the evenings, while her mother was at work and her grandmother knitted in front of a television she did not understand, there was nothing in the flat in English except a bus timetable. So she had read in the library. She had tried the big dictionary first, because it was the biggest. But the big dictionary was hard.

"I read lollop," she said. "To move in an ungainly bounding manner. I don't understand nothing. I read lodger. Also hard." She shook her head sadly at the memory. "Then I read lonely. And I understand everything. All the words."

She had looked at the first pages, she explained, and found his name. She had heard Janet say it. And she had noticed, as no reviewer in thirty-seven years had ever troubled to notice, that the small words in the Thornwick were the only ones a person could understand on their own.

"Your words are easy," she said. "Only yours. So I take only yours."

### [p29]
"And the cards?" said Edmund.

Zofia looked at her hot chocolate. "I copy them in my notebook first. Every word. Then I think: these are his. I give them back." She hesitated. "You are always alone. In the library you talk to nobody, only to correct. You walk home alone. I am also alone. In the day my mum sleep. My grandmother doesn't speak English. At school I don't have the words yet." She shrugged, a small shrug, very grown-up. "So I make a question."

"And the scarf?"

"You were sleeping. It was on the floor. Floor is dirty."

Edmund found, unexpectedly, that he needed to look at the window for a moment.

"There is one more thing," he said, when he could. "The gap. Why did you leave a gap?"

Zofia got down from her chair and led him across the room to the reference corner, where the library's own Thornwick lay open on its stand. She turned the pages carefully, the way he did, so that they would not crease, until she came to the T section. Then she stopped, and put her finger on an entry that was still there.

Teach, verb. To help someone learn something, by showing them, explaining it to them, and waiting patiently while they try.

"I find it," she said. "I have the scissors. But it is the best one. If I cut it, the next person cannot read it. Maybe the next person needs it also." She looked up at him. "So I leave it. And I make a gap. You know what goes in it. You know every word."

Had anyone told Edmund a month earlier that he would one day be reduced to silence by a ten-year-old in a library, he would have corrected their grammar. Now he stood in the reference corner beside a person a third of his size, and could not, for a while, find a single word, large or small.

"Yes," he said at last. "I believe I do."

### [p30]
They repaired the Thornwick that afternoon. Edmund brought the green copy from Lark Cottage, and Janet fed coins into the photocopier, and Zofia cut the six entries out of the photocopies with her round-ended scissors and glued them into the windows with her strawberry glue stick. By any professional standard, the repairs were imperfect. Edmund had never been prouder of a page.

Mrs Lenart signed the form for a library card. As proof of address, Janet accepted the letter she had herself put through the door, on the grounds that it was the most official document in the building.

The club was Janet's idea, and Edmund's name. "A club for small words," he said. "On Saturdays. For anyone who needs them."

"I am agree," said Zofia at once.

"I agree," said Edmund gently. "Agree is already a verb. It does not need any help from am."

The Small Words Club met for the first time the following Saturday, at ten. Zofia came, with her mother and her grandmother, who brought her knitting. So did two other new families from Marsh End, a man from the bakery who wanted to improve his spelling, and Alf, who said he had come to keep an eye on things. Gordon ran a short quiz at the end and, with heroic restraint, accepted octopi. Callum laminated the signs. One of them said Please Be Loud.

Mr Dunmore's report arrived in December. It noted that following the establishment of a community language initiative, a significant increase in the utilisation of the building had been observed, and it recommended, accordingly, the continuation of provision.

"We're staying open," Callum translated.

A letter from Dr Fenn arrived in the same week, written in red ink. It said several things, most of them about menus, but the last line Edmund read many times. The small words, Edmund. I did tell you.

On the noticeboard by the Thornwick, the two lines of cards were still pinned up, because nobody, by then, could bear to take them down. Somebody had written teach in the gap, in round pencil letters. And underneath, on a fresh catalogue card from the box marked Free, Please Take, there was now an answer, in the small, upright hand that had once been the despair of three typists.

Yes. And yes.

It was, Edmund reflected, the shortest entry he had ever written, and quite possibly the best.
