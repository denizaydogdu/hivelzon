## [c1] The Manuscript

### [p01]
My father wrote nine chapters in Turkish and left the tenth, it seemed, to me.

That, at least, was how Rosalind Pike put it in her email, although she put it in the language of publishing, which is to English roughly what a filing cabinet is to a tree. "Following the confirmation of the rights position with Mr Aral's Istanbul publisher," she wrote, "and in view of the apparent absence of a tenth chapter, we would welcome a conversation regarding the completion of the work by its translator."

I read it twice at my kitchen table in Manchester, with the rain doing what rain does in Manchester, which is not so much to fall as to attend. Then I read it a third time, because nobody was paying me to read it and I could therefore afford to be thorough.

I am a translator. For most of my working life I have turned Turkish into English for people who need it to be accurate rather than beautiful: insurance claims, tenancy agreements, the instruction booklets of dishwashers that are, it would appear, deeply offended if you overload them. I once spent a week on a forty-page complaint about a neighbour's apricot tree, and I am still not sure who was in the right. What I had never translated, and had privately decided I never would, was a novel by my father.

Hakan Aral had been dead for eleven months. He had published six novels, none of them in a language I could read without a dictionary at my elbow and a small, familiar ache somewhere under the ribs. The seventh, it turned out, had been waiting in a drawer.

"Completion of the work by its translator," I said aloud to the kettle.

The kettle, being English, said nothing at all.

### [p02]
The manuscript arrived two days later in a cardboard box that had clearly travelled further than I had in years. Inside lay three hundred and twelve typed pages, held together with two black clips and something like faith. My father had refused computers on the grounds that they were impatient. He wrote on a grey typewriter that sounded, when I was small, like rain on the roof of a car, and he corrected by hand, in green ink, because blue, he said, was for clerks.

On the first page was the title: Son Vapur. The Last Ferry. On the second page was the list of contents, and it was there that the trouble began.

There were ten lines. Nine of them were chapter titles, neatly typed, each with its page number. The tenth line had no title and no number. It had only my name, written by hand in green ink: Aylin.

Nobody in Istanbul had found a tenth chapter. His publisher, a gentle man who had printed every one of my father's books and lost money on at least four of them, had searched the flat with my father's oldest friend. They had found receipts, three umbrellas, a tin of old ferry tokens and a great deal of tea. They had not found an ending. My father is believed to have written the whole book in the last two years of his life, at the kitchen table, with the window open, although nobody, not even his publisher, had been allowed to read a page of it until after the funeral.

"It would seem," Rosalind wrote in her second email, "that your father intended you to finish it."

She was being kind. She was also, I suspected, being practical. A novel with nine chapters is a problem, whereas a novel with nine chapters and a final chapter by the author's estranged daughter is a story, and stories sell. She hadn't used the word estranged. She hadn't needed to.

"It is essential that the final text be delivered by the end of March," the email went on, "should the spring publication date be maintained. Should you require any assistance with the Turkish material, please do let us know."

I was, I should point out, the Turkish material.

### [p03]
My mother came round on Sunday with a lemon cake and the expression of a woman who has read the situation before arriving. Judith Marsh is seventy-one, wears her reading glasses on a cord like a referee's whistle, and taught English for eleven years in Istanbul, where she met my father, married him and later, with great courtesy on both sides, stopped being married to him. I was six when we came back to Manchester. I have her surname and the first name he chose for me, which means that my name is a mixed marriage that has lasted rather longer than theirs did.

"Did he ever mention a tenth chapter?" I asked.

"Darling, he never mentioned the first nine. We spoke twice a year, about you and about the weather, and he only listened to the part about you."

"Could he have given it to someone?"

"He might well have. He gave everything away. Umbrellas, books, opinions." She cut the cake into exact eighths, as she has done all my life, as if somebody might check. "Are you going to finish it?"

"I'd rather not."

"That isn't the same as no."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

She looked at the box on the table the way she used to look at my school reports. "You do know he never learned a word of English? Thirty years. I taught half of Istanbul to say could you possibly pass the salt, and that man refused to learn please. He said his language was the only house he owned, and he was too old to move."

I knew. It was one of the facts of my life, like being left-handed or hating marzipan. My father and I had spoken Turkish when I was a child, and I had grown up, and my Turkish had not. Every conversation we had after the age of thirteen took place in a language that one of us had stopped growing in, and it wasn't him.

"Well," said my mother, brushing crumbs into her palm. "It's high time somebody in this family finished something."

### [p04]
I began that night, which is to say that I made a pot of tea, sharpened three pencils I did not need and read the first page eleven times.

The Last Ferry is the story of Nevzat, a man of sixty-eight who sells tickets in a small wooden window at Çınaraltı pier, on the Asian side of Istanbul. He has sold tickets there for thirty-one years. He knows which passengers will argue about the change and which will say thank you, and he knows the sea in all its moods, the way other men know football. Nevzat has a daughter, Deniz, who lives in a cold northern city across another sea, and who telephones on Sundays. The line is bad. Their conversations are worse.

That was all I knew after the first page, and I could have stopped there. I didn't. Having started, I found that I couldn't stop, which is not something I have ever been able to say about a dishwasher manual.

My father's Turkish was plain on the surface and deep underneath, like the water off the pier. There were no long words. There were long silences, though, and he wrote them in a way that made you hear them. Nevzat counting coins. Nevzat watching the last ferry come in with its lights on. Deniz on the telephone, saying "Baba, I have to go," in the same tone every week, as though it were a greeting rather than a goodbye.

I read all nine chapters in one night. By four in the morning the rain had stopped, my tea had gone cold twice and the ninth chapter had ended with Nevzat standing alone at the ticket window, holding something in his hand that the book did not name.

Then I turned the page.

There was no tenth chapter. There was a blank sheet of paper, the colour of weak tea, and on the list of contents, my name.

## [c2] Untranslatable

### [p05]
There is a word in Turkish, hüzün, which dictionaries translate as sadness and which is not sadness at all. Sadness is something that happens to you. Hüzün is more like weather that you live in: a soft, grey, shared melancholy that hangs over a city in winter and that nobody in particular is responsible for. You do not cry about it. You drink tea in it.

My father used the word seventeen times in nine chapters. I counted. I would like to say that I counted because I am a professional, but it was really because I had no idea what to do with it, and counting things is what I do when I have no idea what to do.

It is said that every translation is a small betrayal. Translators say this to each other at conferences, usually late in the evening, in the tone that surgeons use when they discuss lost patients. The truth is less dramatic. Every translation is a series of tiny decisions, each of which is defensible and none of which is quite right.

On page nine, Nevzat looks out at the water and feels hüzün. I tried melancholy, which sounded like a Victorian poet with a cough. I tried sadness, which sounded like a greetings card. I tried the grey mood of the city, which was accurate and dead.

Only after a week of this did I do the thing I had been avoiding. I left the word in Turkish. I let Nevzat feel hüzün, and I let the reader work out, from the rain and the tea and the empty benches, what it meant. Granted, it was a coward's solution. Even so, it was the first honest decision I had made about the book.

### [p06]
Gönül was harder. Gönül is the heart, but not the one that pumps blood. It is the part of you that agrees to things, or refuses them, before your mind has been consulted. When a Turk says that his gönül did not want something, he means that he cannot explain why he said no, and that you should not ask.

In chapter three, Deniz invites her father to visit her in the north. Nevzat says he will think about it, and then the narrator tells us, in five quiet words, that his gönül was willing but his feet were not. I spent two days on those five words. I ended up with "His heart said yes; his feet stayed at the pier," which my father would have hated, because it was too neat. He distrusted anything neat. He said neat sentences were like pressed shirts: very nice, and nobody could breathe in them.

Then there was kıyamamak, which is the worst of all, because it has no English cousin at all. It means to be unable to bring yourself to harm something, or spend it, or use it up, because you love it too much. You cannot kıyamamak a stranger. You kıyamamak a new pair of shoes you never wear, or a jar of your grandmother's jam, or a child.

My father used to say it to me when I was small. I had forgotten that until I found it on page one hundred and four, where Nevzat keeps a letter from Deniz in his breast pocket for two years and never opens it, because he cannot bring himself to. Sana kıyamam, my father would say, when I fell asleep on the ferry and he carried me home instead of waking me. I can't bear to, roughly. I can't bear to spend you.

I translated the line about the letter. Then I went out into the rain without an umbrella, which is not a thing that sensible people in Manchester do, and walked for an hour.

### [p07]
"I was wondering if you'd had a chance to look at my email," said Rosalind on the phone, a week later.

"I've been meaning to ring you," I said, which was true in the sense that I had picked up the phone four times and put it down again.

"Well, I've read your first chapter, and it's wonderful," she said. "Honestly, Aylin, it's the best thing we have had all year. The ferry. The tea. That bit with the letter in his pocket."

"Thank you."

"So you'll do the ending?"

I was standing at the window, watching a man across the road try to fold a pushchair that had opinions of its own. "I'm not sure I can write my father's ending," I said. "I'm not sure I'd know what it was."

"Nobody's asking you to know. We're asking you to write. You're his daughter. You know his voice better than anyone alive."

That was precisely the problem. I knew his voice the way you know a song in a language you don't speak: the shape of it, the rise and fall, but not the words.

"Can I think about it?"

"Of course. Although I should say," and here her register changed, as smoothly as a car changing gear, "that failure to deliver a complete manuscript by the agreed date would, regrettably, result in the postponement of publication until next autumn at the earliest."

"Rosalind, are you threatening me in nouns?"

She laughed, which I liked her for. "I'm being difficult, aren't I? I'm sorry. It's been a long week. I just think the book deserves to exist, and so do you."

I was going to point out that I already existed, with or without the book. I didn't, though, because I had a strong suspicion that she was right about the second part, and wrong about the first.

### [p08]
It was the photocopies that changed everything.

The publisher in Istanbul had sent me the original typed pages, but he had also sent a second set, copies of an earlier draft, because, as he explained in a polite note, "the author wrote many things in the margins, and a translator may find them useful". I had put the copies aside. Margins are where writers argue with themselves, and I had enough arguments of my own.

On the Thursday after Rosalind's call, I could not find the word I needed for a kind of ferry rope, so I opened the copies to see whether my father had changed it. He hadn't. But beside the paragraph, in small, careful handwriting, someone had written a note.

It was not in Turkish.

"Here she is angry but she is not angry," it said. "Is tired."

I turned the page. There were more. Next to Nevzat's first conversation with Deniz: "Do not make beautiful. Make true." Next to the word hüzün, on page nine, the very word that had cost me a week: "Not sad. Not melancholy. Ask A."

For a long, cold moment, I assumed they had been written by someone else. An editor, perhaps, or a friend who knew English, or one of my mother's old students, made helpful in retirement.

Then I looked at the capital A. My father wrote his capital A like a small tent with no crossbar, as if the wind had taken it. He wrote his sevens with a line through them. He pressed so hard with the pen that you could read his letters from the other side of the paper.

The notes in the margins were in my father's handwriting, and they were in English.

## [c3] Istanbul

### [p09]
I flew to Istanbul at the beginning of February, telling Rosalind that I needed to see the setting, telling my mother that I needed to see the flat, and telling myself nothing at all, which is a technique I have refined over many years.

My father had lived for the last twenty years of his life in Kemerburnu, a neighbourhood that slides down a hill on the Asian shore and stops, as if surprised, at the water. His flat was on the fourth floor of a narrow building the colour of old tea, and from its front window you could see the pier, the ticket window and the ferries coming and going across the grey water like slow, polite animals.

So that was where Nevzat had come from. Little did I know, standing on the pavement with my suitcase, how much else had come from that window.

I had not been in the flat since the week of the funeral, and then I had only been there for an afternoon, in a fog of paperwork and condolences, nodding at people whose names I would not remember. Grief, I had discovered, is mostly administration. You think it will be a great wave, and instead it is a long queue at an office where nobody can find the right form.

The key was with my father's friend, Selçuk Bey, who lived on the floor below. He opened his door before I had finished knocking, as if he had been standing behind it since nineteen eighty-five.

"Aylin," he said, and then, in Turkish, "you have your father's way of standing in a doorway. As though you are not sure you are allowed in."

I told him, in my careful, childish Turkish, that I was not sure I was.

He laughed and took my suitcase, and that was the end of the discussion.

### [p10]
Selçuk Bey was seventy-six and had spent forty years as an engineer on the ferries, which meant he could tell you the exact age of any boat on the water from the sound of its engine. He was small and round and wore a cardigan with leather patches on the elbows, and he spoke no English whatsoever. Everything he says in these pages, therefore, I am translating, and he would want me to tell you that he said it better.

He made tea in my father's kitchen, because, he explained, my father's tea had always been better than his own, and the glasses remembered.

"Nevzat is not me," he said, before I could ask. "Everybody thinks Nevzat is me, because of the ferries. Nevzat is him. The ferries are mine. He borrowed them."

"He never worked at the pier."

"No. But he sat by that window every evening for twenty years and watched the man who did. That is how your father worked. He did not do things. He watched them until he understood them, and then he wrote them down."

I asked him about the tenth chapter, and his face did something complicated.

"We looked," he said. "Your father's publisher and I. We looked in every drawer. Under the bed. Inside the typewriter. He kept the eighth chapter in the bread bin once, for a whole winter, so we looked there too."

"And?"

"And there was bread."

The study was exactly as I remembered it, only smaller, the way rooms from your childhood always are. The typewriter sat on the desk under a cloth. Beside it was a dictionary, fat and blue, its spine broken in so many places that it looked like a map of a river. I picked it up without thinking. It was an English dictionary. Turkish to English, English to Turkish.

"For the crossword," said Selçuk Bey, a little too quickly.

### [p11]
My father had never done a crossword in his life. He thought crosswords were for people who were afraid of silence.

I put the dictionary down and said nothing, which is a skill I inherited from him, along with the shape of my hands and an inability to throw away paper bags. For the next two days I searched the flat myself. Not because I distrusted Selçuk Bey and the publisher, but because there are some places only a daughter would think to look: the inside of a winter coat, the back of a framed photograph, the box of old letters under the wardrobe that turned out to contain mostly electricity bills and one drawing of a horse that I had done at the age of seven and that he had kept, apparently, for thirty-one years.

I found no chapter. I did, however, find the photocopies again, or rather their originals: the earlier draft, with its English notes in the margins, in a folder in the bottom drawer of his desk.

There was no mistaking it. The handwriting was his, the green ink was his, and the English was, if I am honest, terrible. Not terrible in the way that machine translations are terrible, which is fluent and empty. Terrible in the way that a child's drawing is terrible: wrong in all its details, and somehow exactly right about the horse.

"Selçuk Bey," I said that evening, holding up the folder. "Who wrote these?"

He was pouring tea, and he went on pouring it, slowly, as if the glass were very large.

"Did he have an English friend? Someone who helped him?"

"He had many friends."

"Selçuk Bey."

He put the teapot down. It was clear that he had promised something to someone, and it was equally clear that the someone was dead, which makes promises heavier rather than lighter.

"Your father," he said at last, "would rather I didn't tell you this."

"I would rather you did."

### [p12]
He sat down. Outside, the evening ferry was coming in, its lights wobbling on the water, and he watched it for a while, as if checking that it had been properly maintained.

"Four years ago," he said, "your father came down to my flat with a cake. He never brought cakes. So I knew something was wrong. I thought he was ill. Instead, he told me that he had found a teacher."

"A teacher of what?"

"I asked the same question. He said, of the language my daughter dreams in."

I want to describe exactly what I felt at that moment, because it seems important, and because I am supposed to be good with words. But the truth is that I did not feel anything for several seconds. It was like missing a step in the dark. Your body knows something has happened before your mind has agreed to it.

"That's not possible," I said. "He never learned English. Mum always said he refused. He told her his language was the only house he owned."

"That was true," said Selçuk Bey, "for thirty years. And then it wasn't." He shrugged, the large, slow shrug of a man who has seen engines fail for no reason and recover for no reason. "People are said to be too old to change. Nobody has told the people."

"Why didn't he tell me?"

"I asked him that too. He said he wanted to be good at it first. He said you were a professional, and he did not want to be your worst client."

I laughed, and then, rather to my surprise, I did not laugh.

Selçuk Bey pushed a glass of tea across the table towards me and looked politely out of the window while I recovered.

"He took English lessons," he said. "Every Tuesday, for three years."

## [c4] Childhood Turkish

### [p13]
Here is what I remember, in the order that it comes back to me, which is not the order in which it happened.

Baba is tall. Baba smells of ink and oranges. Baba says the ferry is called a vapur, and I think he is saying vapour, because it breathes out smoke like a kettle, and for years I believe that every ferry in the world is made of steam and will one day simply float up into the sky. I am five. I am not wrong, exactly.

A translator would call that a false friend: two words in two languages that look alike and mean different things. I have always thought the term was unfair. False friends are not false. They are simply friends who have misunderstood you, which is to say, most of them.

Baba buys simit from a man with a tray on his head. Baba throws the crusts to the seagulls, and the seagulls scream at him as if he owes them money. Baba calls me Ayım, my moon, because my name has the moon in it, and because, he says, I go round and round him and never land.

That is the child speaking. The adult would like to point out that my father's metaphors were not always as kind as they sounded. A moon that never lands is, after all, a moon that keeps its distance. That said, the adult was not there, and the child was, and the child remembers being carried home asleep from the ferry, her cheek against a wool coat, while a voice above her head said, very softly, sana kıyamam.

So that was what it meant. Not I love you, which is a statement. Something more like a refusal. I refuse to spend you. I refuse to wake you up.

### [p14]
We left Istanbul when I was six. After that I came back every summer until I was thirteen, and then every other summer, and then, as these things go, for weddings and for funerals, of which there were not many until there was his.

My Turkish stopped growing at about the age of ten. It is a perfectly good language for a ten-year-old. It can ask for ice cream, describe a cat, complain about a sister I never had and report on the weather in some detail. What it cannot do is argue, apologise, explain why you are going to university in a city he has never seen, or tell a man that you are sorry his book was badly reviewed. If I had kept up my Turkish as a teenager, I would now be able to read my father's novels without a dictionary. If I had read his novels, I might have known him. That second sentence is not a grammatical structure. It is a regret.

Our phone calls followed a pattern. He would ask a question, slowly, in the clear Turkish one uses for foreigners and children. I would answer in my ten-year-old voice, and then, when I ran out of words, I would finish my sentence in English, which he did not understand. Then he would say something in reply, and I would understand only the first half of it, and nod, and he could not see me nodding, because we were on the telephone.

Every sentence between us had two halves, and they never arrived at the same time.

Hardly had I put the phone down after one of these calls, at nineteen, when I burst into tears in the corridor of my student hall, and a girl from the floor above asked whether somebody had died. "No," I told her. "Nobody died. We just can't talk." She gave me a biscuit. It was the right thing to do, and it did not help at all.

### [p15]
On my last morning in the flat, I opened the drawer of his bedside table, which I had been avoiding. Bedside tables are where people keep the things they want to see last.

Inside, there was a pair of reading glasses, a box of throat sweets, a photograph of me at the pier in a yellow raincoat, aged about eight, and a thin school exercise book with a blue cover.

I knew it before I opened it. Some objects carry their own weather.

On the first page, in my handwriting at nine years old, enormous and leaning to the right as if into a strong wind, it said: Baba için İngilizce. English for Baba. Underneath was a list I had made for him one summer, apparently convinced that I could teach him in a fortnight what my mother had failed to teach him in eleven years.

Apple, elma. Ferry, vapur. Seagull, martı. Moon, ay. Then, with a little drawing of a heart that had gone slightly wrong and looked like a potato: I love you, seni seviyorum.

Beside each word there was a small pencil tick, very neat, the kind a teacher makes. I assumed I had put them there myself, at nine, being a thorough and rather bossy child. It did not occur to me to wonder why the ticks were so much smaller than my handwriting.

He had not learned a single one of them, as far as I knew. I remembered giving him the book, and I remembered him putting it in his pocket, and I remembered deciding, at nine, with the fierce logic of a child, that he didn't want my language because he didn't want me.

Such was my certainty that I carried it, mostly unexamined, for twenty-nine years.

On the inside of the back cover, in green ink, in the tent-shaped capitals that I now knew so well, he had written a name, a day, a time and an address. Dilek Hanım. Tuesday, five o'clock. Number four, Sakızlı Street.

## [c5] Tuesdays

### [p16]
I changed my flight, which cost me more than the apricot tree had paid me, and on Tuesday, at five o'clock exactly, I knocked on the door of number four, Sakızlı Street.

I could have gone on Monday. I could have telephoned. But it seemed important, for reasons I could not have defended in a court of law, to arrive at the hour he had arrived, on the day he had arrived, as though the house might recognise the time even if it did not recognise me.

Sakızlı Street turned out to be a short, steep lane of wooden houses, two streets back from the water, with washing lines strung between the windows like the rigging of a ship. Number four had a green door and a lemon tree in a pot that was far too small for it. The woman who opened the door was perhaps sixty-five, with silver hair cut very short and a cardigan buttoned to the neck, and she looked at me for a long moment before she spoke.

"You are Aylin," she said, in English. "You have his eyebrows. I am Dilek. Please, come in. I have been expecting you for some time, though not, I confess, on a Tuesday."

Her name means wish. It also begins, I noticed with a translator's pettiness, with the Turkish word for language, which is dil, and which also means tongue. A wish with a tongue in it. My father would have liked that. He would, I realised, almost certainly have noticed it first.

Her English was beautiful in the way that very old furniture is beautiful: solid, well made and slightly out of fashion. She had learned it, she told me, from books and from the radio, and she had taught it for thirty-four years at a secondary school on the hill, where she had been known to the pupils, not entirely affectionately, as the Grammar.

"Your father," she said, pouring tea, "was the worst student I ever had. And the best. You will want to know which, I expect."

"I'd like to know both."

"So would I," she said. "I have been trying to decide for three years."

### [p17]
He had arrived on her doorstep, she said, on a Tuesday in early spring, carrying a cake and a fat blue dictionary, and announced in Turkish that he wished to learn English and that he had no talent for it whatsoever. She had told him that talent was not required, only Tuesdays. He had given her a look, she said, that suggested he had heard that kind of thing before, from optimists.

"He was not quick," she said. "He was stubborn. Those are different things, but in the long run, stubborn wins."

Not only did he refuse to give up, it seemed, but he also refused to give up his mistakes. He collected them. He had favourites.

"I am agree," said Dilek Hanım, with the weary affection of a woman who has heard a sentence several hundred times. "Every Turkish student says it, because in Turkish we say we are of the same opinion. So I corrected him. I said, Hakan Bey, agree is a verb. We say I agree. He said, I know. I said, then why do you keep saying I am agree? And he said, because agreement is not a thing you do. It is a place where you are. When I agree with someone, I am standing next to them. I am agree."

I laughed, and she smiled, the slightly pained smile of a teacher who has lost an argument on a point of grammar and won it on a point of poetry.

"And the articles," she said. "The and a. You know we have no the in Turkish. Some students put it nowhere. Some put it everywhere. Your father did both, depending on the weather. He said English has too many the, and one day he would give some back."

"And the third person s?"

"Ah. He goes, she goes. He said, why I must put s? Everybody already know it is him." She sighed. "I gave him exercises. He did all of them. And then he went home and made the same mistakes on purpose, beautifully, as if they were jewellery."

### [p18]
I asked her the question I had flown nearly two thousand miles to ask, although I had not known I was going to ask it until it was out of my mouth.

"Why? After thirty years. Why then?"

Dilek Hanım put her glass down and folded her hands in her lap, the way teachers do when they are about to say something that is not on the syllabus.

"He told me in our first lesson," she said. "In Turkish, because he had no English yet. He said, my daughter lives in English. I have been visiting her for thirty years in my language, and I have always arrived at the wrong door. Before I die, I would like to knock on the right one."

For a while neither of us said anything. A ferry sounded somewhere below the houses, two long notes, and a cat walked along the top of the garden wall with the air of an inspector.

"He was supposed to come to you the following April," she said gently. "Did you know? He had bought a coat. A proper English coat, for the rain. He brought it here to show me. He was very proud of the buttons."

I did not know. I could not have known, because he had not told me, and he had not told me because he had wanted to arrive with his sentences ready, like a man who irons his shirt before a visit. He had died in his sleep that October, with the coat hanging on the back of his bedroom door. I had seen it there, I realised. In the week of the funeral. I had thought it was a coat.

"The last year," said Dilek Hanım, "he stopped doing my exercises. He was writing something else. One long piece of homework, every week a few more pages. He would not let me read it. I asked many times. He said, this one I will not be corrected."

### [p19]
She went into the next room and came back with an envelope. It was large and brown, the sort that offices use, and it had been sealed with tape, and then with more tape, in the manner of a man who does not trust envelopes.

On the front, in green ink, in Turkish, he had written a single line. I read it twice before I trusted myself to translate it, even in my head.

Dokuzuncu bölüm çevrilmeden açılmayacak. Not to be opened until the ninth chapter has been translated.

"He gave it to me at our last lesson," said Dilek Hanım. "In September. He made me promise to give it to you if you came, and to keep it if you did not. I did not know whether you would come. I would like to say I was sure. I was not."

"Why didn't you send it to me?"

"Because he said you would come when you were ready, and that the address in the little blue book would bring you. He said you always found things in the end. He said it was your best and most irritating quality."

I took the envelope. It was heavier than I expected, and I held it in both hands, like something that might spill.

"What if I opened it now?" I asked. "Here. Tonight. I've translated eight chapters. Surely eight is nearly nine."

Dilek Hanım looked at me over her glasses, and for a moment I was twelve again, and had not done my homework, and was being looked at by every teacher I had ever had.

"Should you open it before the ninth chapter is finished," she said, "I shall not know, of course. But you will. And I suspect that was the point."

I walked back down to the water in the dusk, holding the envelope against my chest like a schoolgirl holding a report she has not yet dared to read. The last ferry was just leaving Kemerburnu, all its lights on, and in the little wooden window at the pier a man I did not know was counting coins.

## [c6] Chapter Nine

### [p20]
The envelope came back to Manchester in my hand luggage, because I did not trust it to the hold, and it spent the next three weeks on the shelf above my desk, between a Turkish grammar and a dictionary of legal terms, watching me work like a cat that wants feeding.

I told my mother on the telephone. I told her about Selçuk Bey and the dictionary, about Dilek Hanım and the Tuesdays, about the coat on the back of the bedroom door. She listened without interrupting, which she had not done since about nineteen ninety-four, and when I had finished there was a long silence on the line.

"Three years," she said eventually. "Three years of Tuesdays. I asked him for one Tuesday, in nineteen ninety-one. One. So that he could say hello to my parents without me translating. He told me his language was the language of the ferry, and that a man does not change ferries in the middle of the water."

"I'm sorry, Mum."

"Don't be. I'm not jealous, darling. I'm being seventy-one, that's all. It comes and goes." I heard her put the kettle on. "Open the envelope."

"I promised."

"You promised a woman in Istanbul you will probably never see again."

"I promised him," I said, and was surprised to find that it was true.

So I translated chapter nine. Slowly, because it was the last one, and because I had noticed that I was slowing down, in the way you slow down towards the end of a book you do not want to finish, or a visit you do not want to end.

In chapter nine, Nevzat goes into a second-hand bookshop near the pier and buys a dictionary of his daughter's language. It is fat and blue, and its spine is already broken in several places, as though somebody has been searching it for years for a word they never found.

I put my pencil down and sat very still for some time.

### [p21]
It would be an exaggeration to say that chapter nine was about me. It would also be an exaggeration to say that it was not.

Nevzat keeps the dictionary under the counter of the ticket window, and between ferries he studies it, moving his lips. He tries his new words on the tourists, who are mostly confused by them. He says good morning to everybody, regardless of the hour, because it is the only greeting he is sure of, and soon the regular passengers start saying good morning back to him at half past six in the evening, and nobody can remember why.

On Sunday, Deniz telephones. It is raining in her city, she tells him. It is always raining in her city. She is wearing, she says, a yellow raincoat, because when she was small her father bought her one at the pier and she has never been able to wear any other colour since. I had a yellow raincoat once. I had it in the photograph in his bedside drawer.

Deniz begins a sentence in Turkish and finishes it in her other language, and Nevzat, for the first time, understands the second half. He does not tell her. He wants, the narrator says, to arrive at the right door.

Arguably, this is the point at which a translator ought to recuse herself, in the way that a judge does when the defendant turns out to be her cousin. One might suggest that I was no longer reading the book so much as being read by it. Even so, the work had to be done, and I was the only one who could do it, and so I did it, with a box of tissues next to the dictionary of legal terms, which I suspect is not where tissues are usually kept.

Not once did my father use my name in those nine chapters. He did not need to. He had used the raincoat.

### [p22]
The last page of chapter nine is very short. I had read it once, at four in the morning, months before, and had remembered it only as an ending without an ending: Nevzat standing at the ticket window after the last ferry has gone, holding something in his hand that the book does not name.

Reading it again, slowly, in order to translate it, I saw what I had missed. The book did not name the thing. It described it. It was thin, with a soft cover, and its corners had been turned up by many years in a coat pocket, and Nevzat held it, the narrator says, the way you hold a ticket for a journey you have already paid for.

It was an exercise book. Of course it was. It had always been an exercise book.

The final line of the chapter reads, in Turkish, Gerisini ona kendi dilinde söyleyecekti. I translated it as "The rest he would tell her in her own language," and then I sat and looked at it for a long time, because there was nothing wrong with it, and because there was nothing more to do.

It was three o'clock in the morning. I saved the file, and saved it again, and sent it to Rosalind with a message that said, in its entirety, "Nine." Then I took the envelope down from the shelf.

I had held it on the ferry, and in the taxi, and on the plane. I had lifted it a dozen times since, to dust under it, which I never do, or to move it a few centimetres to the left, which serves no purpose at all. I knew its weight the way you know the weight of your own bag.

Even so, I weighed it again in my hand, and this time I let myself notice what I had been refusing to notice for three weeks.

It was far too heavy to be a letter.

## [c7] The Envelope

### [p23]
I telephoned my mother at seven, which is an hour she regards as theoretical. I needn't have worried that she would be annoyed. She arrived at eight in a raincoat buttoned over what were clearly pyjamas, with two croissants in a paper bag and the air of a woman attending an emergency that she had been expecting for thirty years.

"Have you opened it?"

"Not yet. I thought I'd wait."

"For what? Christmas?"

"For you," I said, and she stopped taking her coat off halfway, so that for a moment she looked like a woman who had been partly unwrapped.

We sat at the kitchen table with the envelope between us. Neither of us touched it. She told me, instead, about the day she met my father, which was a story I had heard many times, but never like this.

She had been twenty-eight, and new to Istanbul, and lost. She had asked him for directions in her best Turkish, which at that time consisted mostly of numbers and fruit. He had not answered. He had simply walked her to the place she was looking for, twenty minutes up a hill in the heat, without saying a word, and then bowed slightly, and walked away.

"I thought he was rude," she said. "It took me a year to realise that he had not known how to speak to me, and so he had decided to walk instead. That was your father all over. If he didn't have the words, he did something instead. Usually something enormous and inconvenient."

"Like a novel."

"Like a novel." She looked at the envelope. "Or forty pages of homework. I'm guessing. I could be wrong."

"I hope not," I said, and then, "I don't know what I hope."

"Nobody ever does, darling. That's what makes hoping so tiring." She pushed the envelope an inch towards me with one finger, as you might push a plate towards a child. "Go on. I'm here."

### [p24]
It took me some time to get through the tape. My father had wrapped it round and round, as if the envelope were a parcel being sent to sea, and in the end I had to use the kitchen scissors, carefully, along the top, while my mother held her breath on my behalf.

Inside there was no letter.

There were pages: thirty-eight of them, as I counted later, torn from school exercise books, the lined kind with a red margin down the side. They were covered on both sides in green ink, in the small, careful, heavy handwriting I had first seen beside the word hüzün, the handwriting that pressed so hard that every page carried a ghost of the one before it.

On top lay a small white card, the kind that comes with flowers.

Having spent most of my adult life deciding what other people's words meant, I found I could not read a single one of them. My eyes simply slid off the page. It is a known phenomenon among translators, apparently: the sudden, total inability to read a text that matters too much. I had always assumed it was a myth, like the translator who works best at night. I now know both to be true.

My mother picked up the card, looked at it for a long moment, and then, without a word, handed it to me.

It said: "For translator. Please, no correct. Mistake is mine. I am pay for them three years."

I laughed. Then I put my hand over my mouth, because the laugh had turned into something else halfway through, the way a ferry horn changes note as it goes past you.

"He's being impossible," said my mother, rather unsteadily. "Even now. Even dead, he's being impossible." She took her glasses off and polished them on the sleeve of her pyjamas. "Well? What does it say?"

### [p25]
I did not answer at once, because I had just understood something, and it needed a moment to arrive.

For translator. Not for Aylin. Not for my daughter. He had addressed the card to a job, not a person, and he had done it, I was certain, on purpose. He had known exactly who would open the envelope. He had simply chosen to give me the one name I had never refused.

I thought about the list of contents, with its nine typed lines and its one handwritten name. Everybody had read that name as a wish, or as an instruction: finish this for me. Rosalind had read it that way. So had I. It would appear, however, that we had all been wrong, and that he had meant something much simpler. Chapter ten: Aylin. Not the author of it. Its translator.

Ask A, he had written beside the word hüzün. I had thought it was a note to himself, a question he had meant to ask me and never had. It had been, I now saw, an instruction to his translator.

Had I known on that Sunday in Manchester what was waiting in Istanbul, I would have flown out the next morning. Had I known what was in the box, I would have opened it differently, more slowly, with both hands. Were it not for Dilek Hanım and her terrible honesty, I would have opened this envelope in a hotel room in Istanbul, alone, with eight chapters behind me instead of nine, and I would not have understood half of what I was about to read. If I had opened it in Istanbul, I would still be trying to understand it now.

That was the point, of course. She had been right. He had wanted me to walk the whole road first. You cannot understand the second half of a sentence if you have refused to hear the first.

So I picked up the first page. The title was written at the top, underlined twice. I am not going to tell you what it was yet, because he did not tell me until the end.

The first line was in English.

### [p26]
It was not good English.

I want to be precise about this, because it matters, and because precision is the only thing I have ever been paid for. It was not good English. It would not have passed an examination, nor would it have satisfied Dilek Hanım, who had spent three years trying to put the articles back where they belonged. It was full of the mistakes that every Turkish learner makes, and that I had spent my whole career quietly, invisibly removing from other people's documents, the way you remove fingerprints from a glass before you hand it back.

It was also, unmistakably, not a mistake. Every error in it had been chosen. You could see it in the handwriting: in the places where he had written the correct word, crossed it out, and written the wrong one again, very clearly, above it. By the same token, every correct word that he had crossed out was a word he knew. So hard had he pressed on the paper that the crossed-out words could still be read from the other side.

If he had agreed to learn English in nineteen ninety-one, when my mother asked him to, I would not be holding these pages now. I would, perhaps, be holding something better: thirty years of ordinary conversations, about nothing, in a language we both understood. I have thought about that a great deal since. I am still not sure it would have been better. I am only sure it would have been different.

My mother leaned against my shoulder, as she had not done since I was a teenager, and read the line aloud, slowly, in the careful voice she had used for thirty years to read to beginners.

"Deniz," she read. "This chapter I am write in your language, because in my language I am always arrive too late."

## [c8] The Other Half

### [p27]
I will not quote the whole chapter. It belongs to the book, and the book belongs, now, to anyone who buys it. But I will tell you how it goes, because I read it that morning at my kitchen table, with my mother's head on my shoulder and the croissants going cold in their bag, and because I have read it perhaps a hundred times since, and it has not once gone the same way.

It begins as a letter from Nevzat to Deniz. Then, after a page or two, it forgets that it is a letter and becomes the story again, and then it forgets that it is the story and becomes a letter again, as though it cannot quite decide which of the two it is allowed to be. Dilek Hanım would have put a red line through half of it. I would not change a word.

"In my language I have many word for sea," it says, "and no word for you. In your language I have only few word. But every one is for you."

Nevzat takes the ferry. Then he takes an aeroplane, which, the chapter explains, is a ferry for people who are in a hurry. He arrives in Deniz's city, and of course it is raining, and of course he has a coat for the rain, with very good buttons.

Halfway through, the chapter stops and simply lists some words. Apple, elma. Ferry, vapur. Seagull, martı. Moon, ay. "You write this for me when you are nine," it says. "I put in my pocket. I am not open it many year, because I cannot spend it. In my language is one word for this. Is not possible to translate. Ask A."

At the end, Nevzat stands outside his daughter's door in the rain, and he does not knock.

"Sentence is have two half," he says. "First half is mine, and I am say it now. I am sorry I am late. Other half is yours."

### [p28]
There is a version of this story in which I spend the following week in a state of quiet, dignified grief, and say wise things to my mother, and walk by the canal in the rain. The truth is that I spent most of the following week on the telephone to Rosalind.

"It's extraordinary," she said, when she had read it. She sounded as though she had been crying, and was cross with herself about it. "Truly. I mean that. It's also, of course, not in Turkish."

"No."

"And not entirely in English, either."

"It's in his English."

"Yes. Quite." I heard her shift into the other register, the one with the nouns. "Obviously, a degree of light editorial intervention would be expected, purely in the interests of clarity and the reader's comfort. The correction of the more noticeable grammatical irregularities need not affect the emotional impact of the text."

I had prepared for this. I had, in fact, written down what I was going to say, which I never do, and which my father would have understood perfectly.

"Rosalind," I said, "I must insist that the chapter be printed exactly as he wrote it. Every missing article. Every missing s. If Halyard would rather correct it, then Halyard will need to find another translator, and, I'm afraid, another father."

There was a long pause, the kind of pause in which a publisher looks at a calendar.

"Were it not for the fact that you're entirely right," she said at last, "I would fight you on this."

"I know."

"Granted, it's a risk. Some readers will think it's a mistake."

"It is a mistake," I said. "That's the point. It's three years of mistakes. He paid for them."

My mother, who had come round with a cake and was listening from the sofa with no pretence of doing anything else, gave me a silent round of applause. Afterwards she told me that it was the first time she had ever heard me win an argument with a publisher, and that I had sounded exactly like him, only in the right language.

### [p29]
In the end, we agreed on a compromise, which is to say that Rosalind agreed with me and called it a compromise. The chapter would be printed as written. Before it, there would be a translator's note, no longer than a page.

I wrote that note eleven times. The first ten versions were either too clever or too sad, and the eleventh was, I hope, neither. It reads, in part:

"The tenth chapter of this novel was written by the author in English, a language he began to learn at the age of seventy-one, every Tuesday, for three years. It has not been corrected. The reader will find in it many errors of the kind that Turkish speakers commonly make in English: missing articles, verbs without their final s, and the phrase I am agree, which one teacher in Istanbul has called the most stubborn mistake in the language. The translator would ask the reader to consider that, in this chapter, these are not errors of knowledge but choices. When the author writes I am agree, he does not mean that he agrees. He means that he is standing beside you."

I sent a copy to Selçuk Bey, who could not read it, and who telephoned to tell me that it was very good. I sent one to Dilek Hanım, who could, and who rang me the following Tuesday at five o'clock precisely.

"You have used a semicolon in the third sentence," she said. "I would not have. But I am agree with everything else."

I needn't have written it eleven times, then. She had found the only thing wrong with it in under a minute, and it was a semicolon.

Then she told me something I had not known. In their final lesson, the day he had given her the envelope, she had asked him what she should say if I came, and if I asked why he had never told me.

"He said, tell her I was not hiding," said Dilek Hanım. "Tell her I was practising."

### [p30]
The Turkish edition was due out in the autumn, a few months after the English one. In April, my father's Istanbul publisher wrote to me, gently, in Turkish, to point out a problem. Chapter ten, as he had now seen, was not in Turkish. Somebody would have to translate it. He wondered whether I knew of anyone.

So I did the last thing my father had left for me to do. I translated his English into Turkish.

I did it in my childhood Turkish, mostly, because that was the Turkish he had been talking to all along, and I kept his mistakes wherever Turkish would allow me to, and invented new ones where it would not. Where he had written I am agree, I wrote katılıyorum, which means I agree, but which also, if you take it apart the way he taught me to take things apart, means I am joining. I am joining you. I am getting on board.

It took me three weeks. It was the hardest translation I have ever done and the only one I have ever done in the right direction.

When it was finished, I looked again at the title he had written at the top of the first page, in green ink, underlined twice. Other Half of Sentence. He had left out both articles, naturally. I have put them back for the title of this book, and nowhere else.

I am writing this in April, in Manchester, in the rain. I am wearing a coat that is slightly too big for me, with very good buttons, which arrived last month in a parcel from Kemerburnu, wrapped in a great deal of tape. Across the road a man is still losing his argument with a pushchair.

My father wrote nine chapters in Turkish and one in English, and left the other half of the sentence, it turns out, to me.

Katılıyorum, Baba. I am agree.
