## [c1] Health to Your Hand

### [p01]
The translation earpiece worked perfectly, until Selin's family started using idioms.

Before that, Bruno Costa had been very proud of it. He had tested it for three weeks in London, where he and Selin both worked as programmers. He had tested it with Turkish films and Turkish cooking videos. Every time, a calm voice in his right ear had turned the Turkish into clear, correct English.

"It's amazing," he had told Selin. "With this, I'll understand everything your family says."

Selin had smiled in a way that he didn't completely understand. "Everything they say," she repeated. "Yes. That's exactly what worries me."

Now they were sitting in the back of a taxi in Eskişehir, crossing a bridge over a slow green river. Old houses in pink, blue and yellow stood along the water like a box of sweets. Bruno, who was thirty-one and had given talks to three hundred people, felt his hands getting wet.

"Tell me again," he said. "Your father. What does he like?"

"Trains," said Selin. "Silence. Tea. People who don't talk too much."

"I talk too much."

"I know." She took his hand. "He'll like you anyway. Probably."

"Probably?"

### [p02]
Selin had explained a lot about her father on the plane. Orhan Bey was sixty-four. He had worked on the railways for almost forty years, building bridges and tunnels. Since he had retired, he had spent most of his time in a small room at the top of the house, where he built model trains.

"He doesn't laugh much," she had said. "It isn't personal. He just doesn't."

"Has he ever laughed?"

"Once, when I was eleven. Something about a cat."

There was one more thing Bruno knew. A year ago, Selin had told her parents about him, and her father had asked only one question: could he make tea? Bruno could not make tea, at least not the Turkish way, with two pots, one on top of the other. He had been practising in London for a month, and he had already burned the bottom of one pot.

The earpiece had come in a neat white box with two small pieces, one for each speaker. An app on Bruno's phone did the listening and the translating, and sent the words to both of them. Bruno had planned to give the second earpiece to Orhan Bey, so that the older man could understand him too.

"Let me give it to him," Selin had said, taking it out of the box. "He doesn't accept presents from strangers."

"I'm not a stranger. I'm going to marry his daughter."

"Exactly. That's why you're a stranger."

### [p03]
The house was at the end of a narrow street, and it was painted the colour of a ripe peach. Before the taxi had stopped, the front door was opened and Selin's mother came running out. Nermin Hanım was small and quick, and she hugged Bruno as if she had known him for twenty years. Then she held his face in both hands and said something warm.

"You have become thin," said the earpiece in Bruno's ear. "They do not feed you in that country. We will fix this."

Behind her came Emre, Selin's younger brother, who was nineteen and studied English at the university. He shook Bruno's hand and whispered, "Good luck. Seriously."

Then Orhan Bey appeared in the doorway.

He was tall and serious, and his thick grey hair was a little too long at the sides, so that it covered the tops of his ears. Then he said two words in Turkish.

"You came pleasantly," said the earpiece.

Bruno smiled widely. "Thank you! Yes, I came very pleasantly. The taxi was comfortable, and the driver was excellent."

Selin closed her eyes. Later, she explained that the correct answer was "We found it pleasant", and that nobody in her family had ever reviewed the taxi.

Orhan Bey said nothing. He only touched his ear, as if something had bitten it, and went back inside.

### [p04]
Dinner had been prepared since early morning, and it covered every part of the table. There were stuffed peppers, a pot of beans, rice, a salad of tomatoes and fresh herbs, and something made of meat and potatoes that Bruno would remember for the rest of his life.

He ate everything, and then Nermin Hanım put a second plate in front of him without asking.

Bruno wanted to say something nice to her. He had prepared a sentence, but before he could use it, Orhan Bey looked at his wife and said quietly, "Eline sağlık."

"Health to your hand," said the earpiece.

In his experience, when people said "health" at a dinner table, they were making a toast. He picked up his glass of water, lifted it high and said loudly to Orhan Bey, "Health to your hand too!"

Everyone stopped eating.

Emre put his fork down very slowly.

Selin leaned towards Bruno. "You say it to the person who cooked," she whispered. "It means thank you for the food, your hands did something good. Dad didn't cook anything."

"Oh," said Bruno. "So I've just wished health to the only hands at this table that did nothing."

"Yes."

"Can I take it back?"

"No."

### [p05]
There was a long silence. Bruno could hear a clock somewhere in the house, and his own heart.

When Bruno was nervous, he always went back to Portuguese, the language he had grown up with in Brazil. He didn't even notice he was doing it. So he looked down at his plate and said quietly in Portuguese the sentence that means "That's it, I've ruined everything." Word for word, however, the sentence says something different. It says, "I've kicked the bucket."

Across the table, Orhan Bey's face went very still.

He touched his ear again. His mouth moved once, strangely, as if he had bitten something sour. Then he put down his napkin, stood up without a smile and walked into the kitchen. The door closed behind him.

"Is he angry?" Bruno whispered.

"He's fine," said Selin, a little too fast.

"He must have understood something from my face. My face is terrible when I'm nervous."

"Your face is fine. Eat your beans."

Emre looked at his sister, and his sister looked at the ceiling. Nobody explained anything.

Five minutes later, Orhan Bey came back, sat down, and continued eating as if nothing had happened. His eyes were slightly red. Bruno decided it must have been the peppers.

## [c2] Börek Disaster

### [p06]
The next morning, Bruno woke up at six with one clear plan. If he couldn't win Orhan Bey's heart with words, he would win it with actions.

He found the kitchen empty and the tea pots on the shelf. He had been studying videos about Turkish tea for weeks. He filled the bottom pot with water and put the tea leaves in the top one, exactly as the videos had shown him. In Turkey, tea has been made this way for a very long time.

He was watching the water with great concentration when Orhan Bey walked in.

Neither of them spoke. Orhan Bey sat down at the table with his newspaper and watched the tea instead. When Bruno poured the first glass, it was dark orange, which was correct, and almost full, which, Bruno learned later, was not. A good glass of tea has a little space at the top for your fingers.

Orhan Bey tasted it. He did not make a face, and he did not say anything. He just drank the whole glass and pushed it back across the table for another one.

Bruno decided that this was the best review of his life.

Then Nermin Hanım came in, saw him washing the pots and said something with a big smile.

"May it come easy," said the earpiece.

"May what come easy?" Bruno asked.

### [p07]
Emre, who had come down in his socks, tried to explain. "Kolay gelsin. You say it to anyone who is working. It means, I hope your work is not too hard."

"And what's the English for it?"

"There isn't one," said Emre. "English people just watch you working and say nothing, whereas we always say something. It's very sad."

Five minutes later, Emre reached for the sugar and knocked his father's favourite tea glass off the table. It was a thin old glass with a gold line around the top, and it had been used every morning for thirty years. It broke into three pieces on the floor.

Emre went white. Orhan Bey was in the garden, but his steps were already coming back towards the door.

Bruno didn't think. He picked up the pieces, and when Orhan Bey came in, he held them up and said in English, "I'm so sorry. It was me. I'll buy you a new one."

Then, quietly, he said to Emre in Portuguese the phrase that means "Relax, I'll take the blame." Word for word, it says, "Relax, I'll pay the duck."

Orhan Bey looked at the pieces of glass. He looked at Emre's white face. He touched his ear. Then he turned round, walked straight back into the garden and stood with his back to the house, his shoulders moving up and down.

"He's crying," said Bruno, horrified. "I've broken his favourite glass and now he's crying."

"I don't think so," said Emre, looking at his father very strangely.

### [p08]
That afternoon, the whole house was preparing for the evening. A large group of relatives would be arriving at seven, and Nermin Hanım was making börek, which was a kind of pastry with very thin layers and cheese inside.

Bruno stood at the kitchen door. "I was wondering if I could help," he said, and Selin translated it.

Nermin Hanım looked at him the way a doctor looks at a patient, and then gave him a small brush, a bowl of melted butter and a very simple job: put butter on each layer, carefully, all the way to the corners.

He did it so beautifully that, when the tray went into the oven, Nermin Hanım touched his cheek and said something that the earpiece translated as "Your hands are made of gold."

Then her phone rang. A neighbour needed her urgently, something about a ladder. She put on her shoes, pointed at the oven and gave Bruno one clear instruction, which Selin passed on from the garden: he should not touch anything, and he should take the tray out when the top was golden.

Bruno watched the oven. The top was not golden. After ten minutes, it was still not golden. He thought about efficiency, which was his job. If the oven were a little hotter, he reasoned, the top would turn golden sooner.

So he turned the temperature up. Then, because he was a careful man, he turned it up a little more.

### [p09]
Twenty minutes later, the top of the börek was not golden. It was black.

The smoke alarm went off. Emre and Selin ran in with towels. Bruno stood in the middle of the kitchen holding the tray, while smoke went up to the ceiling like a small grey ghost.

When the windows had been opened and the alarm had finally stopped, they looked at the damage. The bottom half was fine. The top half looked like a road.

"If I hadn't touched the oven," Bruno said sadly, "it would have been perfect."

"If you hadn't touched the oven," said Selin, "we would have had a normal afternoon."

Bruno did his best to save it. He carefully cut away the black top, and he was eating some of the less burned pieces, to see whether they were still good, when Nermin Hanım came back.

She looked at the tray and the open windows. Then, to his surprise, she laughed.

Bruno felt so grateful that he said, "Your börek is still the best thing I have ever eaten, even the black part."

Nermin Hanım smiled and replied, "Ağzına sağlık."

"Health to your mouth," said the earpiece.

Bruno went red. She must have seen him eating the burned pieces. "I'm very sorry," he said. "I'll go and brush my teeth."

Emre sat down on the kitchen floor and laughed until he couldn't breathe.

### [p10]
Later, Selin explained that "Health to your mouth" was the polite answer when someone said something kind. It had nothing to do with teeth.

"Your language," said Bruno, "is a trap."

"Says the man who paid a duck this morning."

He stared at her. "How do you know about the duck? You don't speak Portuguese."

"Emre told me," said Selin quickly. "He looked it up."

Then Orhan Bey came into the kitchen. He saw the black börek on the side and the brush in Bruno's hand, and he raised one eyebrow.

Bruno started explaining the oven and the temperature. After two minutes, he realised he was saying the same thing for the third time, and he told himself in Portuguese to stop filling sausage, which is what people in Brazil say when someone talks a lot and says nothing.

Orhan Bey touched his ear. Then he walked out of the kitchen very quickly, and a moment later, the bathroom door was locked.

That evening, while he was getting ready, Bruno opened the app on his phone. A small line at the top said: two earpieces connected.

"That's strange," he said to Selin. "Where's the second one?"

"In my bag, probably," she said. "It must still be switched on."

He put his earpiece on the charger and went to choose a shirt. At five to seven, with the bell already ringing, he put it in his ear. It made a small, sad sound, and then nothing at all.

## [c3] No Earpiece

### [p11]
The charger, it turned out, had been plugged into a socket that only worked when the kitchen light was on. Nobody in the family had told him, because nobody in the family remembered.

There was no time to fix it. Fourteen relatives were coming through the door, and every single one of them wanted to meet the Brazilian.

Bruno had been trained to work under pressure and to stay calm when a computer system failed in front of a client. Nobody, however, had trained him to be kissed on both cheeks by fourteen strangers while answering questions in a language he didn't speak.

Selin was trapped in the kitchen with her aunts, and Emre had been sent to the shop for bread. So Bruno stood in the living room, smiling until his face hurt.

He remembered a saying from home and said it quietly to himself, in Portuguese. Word for word, it says: "Who doesn't have a dog hunts with a cat." It means that when you don't have the right tool, you use the tool you have.

He didn't have a dog. He decided to hunt with a cat.

He used his hands. He used the six Turkish words he knew. When Selin's uncle Kemal asked him something long and serious, he drew a picture on a napkin: a plane, a heart, and a small house with a peach-coloured wall. Uncle Kemal studied it and then hugged him, which Bruno decided to count as a success.

### [p12]
Things went well for about an hour. Then they went less well.

Bruno had discovered that the safest answer to any question was "yes" in Turkish, said with a big smile. It had worked with the aunts, who had asked whether he was hungry. It had worked with the grandmother, who had asked whether he loved Selin. So when a cousin with a moustache asked him something in a hopeful voice, Bruno said yes again.

Ten minutes later, he found out that he had agreed to sing a Brazilian song for everyone.

He sang it. He sang it badly, with his eyes closed, and the relatives clapped in time and shouted something that he hoped was kind. Then a little girl asked him something, he said yes again, and the whole room cheered. He never found out what that one was. It might have been a promise to visit her school.

Through all of this, Orhan Bey sat in his armchair in the corner and watched. He didn't sing and he didn't clap. If only he would smile, Bruno thought, just once. Although his face showed nothing, he didn't leave the room once, which, Bruno thought, was a kind of progress.

At nine o'clock, the children started asking about something called "the trains". Orhan Bey stood up, looked at Bruno and pointed at the stairs. It wasn't an invitation exactly. It was more like an order. Bruno followed him.

### [p13]
The room at the top of the house was the most beautiful thing Bruno had seen in Turkey, and he had seen the börek before it burned.

A model railway covered three tables. There were hills made of paper, a river made of blue glass, tiny stations, and a bridge with stone arches that had clearly been built by hand.

Orhan Bey pressed a switch, and a little red train came out of a tunnel.

Then, in the middle of the bridge, the train stopped. Orhan Bey pressed the switch again. Nothing happened.

Bruno got down on his knees and looked under the table. In a world without language, at last, there was something he understood: cables. One of them had come loose from a small grey box. He pointed at it and raised his eyebrows. Orhan Bey looked at him for a second and then nodded.

Bruno fixed it in thirty seconds, and the red train continued across the bridge.

Orhan Bey didn't say anything. However, he took a photograph down from the wall and showed it to Bruno. It showed a young man in work clothes standing on a real bridge, high above a valley. He tapped the young man, then tapped his own chest.

"You built it," said Bruno in English, pointing.

Orhan Bey nodded. For a moment, Bruno was sure that the corners of his mouth were about to move.

Then one of the children knocked over a tiny cow, and the moment was gone.

### [p14]
Later, when the relatives had finally started to leave, Bruno carried a pile of plates to the kitchen. He was feeling quite good. Despite the song, the yes, and the cow, he thought the evening had been a success. If somebody had told him that morning that he would survive fourteen relatives without a single translated word, he would never have believed it.

The kitchen door was almost closed. Behind it, he could hear voices.

He stopped.

It was Nermin Hanım, speaking very quietly. Then there was another sound, deeper. It was the sound of somebody trying very hard not to laugh, like a laugh being pushed into a towel. Then the deep voice said something, and Bruno heard one word that he recognised clearly, because it was his own name.

Then both of them laughed, low and helpless, as if they couldn't stop.

Bruno stood in the dark corridor with the plates in his hands. He felt his face go hot.

They were laughing at him. Of course they were. The man who toasted the wrong hands, burned the börek and offered to brush his teeth. He should have known. He should have stayed in London and sent a nice card.

He put the plates down on the little table outside the kitchen, very quietly, and went upstairs before anyone could see him.

## [c4] Night Practice

### [p15]
Bruno couldn't sleep.

He was staying in the small guest room at the end of the corridor, next to the room where Orhan Bey kept his trains. Selin was in her old bedroom downstairs, and he didn't want to wake her up with a sad conversation at one o'clock in the morning.

He wished he had never bought the earpiece. He wished he had learned Turkish properly, the slow and boring way, instead of trusting a machine to do it for him. If he spoke Turkish, he thought, he wouldn't need anyone to translate his feelings. He could tell Orhan Bey himself.

But what would he even say?

He picked up his phone. Earlier that day, Emre had sent him a list called "Survival Turkish". Each phrase had been written out with its real meaning, and Emre had recorded himself saying each one slowly.

Eline sağlık: thank you for the food, to the person who cooked it.

Afiyet olsun: enjoy your meal.

Kolay gelsin: I hope your work is easy.

Ağzına sağlık: thank you for saying something kind.

Hoş bulduk: the answer to "welcome".

Five phrases. Bruno looked at them for a long time. Then he made a decision. By the end of this week, he would have learned them all. This time tomorrow, he would be sitting at that table and saying them correctly, without any machine in his ear.

### [p16]
He couldn't practise in the guest room, because the walls were thin. So he took his phone and went next door, into the train room, where the only light came from a small green lamp on the railway's control box.

He sat down on a low stool between the tables. The little trains stood on their lines, waiting, like a quiet audience.

"Good evening," he told them. "Thank you for coming."

He played Emre's first recording. "Eline sağlık," said Emre's voice, slowly.

"Eline," said Bruno. That part was easy. "Saluk."

It sounded wrong even to him. One letter had a small smile on top, and it seemed to make the sound before it longer and then disappear. Another letter had no dot, and its sound didn't exist in Portuguese or in English. It lived somewhere at the back of the mouth, and it refused to come out.

He tried it twenty times, with his eyes open and closed. The little red train didn't seem impressed.

"Sağlık," he said again. "Salik. Sauluk."

He put his head in his hands. "I've been practising for twenty minutes," he told the train, "and I'm getting worse."

The train said nothing. In this house, he thought, even the trains don't talk much.

### [p17]
After a while, he stopped practising and simply talked. It was easier to talk to small trains than to people.

"Your owner hates me," he said to the red train. "I don't blame him. If my daughter came home with a man who burned my house, I wouldn't like him either."

The train waited.

"The thing is," Bruno went on, in English and then, without noticing, in Portuguese, "I really love her. I'd like to tell him that. I'd like to tell him I'll look after her, and that I'm not as stupid as I seem. I'm actually quite good at my job. People pay me to solve problems."

"I can fix his trains. I just can't talk to him. This time tomorrow, I'll be sitting at his table again, and he'll be leaving the room again."

He thought about the photograph on the wall: the young man on the bridge above the valley. Orhan Bey had spent forty years connecting places that were very far apart. Bruno wished he could build something like that between himself and this quiet, serious man.

"Maybe I should just swallow the frogs," he said in Portuguese. It was what his grandmother used to say when she meant that you sometimes have to accept difficult things in silence. "Smile, eat the beans, go home."

He picked up the phone again. Swallowing frogs could wait until tomorrow. Tonight, he would learn this one word properly, even if it took him all night.

"Sağlık," he said. "Saluk."

### [p18]
"Sağlık."

The voice came from the corner of the room, deep and slow and perfectly pronounced.

Bruno almost fell off his stool.

In the darkest corner, in an old armchair that he hadn't noticed, a tall shape sat with its arms folded. It must have been sitting there the whole time. It had probably heard everything: the practice, the twenty terrible tries, the speech to the red train.

"Sağlık," said Orhan Bey again, a little more slowly. "Not saluk."

Bruno's mouth opened, but no words came out in any language.

How long had he been there? Bruno thought desperately about what he had said. He had told a toy train that its owner hated him. He had said he loved Selin. He had talked about swallowing frogs. Fortunately, most of the embarrassing parts had been in English and Portuguese, and Orhan Bey couldn't have understood those.

At least, that is what Bruno thought.

Orhan Bey reached out and turned on a small lamp next to his chair. In the warm yellow light, his face looked very tired and, for the first time, not quite so serious.

"Again," he said in Turkish, and pointed at Bruno. It was one of the six words Bruno knew.

"Sağlık," said Bruno carefully.

Orhan Bey listened, with his head on one side. Then he nodded once, the way he had nodded at the cable under the table.

## [c5] Blessing

### [p19]
For a while, neither of them said anything.

Then Orhan Bey lifted his hand to his right ear, pushed his grey hair back and took out something small and white. He placed it carefully on the edge of the railway table, next to the red train.

It was the second earpiece.

Bruno stared at it, and then at Orhan Bey, as if one of them might explain.

In Portuguese, when you finally understand something that should have been obvious for a long time, you say that the token has fallen, like a coin dropping into an old telephone. Bruno felt it fall now, loudly, all the way down.

The touched ear. The sudden walks to the kitchen, the garden and the bathroom. The red eyes after dinner. The laughter behind the kitchen door. Two earpieces connected.

"You," said Bruno weakly. "You've been listening. All week."

Orhan Bey picked up the earpiece and put it back in his ear. Bruno, with shaking hands, ran next door for his own earpiece, which had been charging by the bed all evening. It worked. Then Orhan Bey spoke in Turkish, and the calm voice in Bruno's ear turned it into English.

"Since the first evening," he said. "Selin gave it to me before dinner. She told me not to say anything. She said you should be allowed to be yourself."

### [p20]
"So you heard the bucket," said Bruno.

"I heard the bucket." Orhan Bey's mouth moved at one corner. "In Turkish it came out as: I have kicked the bucket. I thought you were dying at my table. Then I understood it was an expression, and I had to leave the room."

"Because you were angry?"

"Because I was laughing." He shook his head. "In my family, you do not laugh at a guest. Not in front of his face. So I went to the kitchen and laughed into a tea towel like a boy at school."

"And the duck?"

"The duck was in the garden." Orhan Bey's shoulders were moving now, just a little. "Pay the duck. I stood in the garden for five minutes. My neighbour asked me whether I was ill."

"And the sausage was in the bathroom."

"The sausage was in the bathroom. And tonight in the kitchen, I was telling Nermin about the dog and the cat. I am sorry if you heard us. We were not laughing at you. We were laughing because of you, which is very different."

All week, Bruno had been sure this man hated him. All week, the man had been hiding in kitchens to laugh at a duck.

"Why didn't you tell me?" he asked.

"At first, because Selin asked me not to," he said. "Then, I will be honest, because I was enjoying it very much."

### [p21]
Then his face turned serious again, but it was a different kind of serious now.

"I will tell you something," he said. "The glass that broke in the morning. I know it was not you. I saw Emre's face. He has had that face since he was four years old, every time he breaks something."

"Ah," said Bruno.

"A man who pays the duck for my son, on his second day in my house, without being asked." Orhan Bey looked at the red train. "If you had told me the truth about the glass, I would have liked you. Because you lied, I liked you more. Please don't tell Selin I said that. It is not a good lesson."

They talked until three o'clock in the morning, and Bruno no longer cared how strange the translations sounded. Orhan Bey explained how a bridge is designed to move a little in the wind. Bruno explained how a computer program is tested. They discovered that they had been doing the same job for years, in different materials.

Orhan Bey asked whether Bruno would be visiting again in the summer, and whether he would mind helping with a new line through the paper mountains.

"I'd love to," said Bruno. "By the summer, I'll have learned more Turkish."

"Learn slowly," said Orhan Bey. "Your mistakes are the best thing in this house since the cat."

### [p22]
On the last evening, Bruno asked Nermin Hanım a question, and Selin translated it: "Would you mind if I cooked dinner tonight?"

He made his grandmother's cheese bread, small and round and golden, the only thing he could cook without a video. Nermin Hanım had to have the oven checked by Emre first, just in case. However, the bread came out perfectly, and nothing caught fire.

Then Bruno made the tea. He made it with two pots, one on top of the other, and when he poured it, he left a little space at the top of each glass for the fingers. He put the first glass in front of Orhan Bey. It was a new glass, with a thin gold line around the top.

Nermin Hanım ate a piece of bread, closed her eyes and said, "Eline sağlık."

This time, Bruno knew exactly what to say. "Afiyet olsun," he answered, and the whole table, even the grandmother, who had come back especially, clapped.

Orhan Bey didn't clap. He was holding the new glass in both hands and looking at the gold line. Then he took the earpiece out of his ear, put it on the table and looked at Bruno across the bread and the tea.

When he spoke, it was in English. It was slow and careful, and the grammar was not quite right, and it was the best sentence Bruno had ever heard.

"Health to your hand, son."
