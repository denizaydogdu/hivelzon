## [c1] Reluctance

### [p01]
"Dear Mr Achterberg, I write, with the greatest possible reluctance, about your hedge."

Constance Webb had written that sentence eleven times before she was satisfied with it. The first version had been too warm, the second too cold, and the third had contained the word "monstrous", which she had crossed out with such force that the nib went through the paper. It was the eleventh, with its two commas standing guard on either side of the reluctance, that finally struck the right note. Reluctance, in her experience, was a word that did a great deal of quiet work. It informed the reader that one was a reasonable person, that one had been patient beyond all reasonable expectation, and that one's patience had now, regrettably, run out.

For twenty-six years she had been head teacher of Hartwell Road School. In that time she had written to parents about head lice, unpaid dinner money, a missing class hamster and, on one occasion still spoken of in the staff room, a father who had parked a tractor across the school gates. Never had she needed eleven drafts.

The hedge stood between her garden and the garden of Hollybank, the house next door, and it was a yew of great age and greater ambition. It was nearly four metres tall. Every afternoon from March to October, at a quarter past three, it swallowed the sun. Its shadow reached her roses at four and her conservatory at five, by which time Constance was usually sitting there with the crossword and a cardigan she ought not to have needed.

She read the letter through once more, signed it "Yours sincerely, Constance Webb (Miss)", and folded it into three exact parts. Then she put on her coat, walked to the post box at the end of Larkspur Lane and posted it, although Hollybank was eleven steps from her own front door. One did not, she felt, push a complaint through a neighbour's letterbox like a leaflet for double glazing. A complaint deserved a stamp.

Little did she know, as she heard it drop into the dark of the box, that she had just begun the longest correspondence of her life.

### [p02]
The trouble, if one wished to be precise about it, had begun in February, when Hollybank changed hands.

Mr Pike had lived at Hollybank for forty-one years, and in all that time he and Constance had exchanged perhaps a thousand words, most of them about the weather and a good many of them about the hedge. Every September, on the first dry Saturday, he had brought out his ladder and his shears and cut the yew from top to bottom, on both sides, with the solemn devotion of a man varnishing his own boat. He had kept it at a polite and neighbourly height. Constance had thanked him each year with a jar of her marmalade, and he had thanked her for the marmalade, and that had been the full extent of their relationship. It had suited them both perfectly.

Not once in those forty-one years had she touched the hedge herself. One did not prune other people's property; it was the sort of thing her mother would have called "taking liberties". If it had occurred to her to wonder why Mr Pike cut her side as carefully as his own, she had put it down to his good manners, and to the fact that a hedge cut on one side only looks like a man who has shaved half his face.

Then, some years ago, Mr Pike had begun to grow tired. His knees were not what they had been; the ladder stayed in the shed, and he cut only what he could reach from the ground. The yew, sensing its chance, had taken full advantage of a long period without supervision, and its top had climbed, year by year, towards the sky. Constance had said nothing. One did not complain to an old man about his knees. In January Mr Pike had gone to live with his daughter in Wexcombe, leaving behind a card that said he would miss the marmalade, and in February Hollybank had been sold to a Mr Desmond Achterberg.

It was said in the village shop that Mr Achterberg had been an inspector of taxes. He was believed to have spent thirty-eight years reading other people's accounts and finding them wanting. Constance had so far seen him only twice: once carrying a grey filing cabinet up his garden path, apparently without help, and once standing at his gate in a hat, looking at the sky as if he suspected it of underpaying.

### [p03]
The reply came four days later. It came, Constance noted with approval, by post, in a long white envelope addressed in a small, upright hand that leaned neither forward nor back but stood exactly where it was put.

"Dear Mrs Webb," it began.

She put the letter down, went into the kitchen, filled the kettle and stood by it until it boiled. Only then did she feel calm enough to continue.

"Dear Mrs Webb, Thank you for your letter of the fourteenth of March, the contents of which I have noted with interest. I regret that you have been inconvenienced by the hedge. I have, however, been advised that its present height is in keeping with its age and character, and I would be reluctant, to borrow your excellent word, to undertake any significant reduction of it without first giving the matter proper consideration.

"I would further observe that it is now the middle of March, and that a pair of blackbirds has begun building in the upper part of the hedge. It is generally accepted that the disturbance of nesting birds between March and August is to be avoided. You will, I am sure, agree.

"Should you wish to discuss the matter further, I would be glad to receive your views in writing. Yours sincerely, Desmond Achterberg."

Constance read it twice. The first time she read what it said, and the second time she read what it meant, which was a different letter altogether, and not a polite one.

"Mrs," she said aloud to the kettle. "Seventy-four years of being Miss Webb, and he makes me Mrs in a single line."

That was not, of course, the real complaint. The real complaint was the blackbirds. She could hardly argue with blackbirds. She was fond of blackbirds. She had, in fact, been feeding these particular blackbirds since the autumn, and it was entirely possible that they had chosen the hedge because of her. To be defeated by one's own birds was a humiliation of a very English kind, and she resented it deeply.

### [p04]
She replied the same evening.

"Dear Mr Achterberg, Thank you for your letter. I should perhaps begin by mentioning that I am not, and have never been, Mrs Webb. It is a small point, and I raise it only in the interest of accuracy, which I feel sure you value as much as I do.

"I am, of course, as fond of blackbirds as anyone. I would not dream of disturbing them. That said, the blackbirds will have left by August, whereas the hedge, I fear, will not. I would therefore be grateful if you would confirm that its reduction to a height of one metre and eighty centimetres will take place as soon as the nesting season is over. That is the height at which Mr Pike kept it for thirty years, and I see no reason why it should not suit you equally well. Yours sincerely, Constance Webb (Miss)."

She was pleased with "whereas". It was a word with its sleeves rolled up.

His answer arrived on Saturday. It was longer than the first, and it had been typed.

"Dear Miss Webb, I apologise unreservedly for the error in your title. It will not happen again. With regard to the hedge, I am not at present in a position to give the confirmation you request. Before any decision is made, it is essential that the question of ownership be settled. I have before me a copy of the original plan of the two properties, dated 1952, which was supplied to me at the time of my purchase. I would draw your attention to the line which marks the boundary between our gardens, and in particular to the small mark in the shape of a letter T which appears upon it. Unless I am much mistaken, the plan makes it plain that the hedge forms part of Hollybank, and that any decision about it is therefore mine. I enclose a copy for your interest."

The copy was grey, blurred and slightly crooked, as if it had been made on a machine that disliked its work. There was a line, certainly. There was something that might have been a T. There was also a small arrow in the corner, faint as a rumour, which presumably pointed north.

Constance held it up to the light and looked at it for a long time. Never in her life had she seen a document so determined to say nothing at all.

## [c2] With Respect

### [p05]
"With respect, Mr Achterberg," Constance wrote on Monday, "I have examined your plan with the greatest care, and I must confess that it tells me very little. It would appear to show a line. I do not doubt that it is a line of considerable importance. Even so, I can see nothing in it which proves that a hedge planted some seventy years ago belongs to one garden rather than the other, and I would gently suggest that a mark resembling the letter T is not, in itself, an argument."

She paused, pen in hand. The phrase "with respect" was, she knew, one of the most dangerous in the English language. Nobody had ever begun a sentence with it intending to show any. It was what judges said before they disagreed with a barrister, and what head teachers said before they disagreed with an inspector, and she had used it in both meanings for most of her working life.

"I would further point out that the hedge stands, for most of its length, rather closer to my house than to yours. I have lived at Fernleigh for forty-one years, and I have always understood it to be Hollybank's hedge. Mr Pike certainly thought so; why else would he have cut it every year? I do not, however, ask you to accept my memory. I ask only that you accept the evidence of your own eyes, and of the sun, which disappears behind the hedge at a quarter past three."

The last sentence pleased her so much that she read it out to the empty kitchen.

"I trust you will forgive the length of this letter. Yours sincerely, Constance Webb."

There was, she reflected, a whole grammar of politeness, and very few people were fluent in it. "I trust you will" meant "you will". "I wonder whether you might" meant "you must". "I am sure you did not intend" meant "you did". And "I trust you will forgive" meant that one had no intention of asking for forgiveness at all, and would be quite astonished to be refused it.

Mr Achterberg, it turned out, was fluent too.

### [p06]
"Dear Miss Webb," his next letter said, "I am most grateful for your careful observations, and I note with interest your remark about the sun. With the greatest respect, I would venture to suggest that the movements of the sun lie outside the scope of the present discussion.

"Your point about Mr Pike is well made, and I have given it due weight. It is, however, my understanding that Mr Pike's annual cutting of the hedge is entirely consistent with its belonging to Hollybank, and that, were it not for his kindness in cutting both sides, you might well have noticed this sooner. It is a principle in matters of property, as in matters of tax, that one is responsible for what one owns. By the same token, I would never presume to cut a hedge that was not my own. I would be most surprised if you did not agree.

"In the meantime, I have taken the liberty of trimming the side of the hedge which faces my garden. You may have observed this on Saturday morning. Failure to maintain a boundary hedge in good order is, after all, the kind of thing that leads to disputes between neighbours. Yours sincerely, Desmond Achterberg."

She had observed it. She had observed it from behind the curtain in her spare bedroom, for the full forty minutes it had taken. He had worked with small, careful shears, standing on a low step, and he had cut his side of the yew into a flat green wall so neat and so straight that it looked as if it had been ironed. He had not, she noticed, touched the top. The top remained where it was, four metres up, wearing the blackbirds like a hat.

It was the phrase "in good order" that kept her awake that night. It suggested, she felt, that the hedge had not been in good order while Mr Pike was alive, and he was perfectly alive, he was simply in Wexcombe. It suggested that she herself was the kind of neighbour who led to disputes. And it was signed with a respect so complete that it left no room for her to object to it.

The following morning, before breakfast, she went down to the bottom of the garden in her dressing gown and looked at the base of the hedge for the first time in years.

### [p07]
It was not a pleasant place. The ground beneath an old yew is dry and dark and covered in a carpet of brown needles, and nothing grows there that has any sense. The trunks rose out of it like a row of grey pillars, twisted and close together, and between them she could just make out the bright, newly cut green of Mr Achterberg's side.

There was something else. Along the far side of the trunks, half buried in the needles, ran a line of short iron pegs, red with rust, each about a metre from the next. She counted nine before the hedge became too thick to see. They stood, she noted, on the Hollybank side of the yew, so close to his lawn that his mower must have been hitting them for months.

Constance considered the pegs for some time. Having considered them, she decided that they proved nothing whatever, except that whoever had put them there in 1952 had been as careless with his measurements as Mr Achterberg was with his titles. Pegs, after all, could be moved. People moved things all the time; she had once found the school's fire bucket in the caretaker's greenhouse. She went back indoors, made toast, and put the pegs out of her mind with the firmness of long practice.

What she needed, she decided over the marmalade, was not an opinion but a fact. Opinions could be argued with; that was their charm. Facts had to be accepted, which was why people who had them were so tiresome.

Her letter that afternoon was short.

"Dear Mr Achterberg, Thank you for trimming your side of the hedge. It looks very tidy. I am sure the blackbirds are grateful for the extra light, which is more than I am able to say. Since neither of us appears willing to accept the other's reading of your 1952 plan, I propose that the matter be settled by somebody who is able to read it properly. Yours sincerely, Constance Webb."

She did not say what she proposed to do. Granted, it was a little underhand. Even so, she reasoned, a head teacher who announces her inspections in advance deserves the school she finds.

### [p08]
The surveyor's office was in Kingsmere, above a shop that sold wool, and it was run by a father and daughter called Quayle. The father, Mr Hollis Quayle, was nearly eighty and spoke very slowly, as though each word cost money. The daughter was about forty, did everything else, and spoke very fast, as though making up the difference.

"Boundary dispute?" said the daughter, before Constance had finished sitting down. "Hedge?"

"How did you know?"

"It's always a hedge. Occasionally a fence. Once a shed, but that was a special case." She opened a large diary. "We can come out and measure in a few weeks, but the full report will take longer. We'll need to find the original plans at the record office, and the record office is not, shall we say, in a hurry. September, probably."

"September," said Constance.

"If you had come in January, I could have done it by the spring," said Ms Quayle cheerfully. "Everybody always wants it yesterday."

Mr Quayle, who had been looking out of the window, cleared his throat. "Yew," he said, as if this explained a great deal.

"I'm sorry?"

"Yew hedges. They're always older than you think. And they're never," he paused for so long that Constance wondered whether he had finished, "quite where people think they are."

Constance paid a deposit she preferred not to think about and took the bus home through the April rain, feeling, for the first time in a month, entirely in control of events. She had a professional on her side. She had a date. She had, in a manner of speaking, called in the inspectors.

It did not occur to her until much later that she had not asked what would happen if the inspectors found against her. At the time it seemed hardly worth asking. Mr Achterberg, she was certain, was about to discover that a T on a crooked photocopy was no match for a woman with a surveyor.

## [c3] The Street Takes Sides

### [p09]
Larkspur Lane had eleven houses, one post box, no shop and, until that spring, nothing whatever to talk about. The village of Nether Ambley lay at the bottom of the lane, and it had a shop, a pub, a bowls club and a book group, all of which had been talking about nothing for years and were ready for a change.

It was Barry Tuttle, the postman, who started it, although he always denied it afterwards. Barry had delivered the post to Larkspur Lane for nineteen years, and he had a professional interest in envelopes. He knew handwriting the way other men knew football teams. So when Miss Webb at Fernleigh began receiving a long white envelope every two days in a small upright hand, and Mr Achterberg at Hollybank began receiving a square cream one in a tall, sloping hand, and both envelopes carried a postmark from the box at the end of their own lane, Barry drew his own conclusions. Being a generous man, he shared them.

"They're writing to each other," he told Priya Dhillon in the village shop. "Next door. With stamps."

"About what?"

"Can't say." Barry tapped the side of his nose. "Professional confidence. Hedge, though."

By the end of the week the whole village knew. By the end of the month it had taken sides.

The book group, which had been reading a long novel about a whale and was glad of the interruption, came down firmly on the side of Constance. Mrs Fairweather, who chaired it, had been taught by her forty years earlier and still could not enter a room without checking that her shoes were clean. A woman like Constance Webb, she said, did not complain without cause. The bowls club, on the other hand, declared for Mr Achterberg, partly because he had joined it in April and bowled with frightening accuracy, and partly because Mr Ogunleye, its captain, lived opposite Hollybank and had a hedge of his own that he did not intend to cut for anybody.

It was Priya, however, who turned a disagreement into an industry. She cleared a space on the board by the shop door, between a lost cat and a man offering to sharpen knives, and wrote across the top in green chalk: "The Hedge. Fifty pence a guess. All money to the village hall roof."

### [p10]
There were three columns. The first said "Down by the first frost". The second said "Still standing at New Year". The third, which Priya had added after some thought, said "Wedding". Nobody would admit to having put money in the third column, but by the end of May it held four pounds fifty.

Constance learnt of the board from Mrs Fairweather, who told her about it in the tone of someone reporting a small fire.

"I trust," said Constance, "that you did not contribute."

"Oh, no," said Mrs Fairweather, who had put a pound on the frost.

It was around this time that the letters began to arrive by hand. Neither of them would have dreamt of walking eleven steps and pushing an envelope through the other's door; that would have been a visit, and a visit would have required a conversation. But the post box at the end of the lane was emptied only once a day, and a reply which arrived the following morning seemed to both of them intolerably slow. It was Barry who found the solution. Since he passed both doors anyway, he said, it would be no trouble at all to carry a letter from one to the other, and if he happened to pass again after lunch, and once more before tea, well, a man had to walk somewhere.

So it was that Barry Tuttle began coming down Larkspur Lane three times a day. He took the letters with great ceremony, held them as though they might go off, and delivered them with a small bow. He was, Constance suspected, enjoying himself enormously. So, she suspected, was Priya, whose takings had never been better. Arguably, so was the whole village. Only the two people at the centre of it all were having, as anybody could see, a perfectly miserable time.

At least, that was what Constance would have said if anyone had asked her. Nobody did. It might well have been because nobody believed she would answer truthfully.

The letters, meanwhile, grew longer. They also grew more polite, which was a very bad sign. In England, as every head teacher knows, people are rude only when they are relaxed. When they are truly angry, they become courteous to the point of cruelty.

### [p11]
"Dear Miss Webb," Mr Achterberg wrote in the middle of May, "I was sorry to learn from a third party that you have engaged the services of a surveyor. I had rather hoped that the matter could be resolved between ourselves. I am, of course, happy to cooperate with any inspection of the boundary, and I would only ask that I be informed in advance of the date, so that I may ensure that the cat is indoors. She takes a keen interest in strangers, and an even keener one in their measuring tapes."

There was a cat. Constance had not known there was a cat. She felt, obscurely, that this was one more thing he had kept from her.

"Dear Mr Achterberg," she replied, "I am so sorry that you had to learn of my arrangements from a third party. I was under the impression that you had told the bowls club, the shop and most of the lane about your own arrangements with the hedge, and I assumed that this was the custom. The surveyors will come on Thursday morning. I hope your cat enjoys them."

They came on Thursday. Ms Quayle measured everything in sight, including, at one point, Constance's shed, while her father sat on a folding chair at the bottom of the garden and looked at the hedge in silence, like a man listening to music. After an hour he got up, walked slowly along the yew, bent down with some difficulty, and pushed the brown needles aside with his stick until he found one of the rusty iron pegs. He looked at it for a long time.

"Interesting," he said.

"What is?" said Constance.

"Can't say, I'm afraid. We don't give opinions before the report." He straightened up and looked at her with what might, in a younger man, have been described as a twinkle. "But I shouldn't throw away your shears, Miss Webb."

"I don't have any shears."

"Ah," said Mr Quayle. "Well. You may want to buy some." And he would not say another word.

Across the hedge, a curtain at Hollybank moved, and then was still.

### [p12]
June was hot, and the correspondence reached its height. Constance's letters now ran to three pages; his ran to four, with headings. They were letters of a kind that could only be written by two people who had spent their whole lives being correct, and who had finally found, in each other, an opponent worth being correct at.

She wrote that she was "sure he had not intended" the hedge to block the light, which meant that he had. He wrote that he "entirely understood her frustration", which meant that he understood nothing and did not wish to. She wrote that she "would not dream of" taking the matter further, which meant that she had already dreamt of it in some detail. He wrote that "with every respect" the absence of a reply to his fourth point "might be thought significant", which meant that he had noticed she had no answer to his fourth point, and had enjoyed noticing it very much.

Such was the heat of the exchange that Barry began to carry a bottle of water.

Then, on the last Saturday in June, a cream envelope arrived that was thicker than usual. Constance opened it at the kitchen table with a knife, as she always did, and something light slid out of the folded pages and fell onto the cloth.

It was a rose. It had been pressed flat and dried with great care between sheets of paper, and it was the deep, dusty pink of the climbing rose she had planted at the foot of the hedge more than thirty years ago. Over the years it had climbed up into the dark of the yew and pushed its way through to the other side, where, she supposed, it flowered for Mr Pike and now for Mr Achterberg, and hardly at all for her.

The letter said only: "Dear Miss Webb, I return herewith a rose of yours which, I regret to inform you, had trespassed. Yours sincerely, Desmond Achterberg."

Constance looked at the rose for a long time. It was, she decided, either the rudest thing anyone had ever sent her, or something else entirely, and she did not know which of the two possibilities she found more alarming.

## [c4] Beatrice

### [p13]
Her sister arrived at the end of August with two suitcases, a cake in a tin and the air of someone who had come to stay for a week and might, if things went well, stay for a fortnight.

Beatrice was four years younger than Constance and had always been, in Constance's view, the less serious of the two. She had married a cheerful man who sold pianos, raised three cheerful children in a house full of noise, and spent most of her life laughing at things Constance had not yet noticed were funny. They loved each other with the deep, patient affection of sisters who have long since stopped expecting the other to change.

Beatrice had not been in the house for an hour before she found the letters.

"Con," she said, "what is this?"

"That is private correspondence."

"It's in a box file. With dividers." Beatrice turned the box over in her hands. "You've labelled the dividers. April, May, June. Con, you've cross-referenced them."

"One likes to be able to find things."

Beatrice sat down at the kitchen table, opened the file at April, and began to read. Constance busied herself with the kettle, and then with the teapot, and then with a cupboard that did not need tidying. From behind her came a series of small sounds: a sniff, a snort, and then a long, helpless wheeze that grew into the kind of laughter Beatrice had last produced, Constance remembered, at their aunt's seventieth birthday party, when the cake had collapsed.

"I'm glad," said Constance, "that you find my difficulties amusing."

"Oh, Con." Beatrice wiped her eyes. "'I trust you will forgive the length of this letter.' Nobody has ever said anything less forgiving in the history of the world." She turned a page. "And he's every bit as bad. Listen. 'I would not wish to presume upon your patience, of which I have, I fear, already received more than my share.' He's lovely. Is he lovely?"

"I have no idea. I have never spoken to him."

Beatrice put down the letter. "You've never spoken to him?"

"There has been no need. We correspond."

"You've been writing to each other every day since March," said Beatrice slowly, "and you've never once said good morning over the fence?"

"It is not a fence," said Constance. "That is rather the point."

### [p14]
It took Beatrice two days to notice the rest.

She noticed that Constance, who had always despised people who watched their neighbours, now sat in the front room at a quarter past nine, at one o'clock and at half past four, which happened to be the three times that Barry Tuttle came down the lane. She noticed that on the one afternoon Barry was late, Constance read the same page of the newspaper four times. She noticed that the pressed rose had not been thrown away but placed, with great care, between two pages of the dictionary on the kitchen shelf, under R.

"You're enjoying this," Beatrice said on the third evening.

"I am doing nothing of the sort."

"You haven't had this much fun since the school inspectors came in 1994."

Constance opened her mouth to object, and then, to her own surprise, closed it again. The inspection of 1994 had been, by common agreement, the finest week of her professional life. She had beaten the inspectors on every point, and one of them had gone home early with a headache.

"That is quite a different matter," she said at last.

"Is it?" Beatrice poured herself more tea. "Do you know, I've just remembered something. When you bought this house, the man from the estate agent's showed us round the garden. Do you remember? He was very young and had a terrible tie. And he stood right there by the hedge and said, 'Of course, the hedge goes with the house.'"

"He meant that it came with the view."

"Did he? I always thought he meant…"

"Bea, he was twenty-two and his tie had ducks on it. He would have said the moon went with the house if he thought it would help." Constance stood up and began collecting the cups. "Besides, the matter is now in professional hands. The surveyor's report is due in September, and when it comes, I would rather we didn't discuss it in that tone."

"Which tone?"

"The tone you're about to use."

Beatrice smiled into her tea and, for once, said nothing at all. It was, Constance reflected afterwards, the most irritating thing she had done all week.

### [p15]
On Saturday Beatrice went to the village shop for milk and came back an hour later with a loaf of bread, a great deal of information and an expression of enormous satisfaction.

"He's sixty-six," she announced, putting the bread down. "He was a tax inspector for thirty-eight years. He never married. He used to live in a flat in the middle of Kingsmere, and when he retired he wanted a garden, so he sold up and came here. He doesn't know a single soul in the village except the bowls club, and Priya says he comes in every morning for a newspaper and talks to her for twenty minutes about nothing, because she thinks he hasn't anyone else to talk to."

"Priya," said Constance, "should be careful what she repeats."

"Con." Beatrice sat down opposite her. "He's lonely. He moved to a place where he knew nobody, and the first thing that happened was that a very clever woman next door started writing him letters. Every day. With jokes in them." She paused. "They are jokes, aren't they? Some of them?"

"Certainly not."

"The one about the sun is a joke."

Constance said nothing. The one about the sun had, admittedly, been a joke, and she had been rather proud of it.

"All I'm saying," said Beatrice gently, "is that it's high time one of you knocked on the other's door. It needn't be a big thing. You could take him a jar of marmalade."

"I would rather you didn't tell me what to do with my marmalade."

"I know you would. You've said that since we were nine." Beatrice reached over and patted her hand. "I'm only saying it because I'm your sister, and I'm the only person in the world who's allowed to."

That evening a cream envelope came with Barry's last delivery. It was shorter than usual. "Dear Miss Webb," it said. "The blackbirds have now left. The nesting season being concluded, I shall give the question of the hedge my full attention. I hope that your sister is enjoying her stay. Yours sincerely, Desmond Achterberg."

"How does he know about me?" said Beatrice, delighted.

"The whole village knows about you," said Constance. "You told them."

### [p16]
Beatrice left on Sunday morning. Her husband came to collect her in a car that smelt of piano polish, and the two sisters stood at the gate for some time, saying the things sisters say when they have already said all the important ones.

"Knock on his door," Beatrice said, getting into the car.

"I shall do no such thing."

"Then at least read his next letter properly. Read what it says, not what you think it means." She wound down the window. "And Con? Be nice. You're a much nicer person than you write."

The car drove away up the lane, and Constance went inside to a house that was suddenly very quiet. She was surprised to find that she minded. She spent the afternoon writing a reply to his last letter, a short and, she thought, almost friendly one, thanking him for his attention and wishing him well with the task. She left it on the hall table for Barry in the morning.

On Monday morning Barry took her letter, and he brought nothing back.

That was not in itself unusual. Mr Achterberg sometimes took a day to reply, especially when a letter required what he called "proper consideration". Constance spent the day weeding the front path, which needed it and which happened to give an excellent view of the lane, and she did not look at the door of Hollybank more than six or seven times.

On Tuesday it rained, and there was nothing at a quarter past nine, nothing at one o'clock, and nothing at half past four.

On Wednesday Barry came down the lane at his usual times, carrying a gas bill, a catalogue for garden furniture and a postcard from Beatrice which said "Have you knocked yet?" There was no cream envelope. There was, as far as Constance could tell, no sound at all from Hollybank, and in all the months since February there had never been three whole days without a letter.

## [c5] Silence

### [p17]
He was sulking. That was the first explanation, and for most of Wednesday evening Constance found it a satisfying one. She had, after all, been almost friendly in her last letter, and there were men who could not bear friendliness from an opponent. It unsettled them. It made them suspect a trap. Mr Achterberg, she decided, was sitting at his desk at this very moment, turning her harmless little sentences over and over in search of the insult he was certain must be hidden in them.

She began a letter on the subject. "Dear Mr Achterberg, I was wondering whether my last letter had perhaps failed to reach you, since I have not had the pleasure of a reply." She read it back and tore it up. It sounded anxious. Worse, it sounded as if she had been counting the days, which she had, but there was no need for him to know it.

The second explanation arrived at about eleven o'clock that night, as she lay awake listening to the rain, and it was much less satisfying. He had given up. He had read her letters from March to August and simply decided, as a reasonable man might, that life was too short to spend it arguing with a retired head teacher about a tree. He would cut the hedge, or he would not cut it, but he would not write to her again. The correspondence was over. She had, presumably, won.

Constance lay in the dark and examined this victory from every side, and found to her considerable annoyance that she did not like the look of it at all.

By Thursday morning a third explanation had begun to form, and she did not care to look at that one directly. She noticed things instead. She noticed that his bins, which he put out every Tuesday evening at seven o'clock exactly, as if he were returning a library book, had not been put out. She noticed that the curtain in his front room, which moved whenever anyone came down the lane, had not moved since Sunday.

It is high time, she told herself firmly, that you stopped behaving like a woman in a novel. Then she went and stood at the window to wait for Barry.

### [p18]
Barry was early. He came down the lane at ten past nine with his bag on his shoulder and his face arranged in the expression of a man who has news and is trying to look as if he hasn't.

"You'll have heard," he said, as soon as she opened the door.

"I have heard nothing. That is precisely the difficulty."

"About Mr Achterberg." Barry lowered his voice, although there was nobody in the lane but a pigeon. "He came off his ladder. Sunday evening. Doing the top of the hedge, he was, at the far end, and the ladder went over in the soft ground. Broke his wrist. The right one."

Constance put her hand on the door frame. "Sunday?"

"Sunday evening, about six. And he didn't tell a soul, did he? Got himself up, wrapped his arm in a tea towel, phoned for a taxi with his left hand and took himself off to the hospital in Kingsmere. Didn't want to make a fuss, apparently." Barry shook his head with a mixture of disapproval and deep respect. "They kept him in. Had to put pins in it. He's to be there another week, they reckon."

"But that was four days ago," said Constance. "Why did nobody…"

"Nobody knew. He only rang Priya last night, because of the cat." Barry reached into his bag and brought out a single key on a piece of green string. "It turns out he'd left one of those machines that feeds it by itself, enough for three days. Very organised man, Mr Achterberg. Only now it's run out, and he can't think of anybody to ask." Barry held out the key. "Priya's got the shop to run. And he did say, she says, that the lady next door might be willing, but that she wasn't to be troubled if she'd rather not."

Constance looked at the key for some time.

"If she'd rather not," she repeated.

"His words. Or Priya's, anyway." Barry was watching her face with professional interest. "Shall I tell her you'll do it?"

"You may tell her," said Constance, taking the key, "that I am perfectly capable of feeding a cat."

She closed the door, stood in the hall with the key in her hand, and found that she was trembling very slightly, which she put down to the cold.

### [p19]
She did not go to Hollybank at once. She went first, without quite knowing why, to the bottom of her own garden.

The rain had stopped in the night and the morning was bright and washed. Her lawn ran down past the roses and the old apple tree to the end of the garden, where the yew stood as it had always stood, dark and enormous. From the kitchen window, the apple tree hid the last few metres of it. She had not been down to the bottom since the weekend. On Monday she had been at the front, and on Tuesday and Wednesday it had rained.

She walked round the apple tree and stopped.

For a stretch of about three metres at the very end, the hedge was no longer four metres tall. It had been cut down, cleanly and with great care, to the height of a tall man, or very nearly. On her side of it stood a garden cane, pushed into the ground, with a piece of string tied round it at exactly the right point: one metre and eighty centimetres. She did not need a tape measure to know it. She had written the number in eleven letters. Beyond the cut stretch, the hedge rose again to its full height as though nothing had happened, and the line between the two was so sudden that it looked like a step.

Through the new gap she could see into the garden of Hollybank for the first time in forty years. There was a neat lawn, a vegetable bed with runner beans climbing their poles, a bench, and, lying on the grass beside the hedge, a long aluminium ladder. Next to it, where somebody had dropped them and nobody had come back for them, lay a pair of shears, already turning brown at the edges with rust.

He had done it. He had waited until the blackbirds left, as he said he would, and then he had given the question of the hedge his full attention, as he said he would, and he had climbed a ladder on soft ground at the age of sixty-six to do the very thing she had asked of him, and he had fallen.

Constance stood very still. A blackbird, presumably one of the evicted family, landed on the cane, looked at her, and flew away.

### [p20]
If she had not written that first letter, he would not now be lying in a hospital in Kingsmere with pins in his wrist. It was a simple sentence, and she was a woman who believed in simple sentences, and there was no way around it that she could find.

She went next door and fed the cat. The cat was a large grey animal with an expression of permanent suspicion, and she ate her breakfast with the air of someone who had been badly treated and intended to write about it. Constance found her food in a cupboard labelled, in that small upright hand, "Audit: Food". It was only then that she learnt the cat's name. It seemed to her a perfectly dreadful name for a cat, and she found herself, to her astonishment, smiling at it.

She filled the water bowl, washed the dish and came home.

At a quarter past three that afternoon, she was sitting in the conservatory with the crossword when something happened that had not happened in that room, at that hour, for the whole of the summer. The sun came in. It came through the gap at the end of the hedge, low and golden, and it lay across the lawn and climbed the stems of her roses and reached, after a while, the edge of the table where her hands were resting.

She had wanted this for six months. She had written letters of great length and considerable cruelty to get it. Now that it was here, it felt a good deal less like sunshine than like evidence.

At four o'clock she put on her coat and walked down to the village shop. Priya, who had clearly been expecting her, said nothing at all about the hedge, which was the most tactful thing anyone had done all week. Constance bought a loaf of bread, a tin of cat food in case of emergencies, and, from the hook at the back where the gardening things were kept, a pair of shears.

She carried them home and down the garden in the last of the light, and stood in front of the hedge for a long moment, feeling their weight. In forty-one years she had never touched it. Then she lifted the shears, opened them, and took the first cut of her life.

## [c6] The Survey

### [p21]
She did not, it should be said, attempt the top. Having seen what a ladder could do to a man of sixty-six, she was in no hurry to discover what it could do to a woman of seventy-four, and she had no wish to be the second person on Larkspur Lane to fall off one for the sake of this hedge. Instead she worked from the ground. She cut back the long untidy shoots on her own side, as far up as she could comfortably reach, and gathered the cuttings into a wheelbarrow, and took them to the heap behind the shed. It took her the whole of Friday morning, and her arms ached in places she had forgotten she possessed.

Then she climbed carefully through the gap, which was now just low enough for a determined person in sensible shoes, and tidied his side as well. She picked up the ladder and laid it neatly along the fence. She cleaned his shears, oiled them, and hung them on the nail inside his shed where they obviously lived, because there was a painted outline of a pair of shears on the wall behind it.

It was absurd, of course. She was aware of that. Only a month earlier, she would have regarded the act of cleaning Mr Achterberg's shears as a betrayal of her entire position. Now it seemed the least she could do, and it was by some distance the most she had done for anyone in years.

She fed Audit twice a day. She needn't have bought the tin of cat food; the cupboard was full of them, in neat rows, labelled by date. On the second evening the cat allowed herself to be stroked, briefly and as a favour. On the third evening she followed Constance to the door and sat on the step and watched her go, which Constance chose to interpret as a sign of respect.

She did not look at his papers. She was not that kind of woman. It was impossible, however, not to see the kitchen table, because the cat's dish stood beside it, and on the table there was a large ring binder, lying open. In it, each in its own clear plastic sleeve, were her letters. All of them. In order.

And on top of the binder, as if he had been working on it when he went out to the ladder, lay a single sheet of paper. It had been started again and again: "Dear Miss Webb" was written at the top and crossed out, written and crossed out, nine times down the page, in that small upright hand. Not one of the attempts had got any further than her name.

### [p22]
On Saturday she telephoned the hospital. It took four transfers and a long piece of recorded music before she reached a nurse on the right ward, who sounded young, kind and very tired.

"Mr Achterberg? Oh, he's fine. He's doing very well." There was a pause. "He's being a bit difficult about the food, to be honest. He sent back the shepherd's pie with a note."

"A note," said Constance.

"Written with his left hand. It took him twenty minutes. It said, 'This is not a pie.' Are you family?"

"I'm his neighbour," said Constance, and found that the word felt different in her mouth than it would have done a week ago.

"Oh! Are you the lady with the letters?" The nurse's voice brightened considerably. "He's told us all about you. He says you're the cleverest woman he's ever had the misfortune to disagree with. I've been meaning to ask him what the disagreement was about, but he always changes the subject."

Constance was silent for a moment. "Would you be kind enough," she said at last, "to tell him that the cat is well, and that the tomatoes are being watered, and that he is on no account to worry about the hedge?"

"I'll tell him. Anything else?"

There was a great deal else. There was, she thought, a whole letter's worth of things, and a letter written in her best and most careful style would have taken her an evening and eleven drafts. But a letter was not what was needed, and in any case the man could not hold a pen.

"No," she said. "That will do."

On Monday, with Audit fed and the tomatoes watered and the hedge as tidy as a woman of seventy-four could make it from the ground, Constance took the bus to Kingsmere. Ms Quayle had mentioned, back in April, that the original plans of the two houses were kept at the county record office, and it had occurred to Constance, somewhere between the shepherd's pie and the tomatoes, that she had spent six months arguing about a document she had never actually seen. It was not a mistake she would have forgiven in a pupil.

### [p23]
The record office was in the basement of a large brick building near the river, and it was staffed, on that particular Monday, by a single young man called Josh, who wore a cardigan that was older than he was and approached every question as if it were a delightful surprise.

"1952?" he said. "Nether Ambley? Larkspur Lane?" He vanished between the shelves, and came back twenty minutes later carrying a long cardboard tube with the delicacy of a man carrying a baby. "Here we are. Building plots, two of them. It would appear that they were sold off by the farm that used to own the land."

He unrolled the plan on a wide table and weighed down the corners with four small bags of sand that he kept, it seemed, for exactly this purpose.

The original was much clearer than Mr Achterberg's copy. The lines were sharp and black, drawn by somebody with a steady hand and a good pen. There were the two rectangles of the gardens, side by side; there was the lane, running along the bottom; there was the boundary between them, a firm straight line; and there, on that line, was the small T.

The difficulty was that neither of the plots had a name. In 1952, neither house had yet been built, and the plan called them simply Plot A and Plot B, as if they were characters in a problem in mathematics. The north arrow, which on the copy had been faint, was on the original entirely missing, because a corner of the paper had at some point been torn away.

"Which one is mine?" said Constance.

Josh looked at the plan for a long time. Then he turned it round and looked at it from the other side. Then he turned it round again.

"Honestly? I'm not sure," he admitted. "Arguably, it depends which way the lane goes. And the T…" He frowned at it. "I think there's some sort of rule about which side the T goes on, but I couldn't swear to it, and my supervisor's on holiday. Sorry."

Constance stood at the wide table and looked at the T, and the T, as far as she could tell, looked back at her and said nothing. She asked for a copy, paid for it, and took it home on the bus. All the way back she held it on her knee, turning it round and round, so that by the time she reached Nether Ambley she had proved to her own satisfaction, at different points on the journey, that the hedge belonged to Hollybank, to Fernleigh, and on one occasion to the farm.

### [p24]
The days that followed were, by the standards of Larkspur Lane, remarkably quiet. No letters were written. Barry still came down the lane three times a day, partly out of habit and partly, Constance suspected, out of hope, but he carried nothing more interesting than bills, and after a while he stopped bowing.

The village, deprived of its entertainment, began to change sides. Mrs Fairweather, who had been so firmly for Constance, came round with a casserole for Mr Achterberg's freezer and stayed to ask, with great delicacy, whether Constance did not feel just a little bit responsible. Mr Ogunleye, the captain of the bowls club, who had been so firmly for Mr Achterberg, stopped Constance in the lane to tell her that he had seen her cutting the hedge from his upstairs window and thought it very decent of her, and that if she needed a man with a ladder, he knew two. At the shop, Priya rubbed out the three columns on the board and wrote, in a fourth, "Get well soon, Desmond", and underneath it, in pencil, at least thirty people from the village had signed their names. Constance read them all. She did not sign. She had something else in mind, and she had not yet worked out what it was.

She watered his tomatoes and picked his runner beans before they grew tough. She left the beans in a bowl on his kitchen table, beside the binder. She wrote a short note to go with them, and then took it away again, because it said either too much or too little.

Every afternoon at a quarter past three the sun came through the gap in the hedge and lay across her lawn. She found that she had begun to watch for it, the way she had once watched for Barry.

On Saturday, at a quarter past nine, Barry came down the lane with a large brown envelope under his arm and a look on his face that she had not seen since the rose.

"It's from Kingsmere," he said. "Quayle and Daughter." He paused on the doorstep, holding it just slightly out of her reach. "It's the report, isn't it?"

## [c7] Tea?

### [p25]
Constance did not open it at once. She took it into the kitchen, put it on the table, made a pot of tea, poured a cup, and drank half of it. Only when she could think of no further reason to wait did she take a knife and slit the envelope along the top, as she always did.

The report ran to eleven pages, which she felt was showing off. It was written in the language that surveyors share with tax inspectors and head teachers: a language in which nothing is ever done by anybody, but things are found, noted and considered to be the case. She read it as she read everything, from the beginning, without skipping.

"Following the inspection of the boundary on the twenty-second of May, and the subsequent examination of the original plan held at the county record office, the following findings are presented for the client's consideration.

"It is the long-established convention in plans of this kind that a mark in the form of a letter T, placed against a boundary line, indicates the party responsible for that boundary, the T being drawn on the side of the land of that party. On the original plan, the T has been placed on the side of Plot A. The orientation of the plan has been confirmed by reference to the lane and to the farm buildings shown on the original. Plot A is the property now known as Fernleigh.

"It would appear that the copy supplied with the sale particulars of Hollybank was produced in reverse, a fault not uncommon in copies of this age, and that the T therefore appears on that copy to stand on the Hollybank side.

"The iron pegs found at the site mark the original line of the boundary. The trunks of the yew hedge stand, along the whole of its length, at a distance of approximately forty centimetres from that line, on the Fernleigh side.

"It is accordingly our conclusion that the hedge is situated wholly within the boundary of Fernleigh, and that it is the property, and the responsibility, of the owner of that house."

At the bottom, under the signature of Ms Quayle, someone had added a single line in old-fashioned handwriting, in pencil: "Told you to keep the shears. Hollis Quayle."

### [p26]
Constance put the report down and sat for a while without moving.

It was her hedge. It had always been her hedge. It had been her hedge in 1983, when the young man with the ducks on his tie had stood beside it and told her, quite correctly, that it went with the house. It had been her hedge in every one of the Septembers in which Mr Pike had climbed his ladder and cut both sides of it, without a word, for nothing but a jar of marmalade, and she had thanked him for the marmalade as though it were a fair exchange, and she had never once asked herself why a man would work so hard on somebody else's property. She knew now. He had simply been kind. He had been kind for forty-one years, and she had not noticed, because it had never occurred to her that the kindness might be needed.

It had been her hedge in March, when she had written to the new neighbour with the greatest possible reluctance. It had been her hedge all through the summer, while she complained to him about its height, its shade and its general attitude, and while he, believing it to be his, trimmed it every Saturday into a smooth green wall so as not to be the kind of neighbour who leads to disputes.

And it had been her hedge on Sunday evening, when he climbed a ladder in the soft ground to cut it down to the height she had demanded, and fell.

So completely had she been wrong that there was almost something restful about it. She had not been partly wrong, or wrong on a technical point, or wrong in a way that could be explained to a governing body with the help of a chart. She had been wrong from the first sentence of the first letter. If she had asked a single sensible question in March, she would not now be sitting in her own kitchen as the owner of a hedge, a surveyor's bill and a very great deal of shame. She had written eleven drafts of that letter, and every one of them had been addressed to the wrong person.

Outside, Audit appeared on top of the low stretch of hedge, walked along it with great dignity, and dropped down onto Constance's side for the first time, as if she had read the report too.

Not until the cup in front of her had gone completely cold did Constance move. Then she went to the desk in the front room, took out a sheet of her best paper and her good pen, and began to write the most formal letter of her life.

### [p27]
"Dear Mr Achterberg, It is with the greatest possible embarrassment that I write to you about my hedge.

"I enclose a copy of the surveyor's report. You will see from it that the hedge which has been the subject of our correspondence since March stands entirely on my land, and has done so since 1952. It follows that every complaint I have made to you about it has been, in effect, a complaint against myself. I can think of no precedent for this in my professional experience, and I very much hope that none will ever arise in yours.

"Had I known the true position, I would never have written to you at all. Were it not for my own stubbornness, you would not have climbed that ladder, and you would not now be in Kingsmere with pins in your wrist. For that I am more sorry than I can easily express, and I have spent a great deal of my life expressing things.

"I propose, therefore, that the remaining reduction of the hedge be carried out at once, at my expense and by a professional, and that it be done without the assistance of either of us. I further propose that the cost of your treatment, your inconvenience and your shears be considered a debt of mine, to be paid in whatever form you think fit. Should you wish to pursue the matter in writing, I shall of course reply. Should you wish, on the other hand, never to hear from me again, I shall understand entirely, and I shall see to it that Audit is fed until you are able to manage.

"Your cat is well. Your tomatoes are ripe. Your beans are in a bowl on the kitchen table. I did not read your papers, although I could not help seeing that you, too, find it difficult to begin a letter.

"Yours, with the greatest possible respect, and for once meaning it, Constance Webb (Miss)."

She read it through only once. It was the first letter in years that she had not drafted at all. Then she sealed it, wrote his name on the envelope, and, after a moment's hesitation, did not put a stamp on it.

Barry came down the lane at one o'clock. She met him at the gate.

"No need for that today," she said, as he reached into his bag. "Would you be kind enough to take this next door? He'll be home this afternoon, I understand."

"He will," said Barry. "Taxi at three." He took the envelope, and looked at it, and looked at her, and for once in his career did not say a single word.

### [p28]
The taxi came at three. Constance did not watch it from behind the curtain. She watched it from the front step, quite openly, as one is entitled to do on one's own front step. She saw Mr Achterberg climb out slowly, with his right arm in a white cast and a sling. He was smaller than she had imagined, and greyer, and he wore the hat. He stood at his gate for a moment and looked up, not at the sky this time, but at the low stretch of the hedge at the far end of the garden, where the sun was coming through.

He did not look at her. He went inside, and the door closed, and Constance went inside too and sat down in the conservatory to wait. She was, she realised, rather good at waiting by now. She had had six months of practice.

At a quarter past three the sun came in.

At twenty past four there was a small sound in the hall: the sound of something light falling through a letterbox onto a mat. She knew it was not Barry. Barry was not due for another ten minutes, and in any case Barry knocked.

It was a cream envelope. Her name was on the front, in a hand she had never seen before: large, shaky, sloping first one way and then the other, as though it had been written with great effort by somebody using the wrong hand. Inside was a single card, and on the card there was a single word.

"Tea?"

Constance stood in the hall and looked at it for a long time.

She considered the reply. She considered "Thank you for your kind invitation", and "I should be delighted", and "With the greatest pleasure". She considered whether eleven drafts would be enough. Then she went into the kitchen, took down a jar of marmalade from the shelf, put on her good coat, and went out of her own front gate and in through his, as one does when one has been invited.

He opened the door before she could knock. For a moment they simply looked at each other, two people who had exchanged some forty thousand words and never one of them aloud.

"Milk?" said Mr Achterberg.

"Please," said Constance Webb.
