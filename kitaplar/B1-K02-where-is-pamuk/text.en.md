# Where Is Pamuk?

## [c1] Free

### [p01]
Three days after we set Pamuk free, her tracker stopped working.

On the computer in Aunt Sevgi's office, there was a map of the sea. For three days, a small green dot had moved across it slowly, like a sleepy insect. Every few hours, a new dot appeared next to the old one. Then, on the third morning, no new dot came. The last one just stayed in the same place, at the edge of a tiny bay that didn't even have a name.

"Maybe the battery is dead," said Ela, one of the other volunteers.

"It's a new battery," said Aunt Sevgi. "It should work for a year."

I looked at that dot until my eyes hurt. Where was Pamuk? Was he hurt again? Was he caught in a net? I had spent the whole spring with that turtle, and now I couldn't do anything for him.

But to understand why I cared so much, you need to know how it all began.

### [p02]
My name is Kerem, and I'm sixteen. I grew up in Istanbul, in a flat high above a busy street, and I used to think the sea was something you looked at through a window. Then, last March, my parents had to work in another country for six months, so I came to live with Aunt Sevgi in Kumlu, a small town on the south-west coast of Türkiye.

Aunt Sevgi is a vet. She has worked at the sea turtle rescue centre for twelve years. The centre is a white building next to the beach, with big blue tanks of sea water inside. Turtles that are hurt or sick stay there until they are strong enough to go home.

On my first day, I was bored. By the end of the first week, I was a volunteer. I cleaned tanks, cut fish into small pieces and wrote everything down in a notebook. I have never worked so hard in my life, and I have never been so happy.

### [p03]
Pamuk arrived in my second week. An old fisherman called Uncle Hasan found him in his net, south of Kumlu, near some rocky hills. The turtle was very weak, and he had a deep cut on one of his front flippers. He was a loggerhead, about as long as my arm, with a brown shell and a big, round head.

"Who gave him the name Pamuk?" I asked. Pamuk means cotton, and he wasn't white at all.

Ela laughed. "Uncle Hasan's granddaughter. She's five. She said his head looked soft, like cotton."

For the first two weeks, Pamuk didn't eat anything. He just floated in his tank while Aunt Sevgi cleaned his flipper twice a day. I sat next to the tank every evening and told him about Istanbul, school and football. I don't know if he understood. But one evening, while I was telling him about my old school, he swam to the side of the tank and took a piece of fish from my hand.

"He likes you," said Aunt Sevgi. "Or he likes your boring stories."

### [p04]
By May, Pamuk was eating so much that we called him the Hungry Prince. His flipper was getting better.

"Is he a boy or a girl?" I asked Aunt Sevgi one day.

She looked at his tail. "With turtles, it isn't easy to tell. Adult males have long tails. Pamuk's tail is short, so he might be a female. Or he might be a young male whose tail hasn't grown yet. He's about twenty, which is still young for a turtle. We can't tell yet."

So we all kept saying "he". It was easier, and nobody thought about it again.

At the beginning of June, Aunt Sevgi showed me a small grey box. "This is Pamuk's tracker," she said. "We'll glue it to his shell. When he comes up to breathe, it sends a signal to a satellite, and we can see on the map where he is."

"And if he's under the water?"

"Then it can't send anything. It also beeps, so if we're very close, we can find it with this." She held up a second box with a short metal stick on top. "The glue is strong, but it won't stay there for ever. One day the tracker will fall off, and that's fine."

### [p05]
We set Pamuk free on a bright morning in the middle of June. Half the town came to the beach to watch. Uncle Hasan was there, with his granddaughter on his shoulders. Ela was filming everything with her phone.

I carried Pamuk to the edge of the water with Aunt Sevgi. He was so heavy now that my arms were shaking. We put him down on the wet sand. For a moment, he didn't move. Then he walked slowly towards the waves, stopped once, as if he was thinking about something, and disappeared into the blue water.

Everybody cheered and waved. I felt excited and sad at the same time.

For three days, I checked the map every hour. The green dot moved south along the coast, past Kumlu, past the rocky hills, further and further. And then it stopped.

Aunt Sevgi made the map bigger. The last signal came from a small bay between two hills. There was no road and no village near it.

"I've lived here all my life," said Ela, "and I've never heard of that place."

## [c2] The Boat

### [p06]
"We have to go there," I said.

Aunt Sevgi shook her head. "Kerem, the sea is big. Even if we find the bay, we won't find Pamuk. A turtle can swim many kilometres in a day."

"But the dot hasn't moved for two days. If he's caught in something, he'll die."

"Or the tracker has broken," she said. "Or it has already fallen off. That happens."

"So we'll find the tracker. And then we'll know."

She didn't answer. She was looking at the map, and I could see she was worried too. Pamuk had been her patient for three months.

That evening, Uncle Hasan came to the centre with a bag of fish for the tanks. When Aunt Sevgi told him about the dot, he looked at the map for a long time.

"I know those hills," he said. "I found your turtle not far from there. There's no road, but my boat can get there in four hours."

"Are you sure?" asked Aunt Sevgi.

"I've been a fisherman since I was fourteen," he said. "If we leave early, we'll be back before dark."

### [p07]
We left the harbour at six the next morning. Uncle Hasan's boat was called Martı. It was old and blue, and it smelled of fish and engine oil, but he loved it more than anything except his granddaughter. Aunt Sevgi brought her bag of medicine, some water and the beeping box. Uncle Hasan gave me a life jacket that was much too big for me.

"Wear it anyway," he said. "The sea doesn't care how big you are."

The sea was flat and shiny, like a mirror. I sat at the front and watched the coast go past: small beaches, dark green pine trees and rocks that were red in the morning sun.

"When I was a boy," said Uncle Hasan, "these beaches used to be full of turtle tracks every summer. You could see them in the early morning, going up from the water and back again. Now you hardly ever see them. Too many people, too many lights."

"Why did the turtles come out of the water?" I asked.

He smiled, but he didn't answer, because just then something jumped out of the sea next to the boat.

### [p08]
It was a dolphin. Then there was another one, and another. Soon there were six dolphins swimming next to Martı, jumping and diving as if they were playing a game with us. I had seen dolphins on television, of course, but I had never seen one so close. I laughed so much that I almost fell into the water.

While we were watching the dolphins, the engine made a strange noise. It coughed twice, like an old man, and then it stopped.

Suddenly everything was very quiet. The dolphins swam away. The boat moved slowly up and down on the water.

"Don't worry," said Uncle Hasan. "This has happened before. She's old, like me."

He opened a small door in the floor and put his head and arms inside. For the next hour, all we could see of him were his legs. Aunt Sevgi passed him tools. I tried to help, but I didn't know the difference between the tools, so I just held the bag of fish.

### [p09]
At last, the engine started again. Uncle Hasan came up with oil on his face and a big smile.

"Good girl," he said, and he touched the side of the boat.

But while he was working, the sky had changed. The bright morning was gone. In the west, over the sea, there was a long line of dark clouds, and they were coming closer. The wind was getting stronger, and the flat sea was now full of small white waves.

Uncle Hasan looked at the clouds, and his smile disappeared.

"Can we go back to Kumlu?" asked Aunt Sevgi.

"No. It's too far now. If we try to go back, the storm will catch us on the open sea." He pointed at the coast ahead of us, where two hills stood next to each other like two green shoulders. "That's your bay. If we get there before the storm, the hills will protect us."

I looked at the clouds, then at the hills. I wasn't sure which one was closer.

## [c3] The Bay

### [p10]
We reached the bay just as the first heavy drops of rain started to fall. It was smaller than I had imagined, with a beach of soft, pale sand and high rocks on both sides. Behind the beach, there were low hills covered with grass and a few pine trees. There were no houses, no roads and no people. It looked like a place that nobody had visited for a hundred years.

Uncle Hasan dropped the anchor close to the rocks, where the water was calm. Then he phoned the harbour in Kumlu and told them where we were.

"We'll stay here tonight," he said. "Tomorrow morning, the sea will be calm again."

Aunt Sevgi took out the beeping box and turned it on. It made a soft hissing sound, but nothing else.

"Nothing," she said. "But we're still too far from the beach. It only works when you're very close."

Then the storm arrived, and we couldn't think about anything else.

### [p11]
For three hours, the wind screamed and the rain fell like stones on the roof of the little cabin. Martı moved up and down, up and down, and I felt a bit sick. But Uncle Hasan was completely calm. He made tea on a tiny cooker and told us stories about other storms he had seen.

"Have you ever been really frightened at sea?" I asked him.

He thought about it. "Once. Forty years ago. Since then, I've always listened to the sky." He looked at me. "Today the sky spoke, and we listened. That's why we're safe."

Around midnight, the wind stopped, almost at once, as if somebody had closed a door. The rain stopped too. I went outside onto the wet deck. The clouds were moving away, and a big yellow moon was shining on the water of the bay.

The beach was silver in the light of the moon. It was the most amazing place I had ever seen.

Aunt Sevgi came out and stood next to me. "Can't sleep?" she asked.

"No," I said. "Can we go to the beach?"

### [p12]
Uncle Hasan rowed us to the beach in his small wooden boat that was tied to the back of Martı. He stayed by the water, but Aunt Sevgi and I walked up onto the sand.

"No lights," said Aunt Sevgi quietly, when I took out my phone. "If there are animals here, light might frighten them. The moon is enough."

She turned on the beeping box again. We walked slowly along the beach, from one end to the other. The box hissed, but it didn't beep. I started to feel disappointed. Maybe the tracker was in the sea. Maybe it had broken in the storm. Maybe I had made everyone come here for nothing.

Then, near the middle of the beach, I stopped.

In front of my feet, there were two long lines in the sand, with a wide, flat path between them. They looked like the tracks of a small tractor. But there were no tractors here. The tracks came straight out of the sea and went up the beach, towards the hills.

### [p13]
Aunt Sevgi went down on her knees and touched the sand. "These are new," she said, and her voice was strange. "They were made after the rain. Maybe an hour ago, maybe less."

"A turtle?" I whispered.

She nodded. "A big one."

We followed the tracks up the beach, slowly and carefully, without saying a word. My heart was beating so loudly that I was sure the whole bay could hear it. The tracks went past the line of dry seaweed, all the way up to the soft sand at the bottom of the hills, where the grass began.

And there they ended.

In front of us, there was a pit in the sand. It was wide and deep, much bigger than a turtle. From inside it, in the dark, came a soft sound. Something was moving down there. Sand was flying out of the pit, again and again, as if someone was digging.

I took a step forward. Aunt Sevgi put her hand on my arm.

"Stop," she whispered. "Don't go any closer."

## [c4] Under the Sand

### [p14]
We sat down on the cold sand, about ten metres from the pit, and waited.

"Is it Pamuk?" I whispered. "What's he doing in there? Is he hurt?"

"I don't think anyone is hurt," said Aunt Sevgi. There was a strange smile on her face, but she didn't say anything else. "Just wait, Kerem. If we go closer now, we'll ruin everything."

Although I didn't understand, I trusted her. So we waited. And we waited. The moon moved slowly across the sky. Uncle Hasan came up from the water and sat down quietly next to us. Sometimes the sand stopped flying, and everything was silent for a long time. Then it started again, softer than before.

I think I fell asleep at some point, because suddenly Aunt Sevgi was shaking my shoulder. The sky over the hills was turning pink and orange. Birds were starting to sing.

"Look," she said.

A head was coming slowly out of the pit. A big, round, brown head.

### [p15]
The turtle climbed out of the pit very slowly, as if she was terribly tired. Then she turned around and did something that I had never seen before. With her back flippers, she started to push sand into the pit behind her. She worked carefully, for a long time, until the pit was full and the sand was flat again. She even threw some sand over the top, so that nobody could see where the hole had been.

Then she turned towards the sea. And when she walked past us, in the pink morning light, I saw her front flipper. There was a long, pale line on it, an old cut that had healed.

"It's Pamuk," I said. "It's really Pamuk!"

But something was different. On the top of Pamuk's shell, where the grey box should be, there was only a small pale mark.

"The tracker's gone," I said. "Where's the tracker?"

Aunt Sevgi didn't answer. She was watching Pamuk with tears in her eyes, and she was laughing at the same time.

### [p16]
Pamuk reached the water, stopped once in the small waves, just like on the day we set her free, and then disappeared into the sea.

"Kerem," said Aunt Sevgi, drying her eyes, "do you know what she was doing all night?"

"She?" I said.

"She. Pamuk wasn't hurt, and she wasn't lost. She was laying eggs. There are about a hundred eggs under that sand right now."

I sat there with my mouth open. Uncle Hasan began to laugh, a big, loud laugh that frightened the birds.

"Now you know why turtles come out of the water," he said. "I told you. When I was a boy, these beaches were full of tracks every summer. Mothers, all of them."

Aunt Sevgi explained it to me as we walked back down the beach. Female loggerheads often swim hundreds of kilometres to lay their eggs on the beach where they themselves were born. They come at night, when it's dark and quiet. And Pamuk had been found in a net just outside these hills.

"She was coming home," I said. "Even then, when she was hurt. She was coming here."

### [p17]
"But where's the tracker?" I asked again.

Aunt Sevgi held up the beeping box. "Let's find out."

We walked along the top of the beach, near the grass. For a few minutes, there was only the soft hissing. Then, about thirty metres from the new nest, the box gave a tiny beep. Then another one, louder. Then it was beeping without stopping.

Here, the sand was smooth and flat. There were old tracks too, but the wind and the rain had almost destroyed them. Aunt Sevgi went down on her knees and pushed her fingers very carefully into the sand at the side, not in the middle.

A moment later, she pulled out a small grey box. It was Pamuk's tracker.

"This is her first nest," she said. "She came here three nights ago, and she dug a hole, just like tonight. The glue broke while she was digging, and the tracker fell off and was buried. Under the sand, it couldn't send a signal to anyone."

"So that's why the dot stopped," I said.

"That's why," she said. "Pamuk didn't stop. Only the tracker did."

### [p18]
That was two months ago. A lot has happened since then.

The rescue centre has added the bay to its map. It still doesn't have an official name, but everybody at the centre calls it Pamuk Bay. Uncle Hasan has taken a group of volunteers there every week all summer, so that we can protect the nests. We have found four more of them, and we've put small fences around every one. If everything goes well, the first baby turtles will come out of the sand in a few days. I'm going to be there when they do.

My parents came back from abroad last week. They say I've changed. I'm browner, I'm stronger and I talk about turtles all the time. They're right.

Last night, I opened my old notebook from the spring. On the first page, in big letters, I had written: Pamuk, loggerhead, male, about twenty years old.

I crossed out one word and wrote a new one.

We were wrong about one thing. Pamuk is a she.
